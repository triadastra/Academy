# 3.3 The Foreign Exchange Market

## The nominal exchange rate

The **nominal exchange rate** is the rate at which one country's currency trades for another. Exchange rates are conventionally expressed as **foreign currency per unit of domestic currency**.

- **Appreciation (strengthening)** — an **increase** in a currency's value, measured by the amount of foreign currency it can buy
- **Depreciation (weakening)** — a **decrease**

## What appreciation does

*Worked example:* the dollar moves from 1 USD = 6.8 RMB to 1 USD = 7.2 RMB.

The **dollar appreciates**, and therefore:

- **US exports decrease** — US products cost more in foreign currency
- **US imports increase** — one dollar now buys more foreign goods
- **Net exports (NX) decrease**

Simultaneously the **RMB depreciates**, so **Chinese exports increase, imports decrease, and NX increases**.

The rule worth memorising: **a stronger currency makes your exports expensive and your imports cheap**, which worsens the trade balance. This is counterintuitive if you assume a "strong" currency must be good, and it is the single most tested idea in the topic.

## Variables that influence net exports

Beyond the exchange rate: **transportation costs** and **government policies** such as the tariffs and quotas of 3.2.

## Trade surpluses and deficits

**NX measures the imbalance in a country's trade in goods and services.**

- **Trade deficit** — an excess of **imports** over exports
- **Trade surplus** — an excess of **exports** over imports

**Why domestic governments often want a surplus:** it **creates job opportunities and promotes technological advancement**.
**Why foreign governments may prefer the alternative:** **efficiency** — goods being produced wherever they are made most cheaply.

## Interest rates and the exchange rate

A rise in the **real interest rate** attracts foreign financial capital, since investors seek higher returns. This **increases demand for the domestic currency** and causes it to **appreciate**.

**Foreign portfolio investment** occurs when domestic residents purchase foreign stocks or bonds, supplying "loanable funds" to a foreign economy — and supplying that country's currency demand in the process.

*Worked FRQ pattern.* Malaysia is in recession. The government implements expansionary fiscal policy — **an increase in government spending, a decrease in taxes, or an increase in transfer payments** (2.1). Government borrowing raises the demand for loanable funds, so **the real interest rate rises**. A higher real interest rate attracts foreign capital, so on a graph of the **foreign exchange market for the ringgit relative to the yen**, demand for the ringgit shifts right and the **ringgit appreciates**. Because the ringgit has appreciated, **Malaysia's imports increase** — the appreciation makes Japanese goods **relatively less expensive** than they were.

Trace that chain carefully; almost every foreign-exchange free-response question is a version of it.

## Exchange rate systems

| System | Definition | Examples |
|---|---|---|
| **Fixed (pegged)** | The government keeps the currency's value fixed against another currency | **Saudi Arabia** — the riyal is pegged at 3.75 SAR per USD |
| **Floating** | The government does **not** intervene; market forces determine the rate | **United States** (USD), **Eurozone** (EUR) |
| **Managed float ("dirty float")** | The central bank **intervenes occasionally** | **China** — the PBOC allows the yuan to trade within a controlled band; **Brazil**; **Singapore**, which manages the SGD against a trade-weighted basket |

The trade-off is between **stability and autonomy**. A fixed rate gives exporters and importers predictable prices, but the central bank must then defend the peg — spending reserves and surrendering control of domestic monetary policy. A floating rate keeps monetary independence at the cost of volatility. A managed float is an attempt to have some of each.
