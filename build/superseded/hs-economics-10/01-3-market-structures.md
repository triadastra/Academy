# 1.3 Market Structures and Factor Markets

## The four market structures

| Structure | Number of firms | Product | Entry | Price |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| **Perfect competition** | Very many | Identical | Free | Price **taker** |
| **Monopolistic competition** | Large numbers | **Differentiated** | Free entry and exit in the long run | Some price-setting power |
| **Oligopoly** | Few | Identical or differentiated | Barriers | Interdependent pricing |
| **Monopoly** | One | Unique | Blocked | Price **setter** |

## Perfect competition

Firms are **price takers**: the market sets the price and each firm can sell as much as it likes at that price, so **marginal revenue equals price**.

**The industry supply curve** shows the relationship between the price of a good and the total output of the industry.

**A firm produces where MC = MR**, and shuts down in the short run if price falls below average variable cost.

In the **long run**, free entry and exit drives economic profit to **zero** — that is, to normal profit (1.2). Profits attract entrants, which lowers price until profits disappear.

## Monopoly

A monopolist faces the **downward-sloping market demand curve**, which changes everything.

**The price effect:** to sell the last unit, the monopolist must **cut the market price on all units sold**, which **decreases** total revenue from the units it was already selling.

Marginal revenue therefore lies **below** price for a monopolist, and the firm produces less and charges more than a competitive industry would — which is the source of monopoly's inefficiency.

A **price-setting firm may only find it optimal to produce where demand is elastic**, since in the inelastic region a price rise would increase revenue and reduce costs simultaneously.

*Worked example:* a monopolist with demand $Q = 120 - P$ and total cost $TC = 2Q^2$ facing a per-unit tax of $12 passes only **part** of the tax to consumers — a monopolist absorbs some of any per-unit tax rather than passing it on in full, because raising price costs it sales.

## Oligopoly and collusion

**Why lasting collusive agreements are difficult to maintain:** each firm realises that **its own profits would increase if it were the only firm to violate the agreement** by increasing production slightly.

This is the essential logic. Collusion raises the price for everyone, which makes cheating individually profitable for each member — so every cartel contains the incentive for its own destruction. The other proposed explanations (that collusion benefits only some firms, or that firms face vertical demand curves) miss this individual incentive.

## Game theory

In a **Nash equilibrium**, no player can improve their outcome by changing strategy alone, given what the others are doing.

**Rock-Paper-Scissors** has **no pure-strategy Nash equilibrium**: whatever your opponent plays, you would want to change, so the only equilibrium is a **mixed strategy** — playing each option with probability 1/3.

## Efficiency and market failure

**Allocative efficiency** requires price to equal marginal cost. It is **violated when firms are price makers (price searchers)** — that is, when they have market power and set price above MC.

**Negative externality.** A cost imposed on third parties not reflected in the market price — pollution from production is the standard example. An increase in the price of oil caused by a cartel or by regulation is **not** an externality; it is a price change.

Where a negative externality exists, **marginal social cost exceeds marginal private cost**, and the market **overproduces** relative to the socially optimal quantity $Q_s$. Where consumers **underestimate** a harm — sugar's long-term health effects, say — **marginal private benefit exceeds marginal social benefit**, with the same result.

**Government intervention** is appropriate to reduce inefficiency where such a divergence exists. To reduce quantity toward the social optimum, a **per-unit tax** works and a **lump-sum tax** does not: only a per-unit tax changes marginal cost and therefore the quantity chosen. A lump-sum tax reduces profit without changing the profit-maximising output at all.

## Factor markets

**Derived demand.** The demand for a factor of production is derived from the demand for the good it produces: **a decrease in the demand for theatre tickets will decrease the demand for actors**.

### Perfectly competitive labour market

The market sets the wage, which is the **marginal factor cost (MFC)** for each individual firm. The firm's demand for labour is its **marginal revenue product (MRP)**, which **slopes downward**. Firms are price takers and **hire where MRP = MFC**.

### Monopsony

A **monopsony** is the only firm in a labour market, which lets it **control the wage**.

It hires where **MFC = MRP**, but pays the wage read off the **supply curve** at that quantity — which is **below** MRP. A monopsonist therefore hires **fewer** workers at a **lower** wage than a competitive market would, mirroring on the buying side what a monopoly does on the selling side.
