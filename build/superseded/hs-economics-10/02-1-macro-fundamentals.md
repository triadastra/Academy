# 2.1 Macroeconomic Fundamentals

## The circular flow

Income flows between households and firms, with **injections** adding to the flow and **leakages** withdrawing from it.

| Injections | Leakages |
|---|---|
| Investment spending | **Saving** |
| Government spending | **Taxes** |
| Exports | **Imports** |

**Saving is a leakage** from the circular flow; consumer spending, government spending, and investment spending are not.

## The marginal propensity to consume

**MPC** is the fraction of an additional dollar of income that is spent rather than saved.

*Worked example:* Adam receives a $500 raise, taking his income to $1,500, and saves $200 of the $1,500. His **MPC out of the raise** is what he spent of the extra $500. Since his saving rose by $100 (assuming he saved $100 before), he spent $400 of the $500 — an MPC of **0.80**.

The MPC drives the **spending multiplier**:

$$\text{multiplier} = \frac{1}{1 - MPC}$$

A higher MPC means a larger multiplier, because each dollar injected is re-spent more times before leaking out.

## Fiscal policy

**Expansionary fiscal policy** — used to address a recession:

- **Increase government spending**
- **Decrease taxes**
- **Increase transfer payments**

**Contractionary fiscal policy** does the reverse.

**A balanced-budget expansion.** Suppose a legislator proposes increasing spending to stimulate aggregate demand while **raising taxes to pay for it**. This is **not** the worst possible combination, and not the best: the two partly offset, but government spending has a **larger** immediate effect than an equal tax change, because the whole of a spending increase enters the flow while only the MPC fraction of a tax cut is spent. The net effect is therefore mildly expansionary.

## Money and banking

**Required reserve ratio.** Banks must hold a fraction of deposits as reserves.

*Worked example:* with a required reserve ratio of 10 percent, a bank holding $100 million in deposits must keep $10 million. If it actually holds $22 million, its **excess reserves** are $12 million.

**Excess reserves are what a bank can lend**, and they drive the money multiplier $1/rr$.

## Monetary policy and the bond market

When the **Federal Reserve purchases bonds**:

1. The **money supply increases**
2. The **interest rate decreases**
3. The **price of bonds increases**

The bond-price relationship follows from the demand: the Fed buying bonds raises demand for them, and **bond prices and interest rates always move in opposite directions**. A bond paying a fixed coupon is worth more when prevailing rates are lower.

Show this on a **money market graph**: the money supply curve shifts right, and the equilibrium nominal interest rate falls.

## The Phillips curve

The **short-run Phillips curve** shows a **trade-off between the unemployment rate and the rate of inflation** — not between the *natural* rate of unemployment and inflation, which is the standard distractor.

In the **long run** there is no such trade-off: the long-run Phillips curve is vertical at the natural rate of unemployment.

## Inflation

**Cost-push inflation** arises when input costs rise — as when a tariff raises the price of imported raw materials.

**Who loses purchasing power:**

- **Households and consumers**, when the prices of food, utilities, and other goods rise
- **Companies**, when the prices of inputs rise — raw materials such as coal and crude oil, and intermediate goods

**In response, companies typically raise the prices of their products to offset inflation, meaning consumers ultimately absorb the increases.** This pass-through is why a tax on producers is rarely paid only by producers.
