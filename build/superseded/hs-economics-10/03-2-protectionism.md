# 3.2 Trade Protectionism

**Protectionism** refers to government policies that **restrict free trade to shield domestic industries from foreign competition**.

## The instruments

### 1. Tariffs — taxes on imports

*Example:* a large US tariff on Chinese electric vehicles, making them more expensive.

**Pros:**

- US producers face **less foreign competition**, since Chinese products become too expensive for consumers, so more domestic products are bought — raising production, revenue, and employment
- The **government gains revenue** from the tariff

**Cons:**

- **US consumers pay higher prices**, since cheaper foreign products now carry a tax. **Demand and consumer surplus decrease**
- Foreign retaliation reduces US **exports**, and with them revenue, production, and employment

### 2. Import quotas — limits on the quantity imported

*Example:* the EU restricting the number of US steel imports.

**Cons:** shortages, higher prices, **and no extra revenue for the government**.

The last point is the key difference from a tariff. A quota raises the price just as a tariff does, but the extra money goes to whoever holds the import licence rather than to the treasury — which is why economists generally prefer tariffs to quotas if protection is going to happen at all.

### 3. Subsidies — government payments to domestic firms

*Example:* India subsidising sugar exports to compete globally.

**Pros:** supports struggling industries, encourages domestic production.
**Cons:** can lead to **overproduction** and trade disputes; the WTO often bans unfair subsidies.

### 4. Non-tariff barriers — regulations, standards, bureaucracy

*Example:* the EU banning hormone-treated US beef on stated health grounds.

These are the hardest to challenge, because a genuine health regulation and a disguised trade barrier can look identical.

## Arguments for protectionism

- **Protecting jobs** — free trade destroys jobs in industries that compete with imports; tariffs protect domestic producers and keep those jobs
- **National security** — an industry vital to security, such as high technology or food, should be protected to prevent dependence on imports that could be disrupted
- **Infant industry** — a new industry argues for **temporary** protection until it matures enough to compete
- **Unfair competition** — producers argue foreign competitors have an unfair advantage from government subsidies
- **Government revenue**

## Arguments against protectionism

- **Higher consumer prices** — tariffs make imports costlier
- **Free trade increases economic growth.** The US International Trade Commission estimated NAFTA could raise US growth by 0.1–0.5% a year
- **A more dynamic business climate.** Protection often left domestic industries **stagnant and non-competitive**; with protection removed, they became motivated to become genuine global competitors
- **Lower government spending.** When a trade agreement removes subsidies, those funds can be redirected
- **Technology transfer.** Local companies gain access to the latest technologies from multinational partners, and multinationals provide **job training** to local employees
- **Harms domestic manufacturers** who use imported goods as raw materials
- **Trade wars and retaliation**

The third argument is the strongest and the least intuitive: protection is often bad for the very industry it protects, because it removes the pressure to improve.

## The case study: US–China tariffs

Tariffs on thousands of imports from China, valued at roughly **$370 billion**, were imposed in **2018 and 2019**.

**Effects.** Much of the **cost of the tariffs was passed on to US consumers and businesses** — the pass-through described in 2.1. A tariff **increases the prices of imported goods, which worsens inflation** in the importing country:

- **Consumers lose purchasing power** as the prices of food, utilities, and other goods rise
- **Companies lose purchasing power** as input prices rise — raw materials such as coal and crude oil, and intermediate products
- Companies **raise their own prices to offset the increase**, so consumers absorb them

This is **cost-push inflation**.

**Retaliation.** When the US imposed tariffs on steel (25%) and aluminium (10%), the EU, China, and Canada prepared **retaliation lists**. American producers such as Harley-Davidson threatened to move production abroad, and the US government then threatened those companies with additional taxes.

**Who suffers from higher duties on German automobiles?** Not only Germany. Higher tariffs on BMW, Volkswagen, and Daimler raise their prices in the American market, and **the network of car-component producers across other EU countries loses too** — estimates suggest Germany might lose about 0.2% of GDP and Poland 0.16%. EU retaliation might in turn cost American jobs.

The lesson generalises: **modern supply chains mean a tariff aimed at one country hits many**, including firms in the country imposing it.

## Discussion

**Discuss the possible short- and long-run results of introducing protectionism to international trade.**

*Short run:* domestic producers in the protected industry gain; consumers pay more; the government collects revenue; retaliation begins.

*Long run:* the protected industry may stagnate without competitive pressure; export industries shrink under retaliation; supply chains reorganise; and the total surplus lost is generally larger than the surplus transferred to the protected producers.
