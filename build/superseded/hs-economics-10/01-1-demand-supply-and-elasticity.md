# 1.1 Demand, Supply, and Elasticity

## Core principles

Two ideas underlie almost everything in microeconomics:

- **People systematically do the best they can to profit** — they **compare marginal benefit with marginal cost**
- **People respond to incentives.** An **incentive** is something that induces a person to act — a reward or a punishment

Note the word **marginal**. Economic decisions are almost never about totals; they are about whether *one more* unit is worth its cost.

## Demand

**Quantity demanded ($Q_d$)** is the amount of a good that buyers are **willing and able** to purchase.

**The law of demand:** $Q_d$ of a good **falls when its price rises**, other things equal.

A **demand schedule** is a table showing the relationship between price and quantity demanded; the demand curve is its graph.

### Demand curve shifters

| Shifter | Effect |
|---|---|
| **Number of buyers** | An increase raises demand |
| **Income — normal goods** | Income up → **demand up** |
| **Income — inferior goods** | Income up → **demand down** |
| **Substitutes** | An increase in the price of one **raises** demand for the other |
| **Complements** | An increase in the price of one **lowers** demand for the other |
| **Tastes** | Whether consumers favour the good |
| **Expectations** | Expected future prices or income |

The essential distinction: a change in the good's **own price** moves you **along** the curve; anything else **shifts** the curve. Confusing the two is the most common error in the topic.

## Supply

**The law of supply:** quantity supplied **rises when the price rises**, other things equal.

### Supply curve shifters

| Shifter | Effect |
|---|---|
| **Input prices** | Higher input prices → supply **decreases** |
| **Technology** | Better technology → supply **increases** |
| **Number of sellers** | More sellers → supply **increases** |
| **Expectations** | Expected future prices |

## Elasticity

**Price elasticity of demand (PED)** measures how much $Q_d$ responds to a change in price.

**The midpoint method** avoids the problem that percentage changes differ depending on the direction of the move:

$$\text{PED} = \frac{(Q_2 - Q_1)/[(Q_1 + Q_2)/2]}{(P_2 - P_1)/[(P_1 + P_2)/2]}$$

**Determinants of PED:**

- **Existence of substitutes** — more substitutes, more elastic
- **Necessity or luxury** — necessities are inelastic
- **Defined broadly or narrowly** — "food" is inelastic, "this brand of cereal" is elastic
- **Long run or short run** — demand is more elastic over time, as buyers find alternatives

**The higher the elasticity, the flatter the curve.**

**Price elasticity of supply (PES)** measures how much $Q_s$ responds to a change in price.

## Price controls

A **price control** is a government limit on price, imposed to **deal with inequalities generated within the market**.

| Control | Definition | Example |
|---|---|---|
| **Price ceiling** | A legal **maximum** price | Rent-control laws |
| **Price floor** | A legal **minimum** price | Minimum wage laws |

A price ceiling only binds if it is set **below** the equilibrium price, where it produces a **shortage**; a price floor only binds if set **above** equilibrium, where it produces a **surplus**. A ceiling above equilibrium or a floor below it changes nothing at all.

**Taxes** are used both to **raise revenue for public purposes** and to **influence market outcomes**.
