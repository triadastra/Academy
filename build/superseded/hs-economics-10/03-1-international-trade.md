# 3.1 International Trade

## Why nations trade

**No country can effectively produce everything it needs**, so nations trade. Trade allows countries **access to products and services they cannot manufacture themselves**.

## Absolute advantage

A nation has an **absolute advantage** if it can manufacture a good with **less labour and capital** than another nation.

*Example:* Japan has an absolute advantage in cameras and Sweden in cars. Japan makes more cameras and Sweden more cars, so both benefit from trading.

## Comparative advantage

A country has a **comparative advantage** when it can produce a good at a **lower opportunity cost** — what it gives up to produce that good.

This is the more important concept, because **trade is beneficial even when one country has an absolute advantage in everything**. What matters is not who is better, but who gives up less.

*Worked example.* Compare China and New Zealand producing industrial goods and fruit:

**Opportunity cost of one ton of fruit** (industrial goods sacrificed per fruit):

- China: $21 / 35 = 0.6$ — for every fruit, China sacrifices 0.6 industrial goods
- New Zealand: $6 / 30 = 0.2$ — for every fruit, New Zealand sacrifices only 0.2

**Opportunity cost of one industrial good** (fruit sacrificed):

- New Zealand: $30 / 6 = 5$ — for every industrial good, New Zealand sacrifices 5 fruits

New Zealand has the **lower** opportunity cost in fruit and the **higher** one in industrial goods, so it should **specialise in fruit and trade for industrial goods**.

The two ratios are reciprocals, which is why a country cannot have a comparative advantage in everything: being relatively better at one thing means being relatively worse at the other.

## How trade benefits nations

- **Increased efficiency** — countries specialise in what they do best, raising global output
- Access to a wider variety of goods
- Lower prices for consumers

## World price and the direction of trade

Let $P_W$ be the **world price** — the price prevailing in world markets — and $P_D$ the **domestic price without trade**.

| Condition | Implication |
|---|---|
| $P_D < P_W$ | The country has a **comparative advantage** in the good and **exports** it |
| $P_D > P_W$ | The country lacks comparative advantage and **imports** it |

When a country imports a good, the domestic price falls to $P_W$: **consumer surplus rises by more than producer surplus falls**, so **total surplus increases**. That gain is the formal case for free trade, and it comes with a distributional catch — consumers gain, competing domestic producers lose.

## Potential downsides of free trade

- **Job losses** — industries that cannot compete shrink, as with US manufacturing moving overseas
- **Dependency** — over-reliance on imports is risky, as shortages during global crises demonstrated
- **Environmental and labour concerns** — some countries may exploit workers or natural resources to cut costs

These are real costs and the honest version of the argument acknowledges them: free trade raises **total** surplus while **redistributing** it, and the losses are concentrated on identifiable groups while the gains are spread thinly. That asymmetry is why protectionist politics is persistent even where the economics is not in dispute.
