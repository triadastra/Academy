# 2.1 Rhetorical Analysis and SPACE CAT

**Rhetoric** is the art of effective or persuasive writing and speaking, especially through figures of speech and other techniques. Rhetorical analysis asks not *what* a text says but *how* it works on its audience.

## SPACE CAT

**SPACE CAT** is an annotation technique that structures rhetorical analysis, especially for the essay section.

| Letter | Element | Questions to ask |
|---|---|---|
| **S** | **Speaker** | Who is the speaker? What do we know about them? What helps you understand them? |
| **P** | **Purpose** | What is the speaker hoping to accomplish? What do they want the audience to do afterwards? |
| **A** | **Audience** | Who is the speaker trying to reach? How do we know? What assumptions does the text make about them? |
| **C** | **Context** | What is the time and place of this piece? What is happening in the world that relates to it? |
| **E** | **Exigence** | What was the spark or catalyst that moved the speaker to act? How did that event affect them? |
| **C** | **Choices** | What rhetorical choices does the speaker make — structure, devices, diction, syntax? |
| **A** | **Attitude** | What is the speaker's attitude toward the subject? Where does it shift? |
| **T** | **Tone** | What evidence demonstrates the tone? |

Note that **exigence** is not the same as purpose. Purpose is what the speaker wants to achieve; exigence is the event that made speaking necessary in the first place.

## Annotating

When annotating, use the shorter version of SPACE CAT — **QCAT**, where **Q** stands for **quote**.

Practical rules:

- **Don't write too much**, or you will run out of time.
- **Use bullet points.**
- **Do not just underline or highlight things.** Highlighting records that a line seemed important; it does not record *why*, which is the only part you can use later.

## Choosing what to analyse

**Analyse the most important devices, not every device.** In Malala's UN speech there are several — imagery, repetition, and others. If you spend your whole essay on three lines of repetition while ignoring the rest of the speech, you will lose marks, because rhetoric accounts for the whole text.

Also **keep transitions in mind** when choosing what to analyse first. If one device leads naturally into the context of the next, that ordering will read better.

Martin Luther King's letter is a good model of structure worth noticing: he begins by explaining that his protests are harmless, and only then moves to admonishing his intended audience — the "white moderate".

## Writing the thesis

**Clarify what choice is involved in the device you analyse** — but **do not make it too narrow**. Describe what is being demonstrated while leaving yourself room for *how* it is demonstrated.

If you are analysing diction, you might state what connotation the diction carries and to what effect, while leaving open where it appears — perhaps when the speaker addresses the audience in one paragraph, and when the speaker describes a scenario in another. This lets you address nuance while keeping your transitions smooth.

## Verbs for thesis and topic sentences

The verb you choose does real work. Some that reliably help:

- **Develops** — works well when the speaker builds something over the course of the text. It also lets you organise the essay around *how* the device develops.
- **Compare / contrast / narrates / illustrates** — same principle: each implies a structure your body paragraphs can follow.
- **Assert / advocate** — makes the speaker's stance on the topic explicit.
- **Emphasise** — simple and effective, though it fits a topic sentence better than a thesis.

Search for suitable verbs if you are stuck; a precise verb often does more for a thesis than another adjective.

## Short-answer questions

You will be given a short speech and asked questions on it — for example, *"In this part, what is the speaker using? Why would the speaker choose that device? What are its effects?"*

Answer in three steps:

1. **Identify** the device.
2. **Explain** why it is used.
3. **Describe** the effect.

**Worked example 1.** *"Ask not what your country can do for you — ask what you can do for your country."* (JFK)

> The speaker uses **antithesis** (contrasting ideas). This highlights civic duty by flipping the perspective. It makes the message memorable and challenges the audience to act.

**Worked example 2.** *"We are the change. We are the hope. We are the future."*

> The speaker uses **anaphora**, repeating "We are". By doing so the speaker builds momentum and empowers the audience to feel collective responsibility.

Both answers are three sentences long and follow the same order every time. That reliability is the point: the marks are for the second and third steps, and a fluent answer that never gets past step 1 scores nothing.
