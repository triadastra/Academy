# 3.2 Relative Clauses

**Relative clauses** are phrases within a sentence that give extra information about a noun.

> Ronan, **who is a boy**, likes playing football.

Here "who is a boy" is the relative clause.

## Relative pronouns

**Relative pronouns** are the pronouns that begin a relative clause. The common ones:

| Pronoun | Used for |
|---|---|
| **who** | People |
| **which** | Things |
| **that** | People or things |
| **where** | Places |
| **whose** | Possession |

## Essential and non-essential clauses

**Essential** (defining) clauses are **needed for the sentence's meaning**. Remove one and the sentence no longer identifies what it was talking about.

> The student **who scored highest** received the prize.

**Non-essential** (non-defining) clauses are **not needed for the meaning** — they are additional information.

> Ronan, **who is a boy**, likes playing football.

**The punctuation follows from the distinction.** Non-essential clauses are set off by commas; essential clauses are not. The commas are a signal to the reader that the clause could be lifted out without loss.

Compare:

> My brother **who lives in Beijing** is a doctor. *(essential — I have several brothers, and this identifies which one)*
>
> My brother, **who lives in Beijing**, is a doctor. *(non-essential — I have one brother, and by the way he lives in Beijing)*

The two sentences make different factual claims about how many brothers the speaker has, purely from the commas.

## Formal and informal relative clauses

The difference is where the **preposition** goes — small grammar words like *to*, *for*, *about*.

- **Formal** — the preposition goes **before** the relative pronoun.
  > The colleague **to whom** I spoke was helpful.
- **Informal** — the preposition goes **after**, at the end of the clause.
  > The colleague **who** I spoke **to** was helpful.

Both are correct English. Choose the formal construction for essays and the informal one for dialogue or informal writing — and note that the formal version generally requires *whom* rather than *who*.
