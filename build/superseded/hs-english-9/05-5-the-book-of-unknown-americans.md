# 5.5 The Book of Unknown Americans

**Author:** Cristina Henríquez
**Published:** 2014
**Genre:** Literary fiction
**Setting:** Newark, Delaware, USA
**Antagonist:** Garrett Miller

## Plot summary

The novel follows Arturo and Alma Rivera, who move from Mexico to Newark, Delaware with their teenage daughter Maribel, who has a brain injury following an accident. Maribel attends a special school while her parents struggle to adapt: Arturo works long hours at a mushroom farm, and Alma faces deep homesickness.

Their lives intertwine with their neighbours Celia and Rafael Toro, and their son Mayor, who becomes close to Maribel despite her condition. As Mayor and Maribel grow closer they are targeted by a school bully, Garrett Miller, who eventually assaults Maribel. Alma tries to shield her daughter, but the incident and other tensions escalate. Arturo loses his job, and the family's American dream begins to unravel.

Mayor defies his parents, steals a car, and takes Maribel out to see her first snowfall, causing panic when she does not return home. **Climax:** believing Maribel is in danger, Arturo sets out to confront the boy he believes responsible, and is shot and killed by Garrett's father.

The tragedy devastates the community. Alma and Maribel prepare to return to Mexico, and the neighbours raise money to bring Arturo's body home. As they leave, Alma realises that Maribel, despite her injury, **has always been herself**, and they drive on carrying both their loss and their hope.

The structure matters: the novel alternates between Alma's and Mayor's narration, interleaved with short first-person testimonies from other residents. No single voice owns the story, which is the formal expression of its central claim about community.

## Major characters

### Alma Rivera — the protective, guilt-ridden mother

*Role:* protagonist, matriarch of the Rivera family.
*Themes:* isolation versus community, maternal love, guilt, fear, adaptation.

*Arc:* Alma begins consumed by guilt over her daughter's accident, which propels her to seek a better life in the US. Though she finds community at the Redwood Apartments and builds deep friendships — especially with Celia — she remains emotionally isolated. Her overprotectiveness, and her silence about Garrett's assault on Maribel, rooted in shame and fear, ultimately leads to tragic consequences. Yet she also learns to reframe her understanding of Maribel, accepting her daughter not as "lost" but as **transformed**.

*Symbolism:* the sacrifices and quiet resilience of immigrant mothers, and the inner conflict between personal guilt and societal expectation.

### Arturo Rivera — the selfless father and martyr

*Role:* breadwinner.
*Themes:* sacrifice, family, the American Dream, dignity.

*Arc:* hardworking and selfless, labouring in grim conditions to fund Maribel's education. His enduring optimism and humour give the family emotional warmth. His death, following a misunderstanding with Garrett's family, positions him as a **martyr** — a man who dies trying to protect what little hope he has.

*Symbolism:* both the dream and the futility of the American immigrant experience — the idea that hard work does not always yield justice.

### Maribel Rivera — lost and reclaimed identity

*Role:* catalyst of the plot.
*Themes:* disability, trauma, identity, love, coming of age.

*Arc:* once vibrant and rebellious, Maribel becomes quiet and confused after her injury. While many treat her as incapable, her relationship with Mayor reveals her inner self and her potential for healing. By the end she reclaims aspects of her identity, and Alma realises she never truly lost her.

*Symbolism:* both the fragility and the resilience of personhood; she challenges societal definitions of value and capability.

### Mayor Toro — the sensitive adolescent

*Role:* Alma's narrator counterpart, and Maribel's friend and love interest.
*Themes:* adolescence, masculinity, love, rebellion, perception.

*Arc:* begins trapped by his father's unrealistic expectations and his own self-doubt. His love for Maribel becomes a vehicle for asserting his identity, but also exposes his immaturity and naivety. His idealised love leads to tension and tragedy — but also growth.

*Symbolism:* second-generation immigrant youth, caught between two cultures and expectations, struggling to find their own path.

### Celia Toro — the bridge between cultures

*Role:* Mayor's mother, Alma's best friend.
*Themes:* friendship, gender roles, nostalgia, endurance.

*Arc:* nurturing and pragmatic, supporting family and friends with wisdom and kindness, balancing emotional intelligence with practicality — especially in her friendship with Alma and in handling Rafael's temperament. She symbolises the longing for a home that no longer exists.

*Symbolism:* the emotional bedrock of the community, and an anchor to the immigrant experience of lost roots and found kinship.

### Rafael "Rafa" Toro — the disillusioned patriarch

*Role:* Mayor's father.
*Themes:* masculinity, authority, trauma, generational conflict.

*Arc:* his harshness comes from wanting his family to thrive, shaped by a traumatic past. His rigidity and obsession with success create conflict, especially with Mayor. He embodies the difficulty many first-generation immigrants face in reconciling past hardship with a new culture.

*Symbolism:* both the trauma of war and migration, and the dangers of unyielding patriarchal expectation.

### Quisqueya Solís — the masked survivor

*Role:* gossip, and narrator of her own story.
*Themes:* shame, survival, identity.

*Arc:* outwardly flamboyant and dramatic, Quisqueya hides a history of abuse and trauma. Her need for control and social status masks a painful past.

*Symbolism:* the complexities of identity formation, and the need to **rewrite one's own narrative in order to survive**.

### Garrett Miller — threat and privilege

*Role:* local bully and antagonist.
*Themes:* racism, privilege, danger, repression.

*Arc:* mostly static — he represents the looming threat to immigrant safety. His harassment of Maribel and the subsequent confrontation with Arturo result in tragedy. Glimpses of his own troubled family life suggest a cycle of abuse and neglect.

*Symbolism:* unchecked privilege, toxic masculinity, and the dangers of silence and complicity.

### Supporting characters as community voices

Each minor character at the Redwood Apartments adds texture to the immigrant mosaic:

- **Benny Quinto** — a former seminarian turned smuggler and prayer leader; contradiction and redemption.
- **Gustavo Milhojas** — a janitor with a tragic past and enduring hope; perseverance and generational sacrifice.
- **Adolfo "Fito" Angelino** — the kind landlord; the evolution of the American Dream.
- **Nelia Zafón and José Mercado** — artists thwarted by systems of oppression; unrealised potential.
- **Micho Álvarez** — a photographer and activist; political resistance and cultural pride.

## Key themes

**Immigration and the American Dream.** Nearly every character came to the US seeking opportunity, safety, or a better future for their children. Yet the reality — poor working conditions, poverty, social alienation — shows the complexity and sometimes the futility of the dream. Arturo's death underscores how even hardworking, optimistic immigrants can be thwarted by systemic barriers and prejudice.

**Isolation versus community.** The immigrants are isolated by language, culture, and trauma; Alma is especially alone because of her guilt and fear. Yet the Redwood Apartments become a small, vibrant community where residents support one another in ways both emotional and material. The two forces run simultaneously rather than in sequence — the novel does not resolve isolation into community, it holds both.

**Love and relationships.** Love appears in many forms: romantic, parental, platonic. The novel asks what it means to love someone changed by trauma, as Alma does with Maribel. Mayor's love is both redemptive and problematic, raising ethical questions about **consent, care, and agency**.

**Trauma and recovery.** Maribel's brain injury, Quisqueya's childhood abuse, and the residents' stories of war, loss, and displacement all leave emotional and physical scars. Maribel's recovery is **slow and nonlinear**, and each character's trauma shapes how they integrate into American society. Healing is possible, the novel suggests, but requires patience, understanding, and community.

**Family and sacrifice.** Immigrant parents sacrifice everything for their children, often at great personal cost. Alma's guilt and resolve drive the migration; Arturo pays with his life.

**Identity and belonging.** Explored through Mayor's adolescent insecurities, Rafa's rejection of the "gringo" label, and Celia's nostalgia for Panamá.

## Critical vocabulary

| Term | Meaning |
|---|---|
| **Voice** | The distinct personality or style of an author as expressed in their writing |
| **Auto-theory** | A concept emphasising self-reflection and personal experience in understanding art and literature |
| **Assimilation** | The process by which individuals or groups adopt the cultural norms of another group, often losing their original identity |
| **Cultural monolith** | The idea that a culture is uniform and unchanging, ignoring its diversity and complexity |
| **Passing (for white)** | A person of non-white ethnicity being perceived as white, often to gain social advantages |
| **Point of view** | The perspective from which a story is told, shaping how events and characters are perceived |
| **Double consciousness** | The internal conflict experienced by marginalised groups in a dominant culture — seeing oneself both from within and through the dominant group's eyes |
| **Latino** | A term for people of Latin American origin or descent, encompassing a diverse range of cultures and identities |

**Cultural monolith** and **double consciousness** are the two most useful for essays on this novel: the chorus of testimonies exists precisely to refuse the monolith, and Mayor's self-perception is a textbook case of the second.
