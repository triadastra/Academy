# 2.3 Reading Poetry and Graphic Novels

Two forms that need their own reading protocols: poetry, where compression makes every choice load-bearing, and the graphic novel, where meaning is carried by images as much as by words.

## TPCASTT: reading a poem

| Letter | Element | What to look for |
|---|---|---|
| **T** | **Title** | Interactive versus naming titles — does it do work, or just label? |
| **P** | **Paraphrase** | What literally happens, stanza by stanza |
| **C** | **Connotation** | Figurative language and its associations |
| **A** | **Attitude** | The speaker's tone |
| **S** | **Shift** | Where does it change, and what changes? |
| **T** | **Title (again)** | Reread the title now that you know the poem |
| **T** | **Theme** | The overall message — one or two words |

Returning to the **title** twice is deliberate. A title often means something different once the poem has redefined its terms, and noticing that change is frequently the strongest observation available.

## The sonnet

- **Format:** 14 lines
- **Rhyme scheme:** ABAB CDCD EFEF GG
- **Pattern:** the first 12 lines set up the idea; the **last two lines are a couplet**, which turns, resolves, or undercuts what came before.

The structure is an argument in miniature: three quatrains to build a case, a couplet to deliver the verdict.

## Two worked poetry analyses

**"One Art" (Elizabeth Bishop) — how the shift contributes to the message.**

Throughout the poem Bishop uses a **lighthearted tone**, repeating that losing "is no disaster". At the end, however, she becomes regretful and shifts to saying it **may look like** disaster. This shows that Bishop was genuinely affected by her experiences of loss. So while teaching the reader not to take grief too hard, she simultaneously shares her own feelings and implies that **it is alright to be upset** — the shift is what makes the poem honest rather than glib.

**"Psyche with the Candle" (MacLeish).**

MacLeish uses **extended metaphor, allusion** to the myth of Psyche and Cupid, **personification, and repetition**. One message the poem suggests is that **the things you hold onto most tightly often end up being the things you lose** — Psyche loses Cupid at the exact moment she looks to be certain of him.

## Reading graphic novels

**Vocabulary of the form:**

| Term | Meaning |
|---|---|
| **Panel** | A single framed image |
| **Border** | The line or frame around a panel |
| **Gutter** | The space **between** panels, where the reader infers movement or time passing |
| **Tier** | A horizontal row of panels |
| **Speech bubble** | A rounded shape containing spoken words |
| **Caption** | Text, often in a box, providing narration or context outside dialogue |
| **Emanata** | Visual symbols — sweat drops, motion lines — showing emotion or movement |
| **Icon** | A simplified image representing a character, object, or idea |
| **Sound effect** | Onomatopoeic words (BOOM, splash) conveying sound within the scene |

The **gutter** is the concept worth dwelling on: nothing is drawn there, and yet the reader supplies all the movement and elapsed time. The form makes the reader complete it.

**Questions to ask of a page:**

- How does the "camera" move — does it pan, zoom, cut?
- What do panel layouts do? Do they have symbolic roles?
- What is the graphic weight — the distribution of black and white?
- What does the composition emphasise by size and placement?

### Worked example: *Maus*

On one page, Art Spiegelman is drawing with flies swirling around him, and at first we do not know why. The first four panels focus on his deteriorating mental state, overwhelmed by the workload following *Maus I*'s publication. The panels then gradually **pan out and move downward**, arriving at an impactful panel of Spiegelman among dead bodies.

**Composition** is used cleverly through **imbalance**: the bodies take up half the panel, emphasising their presence. The raggedly drawn bodies make the page more horrific still. The downward movement of the sequence enacts the descent it depicts.

### Worked example: *Persepolis* and graphic weight

*Persepolis* is the best text for studying **graphic weight**. The veils the women wear are **pitch black**, and Satrapi repeatedly uses that blackness to show how the veil **erased her individuality and identity as a woman** — the figures merge into one another visually.

A Batman panel demonstrates the same technique for a different purpose: strong graphic weight gives Bruce Wayne's face three-dimensional shape, and the cold colours on one side against slightly warmer ones on the other portray him as mysterious and serious. The officer beside him turns his head into shadow, losing definition — which **forces attention onto Bruce's expression**.

### Worked example: onomatopoeia in Kuper's *The Metamorphosis*

Two panels use drawn onomatopoeia to narrate action without dialogue: Grete slowly opens the door to check whether Gregor is visible, grabs the newspaper, then slams the door and locks it again. From this combination of text and visual choices we can tell she is still terrified and repulsed by her brother, and cannot bear the sight of him.

## Comparing a novel with a graphic novel

Compare along consistent categories. Using *The Metamorphosis* as the prose text:

| Category | In prose |
|---|---|
| **Colour** | Kafka's prose evokes a somber, muted atmosphere through description rather than pigment |
| **Panels** | Not divided into panels; chapters and descriptive passages control the pacing |
| **Art style** | Detailed descriptions create vivid mental images of Gregor's transformation |
| **Character design** | Gregor's transformation into an insect is a striking metaphor for dehumanisation |
| **Text elements** | Narration and dialogue convey Gregor's internal struggles and family dynamics |
| **Symbolism** | The metamorphosis symbolises existential alienation and the dehumanising effects of modern life |
| **Narrative structure** | A linear arc tracing the transformation and its consequences |

The value of the grid is that it forces you to ask what the graphic version **must decide** that the prose could leave open — a drawing cannot be ambiguous about how large Gregor is, but a sentence can.
