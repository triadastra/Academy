# 5.1 Romeo and Juliet

## Plot summary

**Act 1.** A street fight breaks out between the servants of the rival Capulets and Montagues; Prince Escalus intervenes. Paris asks Lord Capulet for Juliet's hand, and Capulet invites him to the family party, hoping Juliet will meet him there. Lady Capulet and the Nurse discuss Juliet's age and the match; Juliet is hesitant but agrees to consider him. Romeo, Benvolio, and Mercutio set off to the party in disguise, and Mercutio tells the Queen Mab story to comfort Romeo. At the feast Romeo and Juliet fall in love the moment they meet. Capulet and his nephew Tybalt recognise Romeo but are forced to leave him be.

**Act 2.** Romeo slips away while Mercutio and Benvolio tease him about Rosaline. In the balcony scene the two declare their love and decide to marry. Friar Laurence agrees to help, believing the marriage will end the feud. Tybalt sends a threatening message; the Nurse arrives and takes the wedding details back to Juliet. Friar Laurence warns Romeo about the speed of his love, and marries them.

**Act 3.** A confrontation between Mercutio and Tybalt leads to Mercutio's death. In rage Romeo kills Tybalt and is banished from Verona. The Nurse brings Juliet the news; she is heartbroken but also angry at Romeo. Friar Laurence advises Romeo to visit Juliet before leaving. Capulet, unaware of the secret marriage, decides Juliet will marry Paris within days. Romeo and Juliet spend the night together and part at dawn; Lady Capulet announces the match, and Juliet refuses.

**Act 4.** Juliet goes to Friar Laurence, who gives her a potion that will make her appear dead for 42 hours. She pretends to comply with her parents, then takes the potion, fearing the consequences of waking too early. The Nurse finds her apparently dead, and the wedding turns into a funeral.

**Act 5.** Romeo, never told of the plan, hears of Juliet's death and buys poison. Friar Laurence learns his message never arrived and rushes to the tomb. There Romeo kills Paris, drinks the poison, and dies beside Juliet. She wakes, finds him dead, and takes her own life.

## Study questions

### 1. What does Juliet say on learning Romeo is a Montague?

Two lines matter.

**"O Romeo, Romeo! Wherefore art thou Romeo?"** (2.2.33) — "wherefore" does **not** mean *where are you*; it means **why are you**. Juliet is miserable that the man she loves is a Montague, which deepens the "star-crossed lovers" archetype.

**"What's in a name? That which we call a rose / By any other word would smell as sweet"** (2.2.43–44) — a name does not matter to her; Romeo would be who he is regardless of what he is called. She is willing to love him anyway, pushing the family feud away from their relationship because she does not think it should apply.

### 2. What does the introduction of Friar Lawrence tell us?

**"Within the infant rind of this weak flower / Poison hath residence and medicine power"** (2.3.22–23).

His monologue argues that plants are neither wholly good nor wholly bad — the same substance can heal or kill depending on use. It establishes his moral outlook and, crucially, tells us he is **experienced with herbs and plants**, foreshadowing that he will be the one to supply Juliet's potion. His own line is a paradox that the plot then literalises.

### 3. Who are Laura, Dido, Cleopatra, Helen, and Hero, and what is Mercutio's take on them?

They are famously beautiful women with famously **tragic love stories**. Dido kills herself after Aeneas abandons her; Cleopatra kills herself after Augustus defeats her; Hero throws herself from a tower after Leander drowns.

Most significant is the reference to **Thisbe**, whose story is directly analogous: star-crossed lovers, and a double suicide caused by each believing the other dead. These references — Thisbe above all — **foreshadow the tragic ending**. Mercutio invokes them mockingly, which makes the foreshadowing sharper: he treats as a joke the exact fate the play is preparing.

### 4. What is the significance of the dialogue at the start of Act 3 Scene 5?

These pages are dense with **images of light and darkness — and death**. The lovers argue over larks and nightingales: the lark signals morning, the nightingale night. Phrases such as "jocund day / stands tiptoe on the misty mountain tops" (3.5.9–10) confirm the night is retreating.

**Romeo and Juliet switch sides repeatedly here**, so track who is telling whom to stay and who to leave. Romeo's parting line, "More light and light; more dark and dark our woes!" (3.5.36), directly foreshadows what follows, as Juliet is forced toward Paris. Juliet then says, "Methinks I see thee, now thou art so low, / as one dead in the bottom of a tomb" (3.5.55–56) — foreshadowing again, and this time an accurate vision of the final scene.

### 5. Why is Capulet furious when Juliet rejects Paris?

There are two readings.

**The negative reading:** Capulet has been lying since the party that the choice was Juliet's, and is now angry that she has actually exercised it.

**The more sympathetic reading:** Paris is handsome, rich, and related to the prince. Marrying him would give Juliet considerable security and status, so Capulet may believe he is acting in her interest and is baffled by her refusal.

The better essay presents both and argues for one, rather than asserting either as obvious.

### 6. What can you pull from Mercutio's "grave man" line?

**"Grave man" is a pun** — both *serious* and *on the verge of the grave*. It reveals Mercutio's **trickster personality**: even while dying, he is still making puns about his own death. The joke is also the play's tonal hinge, the last moment of comedy before the tragedy takes over.

### 7. Why does Shakespeare end Act 4 Scene 5 with Peter and the musicians?

Shakespearean plays at the time were not considered classy entertainment; they were for the common folk. This exchange appeals to that audience. The musicians worry about being paid now that Juliet is dead — much as the audience would, since few of them could afford to lose a day's wages. The scene grounds the tragedy in ordinary economic reality immediately after the family's grand mourning.

## Figurative language in the play

**Simile** — "Is love a tender thing? It is too rough, / Too rude, too boisterous, and it pricks like thorn" (I.iv.25). Romeo depicts love as harmful, comparing it to the injury a thorn makes in skin.

**Metaphor** — Romeo compares Juliet to the sun, equating her beauty with something radiant and glorious.

**Foreshadowing** — the double suicide is signalled repeatedly, including through Friar Lawrence's warnings and the allusions to Thisbe.

**Dramatic irony** — in Juliet's Act 3 Scene 5 conversation with her mother, the audience knows the couple are already secretly married, which explains Juliet's refusal in a way Lady Capulet cannot perceive.

**Oxymoron** — "O serpent heart, hid with a flow'ring face! / Did ever dragon keep so fair a cave?" (III.ii.73–74). Spoken by Juliet after Romeo kills Tybalt, expressing her contradictory mixture of love and betrayal.

**Monologue** — Juliet's balcony speech reveals her inner state to the audience: frustration at a family name that keeps them apart, and willingness to surrender her own name for him.
