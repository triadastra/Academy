# 1.1 Literary Devices

Naming a device earns almost nothing on its own. What earns marks is explaining **why** it is used and **what effect** it produces — so learn each term with its function, not just its definition.

## Comparison and figurative language

**Simile** — a comparison using *like* or *as*.

In *Romeo and Juliet*, Romeo says love "pricks like thorn" (I.iv.25), depicting his harsh and harmful experience of love through the injury a thorn inflicts on skin.

**Metaphor** — a direct comparison **without** *like* or *as*. When Romeo calls Juliet the sun, he compares her beauty to something radiant and glorious.

**Personification** — giving human qualities or feelings to non-human things ("the sun smiled"). Metaphorical rather than literal.

**Anthropomorphism** — giving human traits to animals or objects **literally**, often in fables — the talking animals of *Maus*.

The distinction matters: personification is a figure of speech about something that stays non-human; anthropomorphism makes the creature genuinely act as a person within the story.

**Synecdoche** — a part represents the whole ("wheels" for "car").

**Metonymy** — something is referred to by a closely associated thing ("the crown" for "royalty").

**Allusion** — an indirect or passing reference to something, often literary, historical, or biblical, designed to call something to mind without mentioning it explicitly. Steinbeck's *Of Mice and Men* alludes to the Garden of Eden. A description of someone collapsing with hands outstretched to the sky is a biblical allusion to the crucifixion.

## Contradiction and tension

**Oxymoron** — two contradictory terms placed side by side ("jumbo shrimp"). Juliet's outburst after Romeo kills Tybalt — "O serpent heart, hid with a flow'ring face!" (III.ii.79) — piles oxymorons on top of each other to express a contradictory mix of love and betrayal.

**Paradox** — a statement that seems contradictory but reveals a truth ("the more you know, the more you don't know").

An oxymoron is two **words**; a paradox is a whole **idea**. They are the most commonly confused pair in this list.

**Juxtaposition** — placing two contrasting elements side by side. A film that cuts from scenes of war to images of peaceful countryside uses juxtaposition.

**Antithesis** — contrasting ideas in parallel grammatical structure. Kennedy's "Ask not what your country can do for you — ask what you can do for your country" flips the perspective to highlight civic duty, and the balance makes it memorable.

## Irony

**Irony** is a discrepancy between appearance and reality. Three types:

| Type | Definition |
|---|---|
| **Verbal irony** | Not meaning what is said — closest to sarcasm. Saying "What a great day!" during a storm. |
| **Situational irony** | A surprising outcome, the opposite of what was expected. |
| **Dramatic irony** | The audience knows more than the characters do. |

In Act 3 Scene 5, Lady Capulet mistakes Juliet's grief for mourning Tybalt. The audience knows Juliet and Romeo are secretly married, so we understand what her mother cannot — dramatic irony.

## Repetition

**Repetition** — intentionally using a word or phrase two or more times for effect.

**Anaphora** — repetition at the **beginning** of successive clauses. "We are the change. We are the hope. We are the future." The repetition builds momentum and empowers the audience to feel collective responsibility.

**Epistrophe** — repetition at the **end** of successive clauses, the opposite of anaphora. "…of the people, by the people, for the people."

**Epanalepsis** — the same word at the **beginning and end** of one sentence. "Nothing is worse than doing nothing."

**Antimetabole** — repetition of words in **reverse grammatical order**. "Eat to live, not live to eat."

**Parallelism** — two or more elements of a sentence sharing the same grammatical structure.

## Lists and word order

**Asyndeton** — a list **without** conjunctions. "I came, I saw, I conquered." The clipped rhythm creates speed and force.

**Polysyndeton** — a list with **many** conjunctions. "He ran and jumped and shouted and danced…" The piling-up slows the reader and suggests abundance or exhaustion.

**Anastrophe** (inversion) — inverted word order for emphasis or rhythm. "Powerful you have become" — the Yoda construction.

**Apposition** — a noun phrase that explains or qualifies another noun. "Ms. Sedlock, our teacher, …"

**Apostrophe** — direct address to an absent, imaginary, or dead person or thing. "O Romeo…"

## Other essentials

**Foreshadowing** — hinting at something that appears later. The double suicide of the lovers is foreshadowed repeatedly, including in Friar Lawrence's warnings.

**Hyperbole** — exaggerated statements not meant literally. "I'm so hungry I could eat a horse."

**Euphemism** — a mild or indirect expression substituted for a harsh one. "He's in a better place now" instead of naming death.

**Anecdote** — a short, interesting story relevant to the topic, often used to support a point.

**Jargon** — specialised language used by a particular group or profession.

**Monologue** — a long speech revealing a character's inner emotions to the audience or to other characters. Juliet's balcony speech is the standard example: she voices her frustration that a family name keeps them apart, and her willingness to give up her own name.

**Syntax** — sentence structure: how word order, length, and complexity shape meaning. **Short sentences create urgency; long ones suggest depth.**

**Mood** — the atmosphere the author constructs through diction and detail. Mood is what the **reader perceives**; atmosphere is the quality of the setting itself.

**Voice** — the distinct personality or style of an author as expressed in their writing.
