# 5.2 Of Mice and Men

Steinbeck's novella follows two migrant workers in Depression-era California. The exam is likely to focus on **marginalisation** and **the American Dream**, so read the plot below with those two lenses active.

## Chapter summaries

**Chapter 1 — The Salinas River, near Soledad, California.** George and Lennie are introduced, travelling to a new ranch for work. They discuss their dream of owning a farm and living off the land. Lennie has a dead mouse in his pocket, which George takes away. George warns Lennie to avoid trouble and tells him to return to the riverbank if anything goes wrong. Lennie is obsessed with petting soft things, which has caused problems before.

**Chapter 2 — The bunkhouse.** George and Lennie meet Candy, the old swamper, and the boss; George lies that Lennie is his cousin. They meet Curley, the boss's aggressive son, who picks a fight with Lennie. Curley's wife appears, flirting with the men, making George uneasy. Slim, the skilled mule driver, is introduced; his dog has just had puppies. Carlson suggests shooting Candy's old, smelly dog — **foreshadowing** events to come.

**Chapter 3 — The bunkhouse.** George tells Slim about the incident in Weed, where Lennie grabbed a girl's dress and they had to flee. Slim gives Lennie a puppy, which he accidentally kills by petting it too hard. Carlson convinces Candy to let him shoot Candy's dog, symbolising the harsh reality of weakness on the ranch. George, Lennie, and Candy discuss the farm dream, with Candy offering to contribute money. Curley picks a fight with Lennie, who crushes Curley's hand in self-defence.

**Chapter 4 — Crooks' room in the barn.** Lennie visits Crooks, the African American stable hand, isolated because of his race. Crooks taunts Lennie by suggesting George might not return, and Lennie becomes angry. Candy joins them and they discuss the farm. Curley's wife enters, flirts with the men, and threatens Crooks when he asks her to leave. Crooks had offered to help with the farm but withdraws the offer. George is annoyed with Candy for mentioning the dream in front of Crooks.

**Chapter 5 — The barn.** Lennie accidentally kills his puppy. Curley's wife enters and talks to him about her dream of becoming an actress. Confused and frightened, Lennie accidentally kills her when she lets him touch her hair. He flees to the riverbank as George instructed. Candy finds the body and alerts the men, who form a lynch mob.

**Chapter 6 — The Salinas River, where the story began.** Lennie waits by the riverbank, imagining Aunt Clara and a giant rabbit scolding him. George finds him, reassures him, and then shoots him in the back of the head to spare him a worse fate at the mob's hands. Slim comforts George, understanding the decision. The story ends with George and Slim walking away, leaving Lennie's body by the river.

The **circular structure** is deliberate: the novel opens and closes in the same clearing, and the dream ends exactly where it was first spoken.

## Marginalisation

**Every character is marginalised for a different reason**, so revise the differences between them and **how each fits its historical context**.

| Character | Marginalised by |
|---|---|
| **Candy** | Age and disability |
| **Crooks** | Race |
| **Curley's wife** | Gender |
| **Lennie** | Intellectual disability |
| **George and Lennie** | Class — itinerant labour |

**Worked example — Candy.** Candy feels marginalised because of his age and his disability. He does not spend time with George or Slim; he has only his dog. The marginalisation makes sense given that he is a migrant worker: since Candy cannot perform basic labour, his position at the ranch is deeply unstable. This is why he is drawn to the farm — for its **stability** and the hope of joining a **community**.

Notice how that paragraph works: identify the character, name the axis of marginalisation, connect it to the historical role, and then explain what the character wants **because of it**. That structure transfers to any of the others.

## Key symbols

- **Candy's dog** — the fate of the weak and useless in this world; a direct foreshadowing of Lennie's death, down to the method (a shot to the back of the head, by someone who cares).
- **The farm** — the American Dream itself: land, security, and belonging.
- **Rabbits** — Lennie's version of the dream, reduced to something soft he can touch.
- **The riverbank** — safety and return; the frame of the whole book.

## Debate questions

**Were labour conditions in the United States fair?** It depends on the period. Many workers faced long hours, low pay, and unsafe environments. Some argue this was "fair for the time"; others see straightforward exploitation. Workers had little power, and unions fought hard for basic rights. By today's standards those conditions would seem wildly unfair.

**Is the American Dream attainable?** The dream is about upward mobility through hard work, but **not everyone starts from the same place**. Systemic barriers — income inequality, unequal access to education — make it harder for some. Personal effort matters, but so do luck and opportunity. The meaning of "success" has also shifted over time.

**Was George's killing of Lennie immoral?** A genuinely difficult call. George believed he was sparing Lennie a worse fate at the mob's hands. Mercy or murder depends on your view of morality — and the harsh world they inhabit offered few alternatives for someone like Lennie.

**Is Curley's wife portrayed for or against women?** She is often read as a stereotype: flirtatious, lonely, and trouble-causing. But it is equally arguable that she is a **victim of her time**, trapped in a lonely and powerless role — she is never even given a name. Steinbeck may be critiquing how women were treated rather than condemning her. Readers can pity her or see her as a destructive force; the text supports both.

## Historical context

Beyond the Depression and migrant labour, be familiar with **African American history** and the **Harlem Renaissance**, since many of the accompanying poems come from that period. Know **Langston Hughes**, **Zora Neale Hurston**, and **Countee Cullen** — reading one or two poems by each will give you a grasp of their styles. Hughes in particular has a distinctive one.

It is not compulsory to mention historical context in your analysis, but knowing the author and the period will improve it.
