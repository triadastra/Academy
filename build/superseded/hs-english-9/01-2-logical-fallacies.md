# 1.2 Logical Fallacies

A **logical fallacy** is a flaw in reasoning that makes an argument invalid even when it sounds persuasive. These appear in the multiple-choice and short-answer sections, and they are also the fastest way to strengthen your own arguments — by not committing them.

## The fallacies

**Hasty generalisation** — drawing a general conclusion from insufficient, unrepresentative, or biased evidence.
*"I met three rude New Yorkers, so all New Yorkers are rude."* A small sample cannot justify a claim about an entire population.

**Red herring** — introducing an irrelevant topic to divert attention from the original argument, often with emotionally charged distractions.
*Discussing the minimum wage, someone replies, "But small businesses are struggling to survive."* The struggle is real but does not address the question asked.

**Slippery slope** — arguing that a minor action will inevitably lead to extreme consequences, without proving the chain of events.
*"If we allow teens to use social media, they'll become addicted, neglect school, and ruin their futures."*

**Genetic fallacy** — judging an idea by its **origin** rather than its merit.
*"That policy is from a liberal think tank, so it must be bad."*

**Circular argument** — using the conclusion as the premise that proves it.
*"The Bible is true because God wrote it, and we know God wrote it because the Bible says so."* No external evidence is used at any point.

**Begging the claim** — assuming the conclusion within the premise; circular reasoning in simpler form.
*"This product is the best because it's superior to all others."* "Superior" just restates "the best".

**Ad populum** — claiming something is true or good because many people believe it.
*"Lots of people buy this diet pill, so it must work."* Popularity is not evidence.

**Straw man** — misrepresenting or exaggerating an opponent's argument to make it easier to attack.
*A: "We should limit fossil fuel use to fight climate change." B: "So you want to shut down all industries and destroy the economy!"*

**Moral equivalence** — equating minor moral issues with major ones, creating a false sense of equal importance.
*"Lying about your age online is just as bad as committing fraud."*

**Either/or (false dilemma)** — presenting two extremes as the only possibilities, ignoring middle ground.
*"Either we ban all cars, or we let pollution destroy the planet."* Ignores public transport, electric vehicles, and emissions standards.

**Post hoc ergo propter hoc** — assuming that because B happened after A, A caused B.
*"I wore my lucky socks and our team won, so the socks caused the win."* Correlation does not prove causation.

**Ad hominem** — attacking a person's character, appearance, or background instead of their argument.
*"Don't trust his tax policy — he's a dishonest person."*

**Non sequitur** — a conclusion that does not logically follow from the premises.
*"She's good at maths, so she'll be a great artist."*

## Distinguishing the confusable pairs

Several of these overlap, and the exam tests exactly those boundaries:

- **Red herring vs. straw man** — a red herring changes the **subject**; a straw man distorts the opponent's **position** and then attacks the distortion.
- **Circular argument vs. begging the claim** — the same error; circular argument names the loop between two statements, begging the claim names a premise that simply restates the conclusion.
- **Genetic fallacy vs. ad hominem** — the genetic fallacy attacks the **source** of an idea (an organisation, a tradition); ad hominem attacks the **person** making it.
- **Post hoc vs. non sequitur** — post hoc is a specific failure about **causation over time**; non sequitur is any conclusion with no logical link to its premises.

## Practice

Identify the fallacy in each:

1. My last two maths teachers were terrible, so all maths teachers must be bad.
2. We shouldn't worry about climate change — after all, there's still poverty and war.
3. If we allow students to redo their homework, soon they'll expect to retake every test and never take school seriously again.
4. That research comes from a conservative organisation, so it can't be trusted.
5. The Bible is true because it says it's the word of God, and we know God is real because the Bible says so.
6. You shouldn't vote for her; she cheated on her boyfriend in college.
7. Everyone at school is buying this new energy drink. It must be the healthiest option.
8. You want to reduce military spending? So you want the country to be defenceless and overrun by enemies!
9. Littering is just as bad as dumping toxic waste into the ocean.
10. He got into college after buying that lucky keychain. The keychain must have helped him!

**Answers:**

1. **Hasty generalisation** — a broad conclusion about all maths teachers from two examples.
2. **Red herring** — introduces a different issue to divert from climate change.
3. **Slippery slope** — assumes a small action leads inevitably to extreme consequences, with no evidence for the chain.
4. **Genetic fallacy** — attacks where the research came from rather than its quality.
5. **Circular argument** — the claim is supported by reasoning that loops back to itself.
6. **Ad hominem** — attacks character rather than qualifications or policies.
7. **Ad populum** — popularity is not valid evidence for healthiness.
8. **Straw man** — distorts the opponent's argument into an extreme version to make it easy to attack.
9. **Moral equivalence** — compares a minor wrong to a major environmental crime as though they were equally serious.
10. **Post hoc ergo propter hoc** — assumes cause and effect from mere sequence.
