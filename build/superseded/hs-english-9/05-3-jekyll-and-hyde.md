# 5.3 The Strange Case of Dr Jekyll and Mr Hyde

Stevenson's novella is structured as a mystery told mostly from the outside: we follow the lawyer Utterson's investigation, and only in the final chapter does Jekyll's own confession explain what we have been watching.

## Plot

Utterson hears from his relative Enfield about a brutal man named Hyde who trampled a girl in the street — and who holds a cheque signed by the respectable Dr Jekyll. Investigating, Utterson visits Dr Lanyon, an old friend of Jekyll's, who has never heard of Hyde, which alarms Utterson further. He watches the house where the trampling occurred and calls at Jekyll's, finding him absent and learning that the servant Poole treats Hyde almost as a master.

Hyde murders Sir Danvers Carew and disappears. Lanyon dies shortly after witnessing something he refuses to describe.

The final chapter is Jekyll's confession. Having gained the ability to transform into Hyde, Jekyll gains a second life in which all his beastly instincts can be pursued. After Hyde commits crimes, Jekyll resolves to stop transforming — but one day the transformation occurs **involuntarily**, and after returning home Hyde takes over. Jekyll locks himself in and sends Poole for more chemicals, but the potion has lost its effectiveness. He writes the last of his confession and then becomes Hyde permanently.

The involuntary transformation is the turning point of the book: up to that moment Jekyll believes he is in control of the arrangement, and afterwards the arrangement controls him.

## Characters

**Dr Henry Jekyll** — presented as a fine Victorian gentleman: well-mannered, respected, with a wide circle of friends. Central to the plot is his belief in the **duality of human good and evil** and his intention to separate the two. His ability to become Hyde lets him live a double life, engaging in atrocities and unleashing his beastly nature while his reputation remains intact. As he gradually loses control of the transformation he becomes increasingly isolated and depressed, illustrating his struggle against the self he created.

**Mr Edward Hyde** — constantly **dehumanised** from his first appearance, described with words like "savage", "snarled", and "ape-like". Central to his characterisation is his **"deformed" appearance**, which is never specified — the vagueness gives a sense of unease more effective than any detail would be. He attacks two innocent victims, the second fatally. Hyde leaves an impression of horror on every character who meets him, perhaps indicating people's fear of their own malicious side.

**Gabriel John Utterson** — the lawyer through whom most of the third-person narration takes place, slowly unveiling the truth. Kind, polite, respectable, and a trustworthy friend and adviser, he drives most of the plot's progression through his investigation.

**Dr Hastie Lanyon** — a close friend of Jekyll who believes his experiments are **morally objectionable and unscientific**. Traditional and conventional, when he witnesses Hyde transform into Jekyll with his own eyes, the scientific worldview on which his life rests collapses. The shock that such a monstrous being lies within a gentleman disgusts him deeply, and ends his friendship with Jekyll.

**Enfield** — appears in Chapter I, helping shape the reader's initial impression of Hyde.

**Sir Danvers Carew** — a well-respected, distinguished gentleman and a Member of Parliament, characterised as a model Victorian. His murder by Hyde is a stark contrast that characterises Hyde as someone **opposed to traditional Victorian values**.

**Poole** — Jekyll's faithful servant, who alerts Utterson to his master's sudden transformation.

## Themes

### Duality

The duality of human nature is the novella's most central theme, presented primarily through Jekyll: on the surface a respectable, well-mannered model gentleman, he wishes to be a doctor of the highest moral standards yet **yearns to commit the worst atrocities**. The two desires are not sequential but simultaneous.

Duality also appears in the **setting**. London is described with areas of degradation and poverty directly beside wealthy ones. Jekyll's own house makes the point architecturally: the front door has "a great air of wealth and comfort", while the back door — the one Hyde uses — gives an impression of "sordid negligence". One building, two faces.

### Good versus evil

In Jekyll's view, "man is not truly one, but truly two". He acknowledges both sides of himself, and the atrocities Hyde commits illustrate the consequences of giving in to the evil one. Hyde is associated with Christian ideas of Hell and Satan, and Jekyll repeatedly calls him "my devil". The novel proposes that the inherently evil aspect of man is **merely suppressed by society**, not absent from it.

### Reputation

Jekyll separates Hyde from himself precisely so that he can enjoy unleashing his instincts **without damaging his name**. Reputation drives other characters too: during his investigation Utterson never announces his suspicions publicly, partly to protect his friend's standing. Even after Jekyll's death Utterson says of the confession that he would "say nothing of this paper" — if the master has fled or died, "we may at least save his credit".

That final decision is worth pausing on. The book ends with a respectable man choosing to suppress the truth in order to preserve a reputation, which is the same choice Jekyll made at the start.

## Historical context

The novella was written in the 19th century, during rapid industrialisation. London's population grew from 1 million in 1800 to 6.7 million in 1900, producing **massive poverty and social division** — the inequality Stevenson dramatises through his contrasting descriptions of neighbourhoods.

The era was also the heyday of **physiognomy**, the idea that studying a person's physical appearance could reveal their inner morality. This idea is clearly at work in Hyde, whose unspecified deformity is taken by every observer as visible proof of an evil nature.

Reading Hyde through 4.1's question — *what was this society afraid of?* — the answer is the respectable Victorian's fear that beneath the surface of respectability there is nothing keeping the beast in.
