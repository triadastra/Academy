# 5.4 City of Glass

**Original author:** Paul Auster (from *The New York Trilogy*)
**Adapters:** Paul Karasik (writer) and David Mazzucchelli (artist)
**Genre:** Postmodern mystery / existential graphic novel / literary adaptation
**Key themes:** identity, language, reality versus illusion, authorship, obsession, urban alienation, failure

## Postmodern vocabulary

| Term | Meaning |
|---|---|
| **Postmodernism** | A literary movement marked by scepticism, irony, and rejection of universal truths or grand narratives |
| **Fragmentation** | A narrative technique breaking structure, time, or character continuity into disjointed parts |
| **Meaning** | Often questioned or destabilised, suggesting multiple or no fixed interpretations |
| **Irony** | Language or structure expressing something opposite to the expected meaning |
| **Intertextuality** | Referencing or integrating other texts to create new layers of meaning |
| **Genre** | A category of literature; postmodernism often blends or disrupts genres |
| **Subversion** | Undermining traditional norms, values, or expectations, often satirically |
| **Text / paratext** | The main body of a work, and supplementary material — titles, forewords, footnotes — that shapes interpretation |

## Plot and premise

Daniel Quinn, a lonely mystery writer grieving his wife and son, receives a **wrong-number call** meant for "Paul Auster, Private Investigator". Intrigued and desperate, Quinn becomes "Paul Auster" and takes the case from the disturbed Peter Stillman Jr. The task: protect Peter Jr. from his father, recently released from an asylum.

Quinn obsessively trails the enigmatic and seemingly harmless Stillman Sr. through New York. The investigation spirals into a labyrinth of identities, coincidences, and philosophical questions. Quinn meets the **real** Paul Auster — a writer — which blurs the lines further. His sense of self and reality disintegrates as the case becomes increasingly abstract and unsolvable. An ambiguous, unresolved ending leaves him lost and broken.

## Characters

**Daniel Quinn** — the protagonist. Writes detective novels under the pseudonym **"William Wilson"**; his fictional detective is **Max Work**, the identity Quinn imagines himself inhabiting. Emotionally isolated, he becomes consumed by the case and by his assumed identity.

**Peter Stillman Jr.** — the client. Traumatised, he speaks in a unique, broken language as a result of his father's abuse. He represents the **failure of language and communication**.

**Peter Stillman Sr.** — the "villain"? A former linguist and theologian who conducted cruel experiments on his son, obsessed with the Tower of Babel and the Fall of Language. He appears frail and harmless, but his past is monstrous.

**Paul Auster (the character)** — a writer Quinn contacts. He represents the "real" author and is **indifferent to Quinn's plight**, highlighting the separation between author and character.

**Virginia Stillman** — Peter Jr.'s wife, primarily a conduit for the initial contact and payment. She represents the "normal" world Quinn leaves behind.

## Themes

**Identity and doubles.** Quinn becomes "Auster" and writes as "Wilson". Stillman Sr. and Jr. are dark reflections of each other. The real Auster appears. The book asks constantly: *who is who?*

**Language and meaning.** Stillman Sr.'s linguistic theories, Peter Jr.'s broken speech, and Quinn's notebooks devolving into gibberish all point at the **instability and inadequacy of words**.

**Reality versus illusion.** Is the case real? Is Quinn's perception reliable? New York becomes a maze of signs and symbols, and the graphic novel form emphasises constructed realities.

**Obsession and descent.** Quinn's surveillance becomes all-consuming, leading to physical and mental unravelling. The case replaces his life.

**The failure of detection.** The mystery is not solved; it **dissolves**. The detective fails utterly and is destroyed by the process.

**Authorship and creation.** Quinn writes fiction and adopts identities; Auster exists as both character and author. The book is a meta-commentary on storytelling and creation.

**Isolation and alienation.** Quinn is profoundly alone; his connections — phone calls, surveillance — are impersonal and ultimately meaningless.

## Motifs

**The city as labyrinth.** New York is effectively a character: vast, anonymous, full of signs and hidden meanings, isolating and disorienting.

**The notebook.** An archive of obsessive, fragmented writing. It begins as a tool for case notes and degrades as Quinn loses his grip on reality, its entries growing chaotic and finally unintelligible.

**The telephone.** The story begins with the phone call — Quinn's attempt to make meaning in his own life. A second call from Auster marks both an end and a beginning, functioning as a callback.

**The hand and pen.** The pen, first sold to Quinn by a deaf man with the note "Learn to Speak to Your Friends", becomes a symbol of self-expression. As Quinn opens up, his writing grows more subjective — around pp. 102–104 the handwriting shifts into his own and the narration turns philosophical. On pp. 88–89 the pen's direction mirrors his emotional state: poised to record Auster's insight, then stunned and inactive when his expectations collapse. By pp. 130–131 Quinn is visually submerged, beginning with his pen — his identity dissolving with his loss of writing. **His ability to express is his sense of self: once writing fails, so does he.**

**The fingerprint.** Used to identify people, and drawn in the novel in the form of a **maze**. It symbolises how Quinn feels lost no matter where he is — his confusion with his own identity.

**Eggs.** Introduced alongside the Humpty Dumpty analogy. Stillman believes people must be "broken" before achieving greater meaning.

## Free-response preparation

**1. Trace Quinn's psychological and physical descent.**

- **Start:** a grieving, isolated writer living through a pseudonym (William Wilson).
- **Catalyst:** taking on the "Paul Auster" identity — an initial thrill and a replacement self.
- **Obsession:** stalking Stillman Sr. replaces writing and life; meticulous note-taking in the red notebook.
- **Reality blurring:** meeting the real Paul Auster undermines his assumed identity; Stillman Sr.'s bizarre behaviour defies logic.
- **Loss of control:** the notebook becomes chaotic and unintelligible; surveillance becomes meaningless and compulsive.
- **Physical deterioration:** he neglects hygiene, home, and health, and lives on the streets.
- **Breakdown:** unable to distinguish self from role, language fails; he retreats into silence and disappears.

Support this with visual evidence: art-style changes, panel fragmentation, and Quinn's changing appearance.

**2. How does Mazzucchelli's artwork contribute to the themes?** Choose two:

- **Identity** — mirrors and reflections; similar character designs across Quinn, the Austers, and the Stillmans; fragmented panels during identity crises; isolation within the frame. *Effect:* visually shows the instability and dissolution of self.
- **Reality versus illusion** — stark chiaroscuro creating uncertainty; distorted perspectives; dreamlike sequences; panels depicting observation. *Effect:* a constant sense of unease and visual ambiguity mirroring Quinn's unreliable perception.
- **Language and failure** — the visual depiction of Peter Jr.'s unique speech bubbles; Quinn's handwriting degrading in the notebook; chaotic scribbles replacing words. *Effect:* shows the breakdown of communication and meaning **visually**, beyond the text.
- **Alienation** — wide shots of tiny figures in vast cityscapes; characters framed alone or surrounded by negative space; empty streets. *Effect:* conveys profound loneliness and the isolating nature of the urban environment.

**3. How does the book subvert the detective genre?**

*Uses the tropes:* a wrong number initiating the case; a troubled detective (Quinn's grief); a mysterious client (Peter Jr.); a potential villain (Stillman Sr.); surveillance and note-taking.

*Subverts them:* the mystery has **no clear solution**; the "threat" is ambiguous; clues lead nowhere or become meaningless; the detective does not solve anything but is instead destroyed by the process.

**4. The theme of authorship.**

- **Quinn** writes detective fiction under a pseudonym and creates the "Paul Auster" PI persona. He tries to **author the reality of the case** through his notebook — and fails, becoming a character lost in a narrative he cannot control.
- **The real Paul Auster (character)** represents the source of Quinn's assumed identity, indifferent to his plight, highlighting the separation between author and character.
- **Paul Auster (original author)** created the source novel; his presence as a character blurs creator and creation.
- **Karasik and Mazzucchelli (adapters)** are "authors" of the graphic novel version, and their choices — visual style, condensation, emphasis — reinterpret Auster's work, demonstrating **adaptation as a form of authorship**.

*Commentary:* the graphic novel suggests identities are **constructed like stories**, that authorship is unstable (pseudonyms, adaptations), and that trying to control a narrative — a life or a story — can lead to failure and dissolution. Creation involves layers of identity and interpretation.
