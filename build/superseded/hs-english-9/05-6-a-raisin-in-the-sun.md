# 5.6 A Raisin in the Sun

**Author:** Lorraine Hansberry

## Origin of the title

The title comes from Langston Hughes's poem "Harlem", which asks what happens to a dream deferred — and offers, as its first possibility, that it dries up like a raisin in the sun.

The play takes its structure from that question. Every member of the Younger family has a deferred dream, and the drama is what deferral does to each of them.

## Premise

The play is about the Younger family, an African American family living in a small apartment. They receive a **$10,000 life insurance cheque** after the death of Big Walter, the father. Each family member wants to use it differently.

| Character | Wants the money for |
|---|---|
| **Lena Younger (Mama)** | To buy a house for the family |
| **Walter Lee Younger** | To invest in a liquor store for financial support |
| **Beneatha Younger (Bennie)** | Her medical school tuition |
| **Ruth Younger** | Supports Mama, but worries about the family's future |
| **Travis Younger** | Has his own dream of being a bus driver |

The cheque is the play's engine: it makes the family's competing dreams **mutually exclusive**, so that granting one denies the others.

## Walter's emotional climax

The key question of Walter's arc is which moment marks his real change. The candidates:

- When he first tells Mama about his business plan
- When he finds out the money is gone
- **When he tells Lindner they will move in anyway**
- When he wants to impress George Murchison

The **third** is the emotional climax. Losing the money is his lowest point, not his turning point — the change happens when, having lost everything, he refuses Lindner's offer to buy the family out of the white neighbourhood and asserts the family's dignity instead. It is the first moment he chooses pride over money, which is the reversal the whole play has been building.

## Themes

**Dreams deferred.** Each character's dream is postponed by poverty and by racism, and the play tests Hughes's question on four people at once.

**Family and sacrifice.** The money belongs to Mama by right, but the play treats it as the family's shared future — and every decision about it is a decision about who the family is.

**Race, housing, and segregation.** Lindner's offer dramatises the mechanisms of residential segregation: not an outright ban, but a neighbourly proposal backed by money.

**Dignity versus assimilation.** Beneatha's arguments with George Murchison, and her interest in her African heritage, pose the same question the Lindner scene poses to Walter, in a different register.

**Gender and ambition.** Beneatha wants to be a doctor; Ruth's needs are consistently subordinated to Walter's. The play notices both.
