# 4.1 Myth and the Hero's Journey

Myths from unrelated cultures repeat the same shapes. This lesson covers the framework for explaining **why** they do — Campbell's four functions and the hero's journey — plus the biblical material that supplies most allusions in English literature.

## The four functions of myth

According to Joseph Campbell, myths serve four main functions:

| Function | Purpose |
|---|---|
| **Metaphysical** | Teach people about a society's **spiritual systems** |
| **Cosmological** | Explain **why the world is the way it is** |
| **Sociological** | Show **cultural values** and teach people how to live in a society |
| **Pedagogical** | The theory and practice of **teaching** — shaped by beliefs, both cultural and practical |

The functions are a useful comparison tool: two myths may tell entirely different stories while serving the same function, or tell similar stories for different purposes. Comparing Greek and Norse creation accounts, for instance, is mostly a **cosmological** comparison; comparing their heroes is mostly **sociological**.

## The hero's journey

The hero's journey is **the way most myths work** — a recurring structure Campbell identified across cultures.

Its events:

1. **Hero of the known world** — the ordinary starting point
2. **Call to adventure**
3. **The threshold** — crossing out of the known world
4. **Road of trials**
5. **The god figure** — a meeting with the divine or with a mentor
6. **The boon** — what the hero gains
7. **Return**
8. **Changed world**

Note that the world changes, not only the hero. A journey that ends with everything as it was is not this structure.

## Prometheus and Pandora

**Prometheus** stole fire from the gods and gave it to humanity, and was punished eternally for it — bound while an eagle consumed his regenerating liver. The myth is **cosmological** (explaining how humans came to have fire and civilisation) and **sociological** (warning about defiance of divine authority while celebrating the benefactor).

**Pandora** was given a container she was forbidden to open; opening it released every evil into the world, leaving only hope inside. The two myths belong together: Prometheus's gift to humanity is answered by Pandora as humanity's punishment.

## Monsters

**Monsters often reflect or symbolise a society's fears at a specific time.** They show and exaggerate the dangers of a particular thing, usually to discourage or frighten people.

*Frankenstein* embodies anxiety about science outrunning moral responsibility; modern films and books about artificial intelligence going wrong do the same work for a different century. Asking *what was this society afraid of?* is usually the fastest route into a monster text — and the same question opens *Dr Jekyll and Mr Hyde* in 5.3.

## The Old and New Testaments

Biblical allusion is common enough in English literature that the basic distinction is worth holding:

**Old Testament** — the story of creation and the history of the Jewish people (the Torah), together with the messages about the coming of the messiah, which Christians read as pointing to Jesus.

**New Testament** — the story of the birth, death, and resurrection of Jesus; the stories Jesus told; and the messages and history of the beginnings of the Church.
