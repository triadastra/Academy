# 10.1 Transformations

A transformation moves or resizes a figure according to a rule. This lesson covers the four standard transformations, the coordinate rules for each, and symmetry — which is what happens when a transformation maps a figure onto itself.

## Isometries

An **isometry** is a transformation that **preserves distances**. Translations, reflections, and rotations are all isometries; dilations are not, since they change size.

The image of $\triangle ABC$ is written $\triangle A'B'C'$, with $A'$ the image of $A$, and so on.

## Translation

A **translation** (a slide) is an isometry that moves **all points of a figure the same distance in the same direction**.

$$(x, y) \rightarrow (x + a,\ y + b)$$

A **composition of translations** is the result of applying two or more in sequence — and is itself a translation.

## Reflection

A **reflection** is an isometry in which a figure and its image have **opposite orientation**. Every reflection has a **reflection line**.

To draw one:

1. Draw lines **perpendicular to the reflection line** through each point.
2. Measure the distance from each point to the line.
3. Mark the image point on the **opposite side, at the same distance**.

The reflection line is the perpendicular bisector of the segment joining each point to its image — the construction of 1.4 in a new setting.

Coordinate rule across $y = x$: $(x, y) \rightarrow (y, x)$.

Reflection is the only one of the isometries that reverses orientation; a reflected figure cannot be slid or turned back onto the original.

## Rotation

A **rotation** turns a figure around a fixed point by a specified angle and direction.

- The **centre of rotation** is the fixed point. Every point moves along a circular arc around it.
- The **angle of rotation** is the measure by which the figure is turned.
- Direction is **counterclockwise unless stated otherwise**; clockwise corresponds to a negative angle.

Coordinate rules about the origin:

- **90° counterclockwise:** $(x, y) \rightarrow (-y, x)$
- **180°:** $(x, y) \rightarrow (-x, -y)$

To draw one, locate the centre and turn each point by the given angle. To *find* an unknown centre of rotation, construct the perpendicular bisectors of two segments joining points to their images; **their intersection is the centre**, since the centre is equidistant from each point and its image.

## Dilation

A **dilation** changes the **size** of a figure — an enlargement or reduction — about a **centre** and by a **scale factor** $s$.

- Centre at the origin: $(x, y) \rightarrow (sx,\ sy)$
- Centre at $(h, k)$: $(x, y) \rightarrow \big(h + s(x - h),\ k + s(y - k)\big)$

A dilation produces a figure **similar** to the original, in the sense of 7.1: angles are preserved, lengths scale by $s$. If $s > 1$ the figure enlarges; if $0 < s < 1$ it reduces.

## Symmetry

**Symmetry** means a figure can be mapped onto itself by a transformation.

- **Line (reflectional) symmetry** — the figure can be reflected over a line onto itself.
- **Rotational symmetry** — the figure can be rotated about a point by **less than 360°** onto itself.
- **Point symmetry** — a **180° rotation** maps the figure onto itself.

Point symmetry is a special case of rotational symmetry.

### Lines of symmetry

| Figure | Lines of symmetry |
|---|---|
| Isosceles trapezoid | 1 |
| Square | 4 |
| Regular pentagon | 5 |
| Parallelogram | 0 (unless a rectangle or rhombus) |

A **regular $n$-gon has $n$ lines of symmetry** and rotational symmetry of $\frac{360°}{n}$. A regular pentagon therefore has rotational symmetry of $72°$.

Letters make quick examples: **V** has one line of symmetry and no rotational symmetry; **H** has two lines of symmetry.
