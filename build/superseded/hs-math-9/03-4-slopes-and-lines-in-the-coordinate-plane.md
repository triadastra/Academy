# 3.4 Slopes and Lines in the Coordinate Plane

Coordinate geometry lets geometric questions be answered by calculation. This lesson covers slope — the number that captures a line's direction — and what it tells you about parallel and perpendicular lines.

## The slope formula

For two points $(x_1, y_1)$ and $(x_2, y_2)$:

$$m = \frac{y_2 - y_1}{x_2 - x_1}$$

Slope measures **change in $y$ per unit change in $x$** — how steeply the line rises or falls.

## Reading a slope

- **Positive slope** — the line **rises**: as $x$ increases, $y$ increases.
- **Negative slope** — the line **falls**: as $x$ increases, $y$ decreases.
- **Horizontal lines** have slope $m = 0$ — no change in $y$, so the numerator is zero.
- **Vertical lines** have an **undefined** slope — no change in $x$, so the denominator is zero and the fraction is undefined.

The distinction between zero and undefined is worth being precise about. Zero slope is a perfectly good number describing a flat line; an undefined slope means the formula produces no number at all. They are not two kinds of "no slope".

## Parallel and perpendicular lines

Slope gives an algebraic test for the relationships studied in 3.1 and 3.2:

- **Parallel lines have equal slopes.** Same direction, never meeting.
- **Perpendicular lines have slopes that are negative reciprocals** — their product is $-1$.

Vertical and horizontal lines are the exception to the second rule, since one slope is undefined; they are perpendicular, but the product test cannot be applied.

## Why this matters

Coordinate geometry converts a proof into a computation. To show a quadrilateral is a parallelogram, compute the slopes of its sides and check that opposite pairs are equal; to show an angle is right, check that two slopes multiply to $-1$. The synthetic proofs of Unit 2 and the coordinate methods here are two routes to the same conclusions, and part of the skill is choosing the easier one for a given problem.
