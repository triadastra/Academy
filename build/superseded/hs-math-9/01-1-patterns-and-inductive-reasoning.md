# 1.1 Patterns and Inductive Reasoning

Geometry begins with noticing. This lesson covers **inductive reasoning** — the process of forming a general claim from observed patterns — and the counterexample, which is the only thing needed to destroy one.

## Inductive reasoning

**Inductive reasoning** is reasoning based on patterns you observe. If you observe a pattern in a sequence, you can use it to predict what comes next.

Consider the sequence:

$$1,\ 2,\ 6,\ 24,\ 120,\ \ldots$$

Each term is the next factorial: $1!, 2!, 3!, 4!, 5!$. The pattern suggests the next term is $6! = 720$.

## Conjectures

A **conjecture** is the conclusion you reach using inductive reasoning.

In the example above, the conjecture is that the sequence is the factorials, and that the next term is 720.

A conjecture is a reasonable guess, not a proved fact. This is the essential limitation of inductive reasoning: no number of confirming cases proves a general claim, because the next case might break it. Inductive reasoning suggests what is true; it does not establish it. (Deductive reasoning, which does establish it, is the subject of Unit 2.)

## Counterexamples

A **counterexample** is an example for which the conjecture is incorrect.

The asymmetry between confirming and refuting a conjecture is worth stating plainly. A thousand examples that fit a conjecture leave it unproved. **One counterexample disproves it outright.**

This is why, when asked to decide whether a general statement is true, the efficient first move is to hunt for a counterexample. If you find one, you are finished. If you cannot find one after genuine effort, you have reason to attempt a proof.
