# 3.2 Proving Lines Parallel

The previous lesson started from parallel lines and deduced angle relationships. This lesson reverses the direction: given the angle relationships, conclude that the lines are parallel. As 2.1 established, the converse of a true statement needs its own justification — which is why each result here is stated separately.

## The converses

**Postulate 3-2 (Converse of the Corresponding Angles Postulate):** If two lines and a transversal form **corresponding angles that are congruent**, then the two lines are **parallel**.

**Theorem 3-5 (Converse of the Alternate Interior Angles Theorem):** If two lines and a transversal form alternate interior angles that are congruent, then the lines are parallel.

**Theorem 3-6 (Converse of the Same-Side Interior Angles Theorem):** If two lines and a transversal form same-side interior angles that are supplementary, then the lines are parallel.

**Theorem 3-7 (Converse of the Alternate Exterior Angles Theorem):** If alternate exterior angles are congruent, the lines are parallel.

**Theorem 3-8 (Converse of the Same-Side Exterior Angles Theorem):** If same-side exterior angles are supplementary, the lines are parallel.

## Using them

The five converses mirror the five results of 3.1 exactly, with hypothesis and conclusion exchanged. In practice this means:

- To find an **angle**, given that lines are parallel → use 3.1.
- To prove lines **parallel**, given angle facts → use 3.2.

Identifying which direction a problem needs is usually the whole difficulty. If the diagram is marked with parallel arrows, you are in 3.1; if the question asks you to show two lines are parallel, you are in 3.2.

## Constructing parallel and perpendicular lines

The converses justify the classical constructions, since each works by *creating* the angle relationship that forces parallelism.

### Constructing a line through N parallel to line L

1. Label two points $H$ and $J$ on $L$, and draw $\overline{HN}$.
2. Construct angle 1 with vertex at $N$ so that $\angle 1 \cong \angle NHJ$, and the two are **corresponding angles**.
3. The line through $N$ along that ray is parallel to $L$.

The construction is valid precisely because of Postulate 3-2: congruent corresponding angles force the lines to be parallel. Copying the angle is the technique from 1.4.

### Constructing a perpendicular from point R to line L

1. Open the compass to a size greater than the distance from $R$ to $L$. With the compass point on $R$, draw an arc intersecting $L$ at two points, $E$ and $F$.
2. Place the compass point on $E$ and draw an arc.
3. Keeping the same setting, place the compass point on $F$ and draw an arc intersecting the previous one. Label the intersection $G$.
4. Draw line $RG$.

Both $R$ and $G$ are equidistant from $E$ and $F$, so $RG$ is the perpendicular bisector of $\overline{EF}$ — the same equidistance principle as the constructions in 1.4.
