# 4.1 Congruent Triangles

Two figures are congruent when one can be laid exactly on the other. For triangles, you do not need to check all six pairs of parts — certain combinations of three are enough. This lesson covers those shortcuts and what to do once congruence is established.

## Congruent figures

**Congruent figures** have the same size and shape, and can exactly fit each other when moved. **Matching vertices are corresponding vertices**, and their matching sides and angles are **corresponding parts**.

Order matters when naming congruent triangles. Writing $\triangle ABC \cong \triangle DEF$ asserts that $A$ corresponds to $D$, $B$ to $E$, and $C$ to $F$ — so the statement carries all six correspondences.

**Theorem 4-1:** If two angles of one triangle are congruent to two angles of another triangle, then the **third angles are congruent**. This follows immediately from the angle-sum theorem: if two pairs match, the remaining angles must both equal $180°$ minus the same total.

## The congruence postulates

Six pairs of corresponding parts must match for congruence, but only three are needed to guarantee it — in the right combinations:

- **SSS (Side-Side-Side)** — three pairs of sides congruent.
- **SAS (Side-Angle-Side)** — two sides and the **included** angle.
- **ASA (Angle-Side-Angle)** — two angles and the **included** side.
- **AAS (Angle-Angle-Side)** — two angles and a **non-included** side.
- **HL (Hypotenuse-Leg)** — for **right triangles only**: hypotenuse and one leg.

Two combinations that do **not** prove congruence:

- **AAA** — equal angles give the same shape but not the same size; this is *similarity*, covered later.
- **SSA** — two sides and a non-included angle can produce two different triangles.

The word **included** is what separates SAS from SSA. In SAS the angle sits between the two sides; in SSA it does not, and the triangle is not determined.

**HL** is the one exception where a non-included arrangement works, and it works only because the right angle is already fixed.

## CPCTC

Once two triangles are known to be congruent:

> **Corresponding Parts of Congruent Triangles are Congruent (CPCTC).**

This is the standard second half of a proof. First establish congruence using one of the postulates above, then use CPCTC to conclude that some *other* pair of parts — the one the question actually asked about — is congruent.

The order is fixed and cannot be reversed: CPCTC is a consequence of congruence, never a way of proving it.
