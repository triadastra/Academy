# 11.1 Solids: Surface Area and Volume

This lesson moves from plane figures to three dimensions. The organising insight is that surface area is always a plane-area calculation in disguise — unfold the solid and add up the flat pieces — while volume follows a small number of related formulas.

## Polyhedra

A **polyhedron** is a three-dimensional shape whose surfaces are polygons.

- A **face** is one of those polygons.
- An **edge** is a segment formed by the intersection of two faces.
- A **vertex** is a point where three or more edges meet.

A **cross section** is the shape formed by the intersection of a solid and a plane.

**Euler's Formula:** for any polyhedron,

$$F + V = E + 2$$

where $F$, $V$, and $E$ are the numbers of faces, vertices, and edges. In two dimensions it reduces to $F + V = E + 1$, where $F$ is the number of regions formed by $V$ vertices and $E$ edges.

## Prisms and cylinders

A **prism** is a polyhedron with at least two congruent parallel faces, the **bases**; the remaining faces are **lateral faces**.

A **cylinder** has two congruent parallel **circular** bases. Unrolling its curved surface gives a rectangle — which is exactly why its lateral area is a perimeter times a height.

- An **altitude** is a perpendicular segment joining the planes of the bases; the **height** $h$ is its length.
- A prism or cylinder may be **right** or **oblique**.

**Theorem 11-1 (Prism):** $L.A. = ph$ and $S.A. = L.A. + 2B$, where $p$ is the base perimeter and $B$ the base area.

**Theorem 11-2 (Cylinder):** $L.A. = 2\pi rh$ and $S.A. = L.A. + 2\pi r^2$.

The two are the same formula: base perimeter times height, since the circumference $2\pi r$ *is* the perimeter of a circle.

## Pyramids and cones

A **pyramid** is a polyhedron in which one face (the base) may be any polygon, and the other faces are triangles meeting at a vertex.

- The **altitude** is the perpendicular segment from the vertex to the plane of the base.
- The **slant height** $\ell$ is the length of the altitude of a **lateral face**.
- A **regular pyramid** has a regular polygon base and congruent lateral faces.

A **cone** has a circular base. In a right cone the altitude runs from the vertex to the centre of the base, and the **slant height** is the distance from the vertex to a point on the circumference.

**Theorem 11-3 (Regular Pyramid):** $L.A. = \tfrac{1}{2}p\ell$ and $S.A. = L.A. + B$.

**Theorem 11-4 (Cone):** $L.A. = \pi r\ell$ and $S.A. = L.A. + \pi r^2$.

Do not confuse the **height** with the **slant height**. The height, the slant height, and the base radius form a right triangle, so the Pythagorean theorem converts between them — a frequent first step in these problems.

## Volumes

- **Prism:** $V = Bh$
- **Cylinder:** $V = \pi r^2 h$
- **Theorem 11-8 (Pyramid):** $V = \tfrac{1}{3}Bh$
- **Theorem 11-9 (Cone):** $V = \tfrac{1}{3}\pi r^2 h$

The factor of $\tfrac{1}{3}$ is the pattern: **a pyramid or cone holds one third of the prism or cylinder with the same base and height.**

## Spheres

A **sphere** is the set of all points in three-dimensional space equidistant from a fixed point, the **centre**.

- A **radius** joins the centre to a point on the sphere.
- A **diameter** passes through the centre with both endpoints on the sphere.
- A **great circle** is the largest circle on the sphere, containing the centre.

**Theorem 11-10 (Surface Area):**

$$S.A. = 4\pi r^2 = \pi d^2$$

**Volume:**

$$V = \tfrac{4}{3}\pi r^3$$

The surface area is four times the area of a great circle — a memorable relationship, and a useful check that the formula has been recalled correctly.
