# 1.2 Points, Lines, and Planes

Geometry is built on three terms that are deliberately left undefined, together with statements accepted without proof. This lesson introduces those foundations, since every later theorem rests on them.

## The three basic figures

- A **point** is a location. It has **no size**, and is represented by a small dot and named with a capital letter.
- A **line** is a series of points that extends in two opposite directions **without end**. It can be named by any two points on it, or by a single lowercase letter.
- A **plane** is a **flat surface with no thickness**. It extends without end, and contains many lines and points.

Each is idealised. A drawn dot has size and a drawn line has width, but the objects they represent do not. Geometry reasons about the ideal objects, and the drawing is only an aid.

## Collinear and coplanar

- **Collinear points** are points that lie on the same line.
- **Coplanar** points and lines are those that lie in the same plane.

## Postulates

A **postulate**, or **axiom**, is an accepted statement of fact — something assumed without proof, so that other things can be proved from it.

Three postulates about points, lines, and planes:

- **Postulate 1-1** — Through any two points there is **exactly one** line.
- **Postulate 1-2** — If two lines intersect, then they intersect in **exactly one point**.
- **Postulate 1-3** — If two planes intersect, then they intersect in **exactly one line**.

Note the phrase *exactly one* in all three. Each postulate asserts both existence and uniqueness, and problems often turn on the uniqueness half.

## Always, sometimes, or never

A standard exercise asks whether a statement is always, sometimes, or never true. Working through these tests whether the postulates have really been understood:

- **Intersecting lines are _____ coplanar.** *Always* — two intersecting lines determine a plane.
- **Two planes _____ intersect in exactly one point.** *Never* — by Postulate 1-3, planes that intersect do so in a line, not a point.
- **Three points are _____ coplanar.** *Always* — any three points lie in some plane.
- **A plane containing two points of a line _____ contains the entire line.** *Always*.
- **Four points are _____ coplanar.** *Sometimes* — three always are, but a fourth may lie off that plane.
- **Two lines _____ meet in more than one point.** *Never* — by Postulate 1-2, unless they are the same line.

The pattern to notice: *three* points are always coplanar but *four* are only sometimes. Three points is the minimum that determines a plane, and beyond that minimum the guarantee disappears.
