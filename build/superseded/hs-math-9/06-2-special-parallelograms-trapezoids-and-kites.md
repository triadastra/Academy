# 6.2 Special Parallelograms, Trapezoids, and Kites

Each special quadrilateral has a signature diagonal property, and those properties are both what the shapes give you and what identifies them. This lesson covers rhombi, rectangles, squares, trapezoids, and kites.

## Diagonal properties

**Theorem 6-9:** Each diagonal of a **rhombus bisects two angles** of the rhombus.

**Theorem 6-10:** The diagonals of a **rhombus are perpendicular**.

**Theorem 6-11:** The diagonals of a **rectangle are congruent**.

The converses give tests rather than properties:

**Theorem 6-12:** If one diagonal of a parallelogram bisects two angles, the parallelogram is a **rhombus**.

**Theorem 6-13:** If the diagonals of a parallelogram are **perpendicular**, it is a **rhombus**.

**Theorem 6-14:** If the diagonals of a parallelogram are **congruent**, it is a **rectangle**.

The pattern is worth committing to memory:

| Shape | Diagonals |
|---|---|
| Rhombus | Perpendicular, and bisect the angles |
| Rectangle | Congruent |
| Square | Both — congruent **and** perpendicular |

A **square** inherits both, since it is both a rhombus and a rectangle.

## Areas from diagonals

Because a rhombus has perpendicular diagonals, its area is

$$A = \tfrac{1}{2} d_1 d_2$$

where $d_1$ and $d_2$ are the diagonal lengths. The same formula applies to a kite, for the same reason.

## Trapezoids

A **trapezoid** has exactly one pair of parallel sides, called the **bases**; the nonparallel sides are the **legs**.

$$A = \frac{(a + b)h}{2}$$

where $a$ and $b$ are the base lengths and $h$ is the height — the perpendicular distance between the bases. The formula is the average of the two bases times the height, which is the sense in which a trapezoid sits between a rectangle and a triangle.

**Special trapezoids:**

- **Right trapezoid** — has a right angle.
- **Isosceles trapezoid** — has congruent legs.

**Theorem 6-15:** The **base angles of an isosceles trapezoid are congruent**.

**Theorem 6-16:** The **diagonals of an isosceles trapezoid are congruent**.

### The midsegment

The **midsegment** of a trapezoid connects the midpoints of the two legs. It is **parallel to the bases**, and its length is the **average of the base lengths**:

$$m = \frac{a + b}{2}$$

This is the trapezoid counterpart of the triangle midsegment theorem in 5.1 — and in fact the triangle version is the special case where one base has length zero.

### Strategies for trapezoid problems

Trapezoids are usually handled by cutting them into shapes you already know:

- Drop **altitudes from the upper base to the lower base**, giving two right triangles and a rectangle.
- Cut into a **triangle and a parallelogram**.
- For an isosceles trapezoid, cut into a **right triangle and a right trapezoid**.
- **Construct lines parallel to the legs.**
- **Extend the legs to intersect at a point** — especially useful for isosceles trapezoids, since it produces an isosceles triangle.

## Kites

**Theorem 6-17:** The **diagonals of a kite are perpendicular**.

A kite has two pairs of adjacent congruent sides. One diagonal is the perpendicular bisector of the other, which follows from the perpendicular bisector theorem of 5.1: both endpoints of the long diagonal are equidistant from the endpoints of the short one.
