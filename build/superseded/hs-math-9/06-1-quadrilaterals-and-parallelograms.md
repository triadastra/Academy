# 6.1 Quadrilaterals and Parallelograms

Quadrilaterals form a hierarchy, and knowing which shapes are special cases of which is half the subject. This lesson classifies them, then states the properties of parallelograms and the conditions that prove a quadrilateral is one.

## Classifying quadrilaterals

- **Parallelogram** — a quadrilateral with **both pairs of opposite sides parallel**.
- **Rhombus** — a parallelogram with **four congruent sides**.
- **Rectangle** — a parallelogram with **four right angles**.
- **Square** — a parallelogram with **four congruent sides and four right angles**.
- **Trapezoid** — a quadrilateral with **exactly one** pair of parallel sides.
- **Isosceles trapezoid** — a trapezoid whose nonparallel opposite sides are congruent.
- **Kite** — a quadrilateral with **two pairs of adjacent sides congruent** and no opposite sides congruent.

The hierarchy: rhombus, rectangle, and square are all parallelograms, and a **square is both a rhombus and a rectangle** — it satisfies both extra conditions.

Note that a trapezoid has *exactly* one pair of parallel sides, which is what keeps parallelograms out of that category.

## Properties of parallelograms

**Theorem 6-1:** **Opposite sides** of a parallelogram are **congruent**.

**Theorem 6-2:** **Opposite angles** of a parallelogram are **congruent**.

**Theorem 6-3:** The **diagonals** of a parallelogram **bisect each other**.

**Theorem 6-4:** If three or more parallel lines cut off congruent segments on one transversal, then they cut off congruent segments on every transversal.

## Proving a quadrilateral is a parallelogram

**Theorem 6-5:** If **both pairs of opposite sides** of a quadrilateral are congruent, then it is a parallelogram.

The converses of Theorems 6-1 to 6-3 all hold, so a quadrilateral is a parallelogram if **any** of the following is true:

- both pairs of opposite sides are parallel (the definition);
- both pairs of opposite sides are congruent;
- both pairs of opposite angles are congruent;
- the diagonals bisect each other;
- one pair of opposite sides is both parallel **and** congruent.

The last is the most efficient in proofs, since it requires establishing only one pair — but it needs *both* conditions on that pair. One pair parallel and a different pair congruent does not suffice.

## Coordinate proofs

Quadrilaterals are often handled with the coordinate methods of 3.4. To identify a quadrilateral with given vertices — say $(-4, 4)$, $(-6, 0)$, $(-4, -4)$, $(-2, 0)$ — compute the slopes of the sides to test for parallelism and perpendicularity, and the lengths to test for congruence. Equal slopes on opposite sides prove a parallelogram; a slope product of $-1$ proves a right angle.
