# 8.1 AI Fundamentals and History

## Weak and strong AI

**Weak AI (Narrow AI)** is designed for **specific tasks**. Examples: Siri, Alexa, recommendation systems, self-driving vehicles.

**Strong AI (General AI)** is theoretical human-level intelligence capable of **any intellectual task**. It is **not yet achieved** and remains a research goal.

Everything that currently exists — including the systems that appear most general — is weak AI. The distinction is about breadth, not competence: a system can be superhuman at one task and still be narrow.

## Key elements of AI

Three ingredients are required:

- **Processing power** — high-performance hardware such as GPUs and TPUs. Massive memory plus extremely rapid processing.
- **Quality data** — large, clean datasets for training.
- **Effective algorithms** — machine learning models and neural networks.

All three must be present. The algorithms behind modern neural networks date from the 1980s; what changed was the availability of the other two.

## History of AI

**1950 — Alan Turing** asks: *"Can machines think?"* He offers a test, now famously known as the **Turing Test**, in which a human interrogator tries to distinguish between a computer and a human from text responses alone.

**1956 — John McCarthy** coins the term **"artificial intelligence"** at the first-ever AI conference, at Dartmouth College.

**1967 — Frank Rosenblatt** builds the **Mark 1 Perceptron**, the first computer based on a neural network that "learned" through trial and error.

**1980s** — Neural networks using a **back-propagation** algorithm to train themselves become widely used in AI applications.

**1995 — Stuart Russell and Peter Norvig** publish the first major textbook on AI, *Artificial Intelligence: A Modern Approach*. It sets out **four potential goals or definitions of AI**, differentiating computer systems on the basis of **rationality versus human-likeness**, and **thinking versus acting** — a 2×2 that is still the standard framing.

**1997 — IBM's Deep Blue** defeats chess champion Garry Kasparov.

**2016 — DeepMind's AlphaGo** defeats Lee Sedol at Go.

**2023 — Rise of large language models (LLMs)** such as ChatGPT.

## The milestones in summary

| Year | Event | Significance |
|---|---|---|
| 1950 | Turing Test proposed | Framework for evaluating machine intelligence |
| 1997 | Deep Blue defeats a chess champion | AI excels at **rule-based** games |
| 2016 | AlphaGo defeats a Go champion | AI masters **complex, intuitive** strategy |
| 2023 | Rise of LLMs | Natural language processing reaches new heights |

The gap between 1997 and 2016 is the informative one. Deep Blue succeeded largely by **searching** — evaluating enormous numbers of positions with hand-written rules. Go's branching factor makes that approach hopeless, so AlphaGo had to **learn** an evaluation instead. The shift from search to learning is the central story of the period.
