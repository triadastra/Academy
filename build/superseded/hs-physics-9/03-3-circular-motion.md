# 3.3 Circular Motion

An object moving in a circle at constant speed is accelerating, because its direction is changing. This lesson covers uniform circular motion, the force that produces it, why centrifugal force does not exist, and how banked curves let a vehicle turn without relying on friction.

## Uniform circular motion

**Uniform circular motion (UCM)** is movement at constant speed around a circle.

- **Velocity** is always **tangential** to the circle.
- **Acceleration** is always directed **toward the centre** — this is **centripetal acceleration**.

The apparent paradox — constant speed but non-zero acceleration — dissolves once acceleration is understood as the rate of change of *velocity*, a vector. The magnitude is unchanged; the direction changes continuously, and that is a change in velocity.

**Centripetal force** is the net force required to keep an object moving in a circle. It is not a new kind of force: some real force — tension, friction, gravity — must supply it.

## Centrifugal force is not real

**There is never a centrifugal force.** This is the most important point in the lesson.

A passenger in a turning car feels pushed outward. The explanation:

1. **Draw the free-body diagram** and identify the forces genuinely acting on the passenger.
2. **Identify the real force**: an inward force — friction from the seat, or a push from the door — keeps the passenger moving in a circle.
3. **Explain the sensation**: the body tends to continue in a straight line, by Newton's first law, so it feels as though something pushes outward. What is actually happening is that the car turns and the passenger's inertia resists.

The outward feeling is an inertial effect, not a force.

## Unbanked curves

When a car goes around a flat curve, **static friction provides the centripetal force** — static, not kinetic, as long as the tyres do not slip.

If the friction is insufficient, the car **tends to move more nearly in a straight line**, by Newton's first law, continuing along the tangent.

If the tyres slip, the friction becomes **kinetic**, which is bad in two ways: kinetic friction is generally smaller than the maximum static friction, and it opposes the car's motion rather than pointing usefully toward the centre — making it hard to regain control and continue around the curve.

The **maximum speed on an unbanked curve is independent of the mass of the car**, since both the required centripetal force and the available friction scale with mass, and the mass cancels.

## Banked curves

**Banked curves** are tilted to help vehicles turn **without relying on friction**.

Banking is used to:

- avoid relying on friction when turning on a road;
- allow **planes to turn**, since they have no friction with a surface to use;
- allow **race cars to travel at higher speeds**;
- reduce **wear on tyres**.

For every banked curve there is one **design speed** at which **no friction is required** — the horizontal component of the normal force supplies exactly the centripetal force needed.

Away from that speed, friction makes up the difference:

- **Too fast** — friction acts to keep the car from sliding **outward**.
- **Too slow** — friction acts to keep the car from sliding **inward**.

As with the unbanked case, the **banked design speed is independent of the mass of the car**.
