# 4.1 Forces and Equilibrium

This lesson applies vector addition to forces. The organising idea is **equilibrium** — the condition in which forces cancel — together with the related concepts of the equilibrant, apparent weight, and how forces resolve on an inclined plane.

## Equilibrium

An object is in **equilibrium** when the **net force acting on it is zero**. This happens when the object is **at rest** *or* **moving with constant velocity**.

That second case is the one worth pausing on. Equilibrium does not mean stationary; it means unaccelerated. A car at a steady 60 km/h on a straight road is in equilibrium.

Graphically, **if the force vectors form a closed triangle (or polygon), the net force is zero** and the object is in equilibrium — the same closed-polygon rule from 3.1.

Whether three given forces can produce equilibrium is therefore a question about whether they can close a polygon. Forces of 10 N, 15 N, and 20 N can, because no one of them exceeds the sum of the other two. Forces of 100 N, 150 N, and 300 N cannot: 300 N is greater than $100 + 150$, so the two smaller forces cannot together balance the largest, whatever their directions.

## The equilibrant

The **equilibrant** is the force that balances all the others. It has the **same magnitude as the resultant but acts in the opposite direction**.

- **For two forces**, the equilibrant is equal in magnitude and opposite in direction to their resultant.
- **For three or more forces**, first find the resultant of the forces, then take the equilibrant equal in magnitude and opposite in direction.

For example, a force of 1000 N at 15° north of east requires an equilibrant of **1000 N at 15° south of west**.

## Apparent weight

**Apparent weight** is the force exerted by a scale on the object resting on it.

- **In equilibrium**, with no acceleration, apparent weight equals true weight.
- **When accelerating**, they differ — which is why a lift feels heavy as it starts upward and light as it starts downward.

This connects to 1.1: a scale reads the force it exerts, which equals weight only when nothing is accelerating.

## Motion along a level surface

For **constant-speed motion**, the applied force equals the friction force — the two balance, so the object is in equilibrium.

If the applied force doubles, the object **accelerates**, with friction remaining constant. Friction does not rise to match an increased applied force once the object is already sliding, which is why the balance breaks and acceleration begins.

## Motion along an inclined plane

On an incline, the **gravitational force splits into two components**:

- one **parallel to the slope**, which causes motion down the slope;
- one **perpendicular to the slope**, which determines the normal force.

Resolving weight along and perpendicular to the surface — rather than horizontally and vertically — is what makes incline problems tractable, because the motion is along the slope. Choosing axes to match the motion is a general problem-solving move, not a trick specific to inclines.

## Drag and terminal velocity

**Drag force** resists the motion of an object through a fluid such as air or water. It depends on:

- the **motion of the object** — its speed;
- the **properties of the object** — size and shape;
- the **properties of the fluid** — viscosity and temperature.

**Terminal velocity** follows from this. A falling object initially accelerates downward; as it speeds up, drag increases; when drag grows equal to weight, the net force is zero, the object stops accelerating, and it falls at a constant terminal velocity. Terminal velocity is simply free fall reaching equilibrium.
