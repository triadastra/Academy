# 5.2 Conservation of Energy

Energy is never created or destroyed, only moved and converted. This lesson covers the distinction between conservative and non-conservative forces, the conservation of mechanical energy, what happens when friction is present, and the definitions of power and efficiency.

## Conservative and non-conservative forces

- A **conservative force** does work that is **path-independent** — the work depends only on the start and end points. Gravity is the standard example. Energy is not lost, and the net work around a closed loop is zero.
- A **non-conservative force** does work that **depends on the path taken**. Friction is the standard example; it removes energy from the system.

A practical test: **if a potential energy can be defined for a force, that force is conservative.** Potential energy depends only on position, so it can only exist where the work depends only on position.

## Mechanical energy and its conservation

**Mechanical energy** is the sum of the kinetic and potential energies in a system:

$$E = KE + PE$$

**The principle of conservation of mechanical energy**: when **only conservative forces act**, the total mechanical energy of a system remains constant.

$$E_i = KE_i + PE_i = E_f = KE_f + PE_f$$

Including elastic potential energy, this is:

$$KE_i + GPE_i + EPE_i = KE_f + GPE_f + EPE_f$$

The conditional clause — *only conservative forces* — is what the next section relaxes.

## The law of conservation of energy

Energy can be **transformed from one form to another**, and **work is done when energy is transferred from one object to another**.

> **The total energy is neither increased nor decreased in any process.** Energy can be transformed from one form to another and transferred between objects, but the total amount remains constant.

## Energy conservation with dissipative forces

A **dissipative force** reduces the **mechanical** energy of a system but not its **total** energy. Friction is the example.

The energy is not destroyed — it becomes thermal energy. **The thermal energy produced by a constant friction force equals the work done by friction.** A box sliding down a ramp with friction loses mechanical energy, and exactly that much energy appears as heat.

For motion with both gravity and friction:

> The initial mechanical energy equals the final mechanical energy plus the energy transformed by friction.

$$\tfrac{1}{2}mv_i^2 + mgh_i = \tfrac{1}{2}mv_f^2 + mgh_f + F_{fr}d$$

The friction term is what the mechanical-energy-only version was missing.

## Power

**Power** is the **rate at which work is done** — equivalently, the rate at which energy is transformed. Average power is the work done divided by the time taken:

$$\bar{P} = \frac{W}{t} = \frac{\Delta E}{t}$$

The SI unit is the **watt (W)**, where:

- $1\ \text{W} = 1\ \text{J/s}$
- $1\ \text{kW} = 1000\ \text{W}$
- $1\ \text{hp (horsepower)} = 746\ \text{W}$

As with work, force and displacement should be in the same direction.

Power distinguishes *how much* energy is transferred from *how fast*. Two engines may do the same work; the more powerful one does it in less time.

## Efficiency

**Efficiency** is the ratio of the useful power output of an engine to the power input:

$$e = \frac{P_{\text{out}}}{P_{\text{in}}}$$

**Efficiency is always less than 1.0.** No engine can create energy, and no engine can transform energy from one form to another without some energy going to friction and thermal energy.

Note that this does not contradict conservation of energy — it follows from it. The energy is all still there; some of it has simply become a form the engine cannot use.
