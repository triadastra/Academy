# 1.2 Graphing Data

A graph turns a table of numbers into a relationship you can see, and it is the standard way experimental results are presented in physics. This lesson covers identifying variables, drawing a graph correctly, recognising the common relationships, and using a graph to predict.

## Identifying variables

- The **independent variable** is the factor that is changed or manipulated during an experiment.
- The **dependent variable** is the factor that depends on the independent variable.

A **line graph** shows how the dependent variable changes with the independent variable.

Deciding which is which is the first step of any experiment, because it determines the axes and, more importantly, the direction of the claim being tested.

## Drawing a graph

**1. Choose a simple scale.** Use a scale that avoids awkward numbers — 1 large square = 1 N or 2 N, rather than 3 or 7 — and ensure the graph uses **most of the paper**. A cramped graph wastes precision.

**2. Label the axes.** The **independent variable goes on the $x$-axis** (horizontal), the **dependent variable on the $y$-axis** (vertical).

**3. Plot the data.** Mark **at least five data points**, using an "X" for each.

**4. Draw the line or curve.** If the points form a straight line, draw the **best-fit line** — a single straight line passing as near as possible to all the points, not a zigzag joining them. If they form a curve, draw a smooth curve through them.

## Anomalous points

If a point lies far from the line, **check the measurement again**. This is an **anomalous point**, and you may choose to ignore it.

The obligation is to re-check first. An anomalous point may be a mistake, or it may be the most interesting result in the experiment; discarding it without looking is how real effects get missed.

## Types of relationship

**Linear relationship** — represented by

$$y = mx + b$$

where $m$ is the slope and $b$ the $y$-intercept. The slope may be positive, negative, or zero.

There is a special case worth naming: **if the graph passes through the origin, the variables are directly proportional** — if one doubles, so does the other. A straight line alone is not enough for proportionality; it must also pass through the origin.

**Quadratic relationship** — the curve rises more quickly as $x$ increases.

**Inverse relationship** — a hyperbola, in which $y$ decreases as $x$ increases.

## Predicting values from a graph

Once a line is drawn, it can be used to estimate values that were not measured. For a spring tested with different weights, plotting weight in grams against length in centimetres allows you to read off the length at, say, 20 g — either from the graph directly or from the equation of the best-fit line.

Reading between measured points is interpolation and is generally reliable; extending beyond them is extrapolation, and assumes the relationship continues to hold.
