# 7.1 Two Kinds of Mass and Einstein's Theory of Gravity

Mass appears in physics in two apparently unrelated roles: as resistance to acceleration, and as the source of gravitational attraction. There is no obvious reason these should be the same quantity — and the discovery that they are led Einstein to a new account of gravity itself.

## Inertial mass

**Inertial mass** measures how much an object **resists a change in motion** when a force is applied. Informally, it is how "stubborn" an object is when you try to push it.

**How it is measured:** apply a force to the object and measure how much it accelerates. If you push two objects with the same force, the one with the **larger inertial mass accelerates less** — this is $F = ma$ read as a definition of $m$.

Note that gravity plays no part in this definition. Inertial mass could be measured in deep space.

## Gravitational mass

**Gravitational mass** measures how strongly an object **interacts with the force of gravity**.

**How it is measured:** with a balance scale, as when weighing something on Earth. It is the mass that appears in Newton's law of universal gravitation.

## Are they the same?

**Yes.** According to the **Principle of Equivalence**, inertial mass equals gravitational mass.

This is a genuinely surprising result rather than a definition. One quantity describes resistance to being pushed by *any* force; the other describes the strength of one *particular* force. Nothing in Newtonian mechanics requires them to be equal — yet every experiment finds that they are.

The equality also explains Galileo's result from 2.4. A heavier object is pulled harder by gravity (greater gravitational mass) but is correspondingly harder to accelerate (greater inertial mass), and the two effects cancel exactly. That is why **all objects fall with the same acceleration**.

## Einstein's theory of gravity

**Einstein used the Principle of Equivalence as the foundation for his theory of gravity.**

In Einstein's view, **gravity is not a force** in the way Newton described. Instead, **mass bends space, and objects move along this curved space**.

An object in free fall is not being pulled; it is travelling the straightest available path through a geometry that mass has curved. This reframes the equivalence of the two masses from a coincidence into a necessity — if gravity is a property of space rather than a force acting on objects, then everything must follow the same paths regardless of composition or weight.

## Acceleration due to gravity at a distance

Newton's law still describes gravity accurately in ordinary conditions. Solving it for the acceleration due to gravity at Earth's surface gives the familiar $g = 9.8\ \text{m/s}^2$.

**Far from Earth's surface, the gravitational force decreases** as distance from Earth's centre increases. Writing $r$ for the distance from Earth's centre and $R_E$ for Earth's radius, the acceleration due to gravity at distance $r$ falls off with $r^2$ — which is why $g$ is treated as constant only near the surface, where $r \approx R_E$ throughout the motion.
