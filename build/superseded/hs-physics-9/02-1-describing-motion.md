# 2.1 Describing Motion

Before motion can be measured it has to be described precisely, and that requires more care than it first appears — whether an object is moving at all depends on what you compare it to. This lesson sets up the vocabulary: frames of reference, the vector/scalar distinction, and the difference between distance and displacement.

## The branches of the subject

- **Mechanics** is the study of motion and force.
- **Kinematics** is the study of motion — how things move.
- **Dynamics** is the study of force — why they move.

This chapter is kinematics: describing motion without asking what causes it.

## Frames of reference

An object is **in motion if it changes position relative to a frame of reference**. Whether an object moves therefore **depends on your point of view**.

A **frame of reference** is a place or background used to compare whether an object is in motion, and it is **always assumed to be stationary**.

This is not a technicality. A passenger seated on a train is stationary relative to the carriage and moving at speed relative to the platform, and both statements are correct. Any claim about motion is incomplete until the frame is stated.

## Coordinate systems

A **coordinate system** defines a zero point and the direction of increasing values. The **origin** is the point where both variables equal zero.

Setting up a coordinate system is what allows direction to be expressed as a sign: once a positive direction is chosen, motion the other way is negative.

## Vectors and scalars

- A **vector** has both **magnitude and direction** — position, displacement, velocity.
- A **scalar** has **magnitude only** — distance, time, speed.

Vectors are represented by arrows, where length shows magnitude and orientation shows direction.

## Position, distance, and displacement

Three related terms that are constantly confused:

- **Position** is an arrow from the origin to the object. It is a vector, and it says *where an object is*.
- **Distance** is how far apart objects are, or how far something travelled. It is a **scalar**.
- **Displacement** is the **change in position** — the vector distance moved in a particular direction.

Distance and displacement both describe a *change* of location; position describes a *location*. And displacement carries direction while distance does not, which is why an object that returns to its start has travelled a non-zero distance but has zero displacement.

**Time interval** is the difference between two times, with the SI unit of seconds.

## Motion diagrams

A **motion diagram** is a series of images showing the positions of a moving object at equal time intervals. Its purpose is to visualise displacement and velocity — equal spacing means constant velocity, and changing spacing means acceleration.

The **particle model** is a simplified motion diagram in which the moving object is replaced by a series of points. Reducing the object to a point removes its size and shape from the problem, which is legitimate whenever those do not affect the motion being studied.
