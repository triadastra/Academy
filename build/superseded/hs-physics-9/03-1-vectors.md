# 3.1 Vectors

Motion in one dimension needs only a sign to express direction. Motion in two dimensions needs vectors. This lesson covers how vectors are represented, the three methods for adding them, subtraction, multiplication by a scalar, and resolution into components.

## Representing a vector

A **vector** has magnitude and direction; a **scalar** has magnitude only. Examples of scalars are mass, speed, and time.

A vector is drawn as **a line with an arrow**, where the **length gives the magnitude** and the **orientation gives the direction**. In symbols it is written $\vec{A}$ — boldface with a small arrow — while $A$ in italics refers to the magnitude alone.

### Stating direction

Direction must always be defined or stated in the final answer. Several conventions are available:

- **Signs**, for straight-line motion: up and right positive, down and left negative.
- **Compass bearings**, such as 40° north of east.
- **Plain directions**: up, down, left, right.

The same direction can be described in more than one way — 30° west of north is the same as 30° from the $-y$ axis, and 30° west of south is the same as 60° south of west. **If a question provides compass directions, use them**, since converting invites error.

### Drawing to scale

Use a ruler and compass, and choose a **scale** — for example 1 cm = 10 m/s, so 40 m/s east is drawn 4 cm long pointing east. A scale bar makes large vectors manageable.

## Adding vectors

The **resultant** (or net vector) is the sum of two or more vectors. Three methods give the same answer.

### Tail-to-tip method

Move the tail of one vector to the head of another, **without changing its magnitude or direction**. The resultant points **from the tail of the first vector to the head of the last**.

Vector addition is **commutative**: $\vec{A} + \vec{B} = \vec{B} + \vec{A}$, so the order does not matter.

**If three or more vectors form a closed polygon, there is no resultant** — the net vector is zero.

### Parallelogram method

Place the two vectors **tail-to-tail**, use them as adjacent sides to construct a parallelogram, and the **diagonal from the common origin is the resultant**.

### Pythagorean theorem

For vectors at right angles, calculate the resultant mathematically:

$$R = \sqrt{A^2 + B^2}, \qquad \theta = \arctan\left(\frac{b}{a}\right)$$

The arctangent gives the angle **only when the vectors are perpendicular**.

For example, forces of 30 N due north and 40 N due east give a resultant of $\sqrt{30^2 + 40^2} = 50$ N, at $\arctan(30/40)$ north of east.

### Two facts about magnitudes

- The magnitude of a vector sum is **not always greater** than the magnitude of either contributing vector — opposing vectors partly cancel.
- The magnitude of the resultant of two vectors is **never greater than the sum** of their individual magnitudes.

## Subtracting vectors

Two vectors are **negatives** of each other if they have the same magnitude but opposite directions.

Subtraction is addition of the negative:

$$\vec{A} - \vec{B} = \vec{A} + (-\vec{B})$$

So no new technique is needed: reverse the second vector and add.

## Multiplying a vector by a scalar

A vector $\vec{A}$ multiplied by a scalar $c$ gives a vector of magnitude $cA$:

- if **$c$ is positive**, the product has the **same direction**;
- if **$c$ is negative**, the product has the **opposite direction**.

## Resolving vectors into components

Any vector can be broken into **$x$ and $y$ components** using trigonometry. Adding vectors by components is the general method:

$$R_x = \sum A_x, \qquad R_y = \sum A_y$$

then combine them with the Pythagorean theorem for magnitude and the arctangent for direction.

This is the approach to use when vectors are **not** at convenient right angles to each other — resolve each into perpendicular components first, and the geometry becomes right-angled again.

## Relative velocity: wind and current

Vector addition explains everyday combinations of motion:

- A **tailwind** comes from behind and increases velocity.
- A **headwind** comes from the front and decreases it.
- A **crosswind** comes from the side and can affect both components.

A boat travelling at 30 m/s due east across a river 120 m wide, with a current of 12 m/s south, has a resultant velocity found by adding the two vectors — and, because the eastward component is unaffected by the current, the crossing time depends only on that component.
