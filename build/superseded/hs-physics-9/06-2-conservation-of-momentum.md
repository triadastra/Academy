# 6.2 Conservation of Momentum

Momentum has a property that makes it exceptionally useful: under the right conditions it does not change at all. This lesson states the law of conservation of momentum, defines the conditions it requires, and applies it to recoil — the case where two objects push apart from rest.

## The law

> **The momentum of an isolated system of objects remains constant.**

Equivalently: **the momentum of any closed, isolated system does not change.**

Two definitions are needed, and both conditions must hold:

- A **closed system** does not gain or lose mass.
- An **isolated system** has **zero net external force** — the only significant forces are those between the objects within the system.

The reason this follows from 6.1 is straightforward. If the net external force is zero, then $\Delta \vec{p}/\Delta t = 0$, so the total momentum cannot change.

## Applying it to collisions

**The sum of the momenta of the colliding objects is the same before and after the collision** in a closed, isolated system:

$$\sum \vec{p}_{\text{before}} = \sum \vec{p}_{\text{after}}$$

The forces the objects exert on each other during the collision are *internal* to the system, and internal forces cannot change the system's total momentum — they only redistribute it between the objects.

This is what makes the law so powerful in practice. Collisions involve large, rapidly changing, hard-to-measure forces, and conservation of momentum lets you ignore all of them and compare only the states before and after.

## Recoil problems

**Recoil** is the case where two objects, initially at rest, push apart. Examples are **rocket propulsion** and **gun recoil**.

For rocket propulsion, consider the rocket and its fuel as an isolated system with no external forces. **In the reference frame of the rocket, the total momentum of rocket plus fuel is zero** before launch.

Since total momentum is conserved and started at zero, it must remain zero. Therefore, after the separation:

- The **momenta of the two objects are equal in magnitude and opposite in direction** — they must cancel.
- Their velocities are related by

$$\vec{v}_{Af} = -\frac{m_B}{m_A}\vec{v}_{Bf}$$

- The velocities therefore **depend on their relative masses**, and the **less massive object acquires the greater velocity**.

This is why a rifle recoils more slowly than its bullet travels, and why a rocket can accelerate by throwing a comparatively small mass of exhaust away very fast. Equal and opposite *momenta* do not mean equal and opposite *speeds*.
