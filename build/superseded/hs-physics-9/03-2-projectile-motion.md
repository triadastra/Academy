# 3.2 Projectile Motion

A projectile follows a curved path, which looks like a harder problem than straight-line motion. It is not — because the horizontal and vertical motions are **independent**, and each is a problem already solved. This lesson establishes that independence and shows how to use it.

## Definitions

- A **projectile** is an object shot through the air, moving under the influence of gravity alone (ignoring air resistance).
- Its **trajectory** is the curved flight path it follows, which is **parabolic** in the ideal case.

## The independence of motion

The central principle:

> **Analyse the horizontal and vertical motion separately.**
> **Horizontal:** constant velocity ($a_x = 0$).
> **Vertical:** free fall ($a_y = g$).

Because the only force is gravity, and gravity acts vertically, there is nothing to change the horizontal velocity. The horizontal motion is uniform motion from 2.2; the vertical motion is free fall from 2.4.

Two consequences follow, and both are examined regularly:

- **The horizontal and vertical motions are independent.**
- **The time from launch to landing is the same for both** — time is the quantity that links them, and it is the only one. This is why solving for time in one direction lets you solve the other.

The **velocity vector is always tangent to the parabola** at any point.

### The monkey and hunter problem

A hunter aims at a monkey that drops from its branch at the instant the shot is fired. Where should the hunter aim?

**Directly at the monkey.** The bullet and the monkey experience the same gravitational acceleration, so both undergo the same vertical displacement in the same time. Whatever distance gravity pulls the bullet below its aim line, it pulls the monkey the same distance below its branch.

This is the clearest demonstration of independence: the bullet's horizontal motion does not affect how fast it falls.

## Solving projectile problems

The general method is to list the known quantities separately for each direction, use one direction to find the **time of flight**, and then use that time in the other direction.

### Launched horizontally

The initial vertical velocity is zero, so the **time of flight is determined entirely by the height** it falls. Once time is known, the **horizontal range** is $x = v_x t$, since horizontal velocity is constant.

Note what this means: **flight time is influenced by the height, not by the horizontal velocity.** An object launched horizontally and one dropped from the same height land at the same moment.

### Launched at an angle

Resolve the initial velocity into components using 3.1. A football kicked at 45° above the horizontal with an initial velocity of 10.0 m/s has both a horizontal and a vertical component, and the standard quantities to find are:

- **flight time** — from the vertical motion;
- **maximum height** — where the vertical velocity is zero;
- **range** — the horizontal distance, from $x = v_x t$;
- **velocity and acceleration at maximum height** — the vertical velocity is zero, the horizontal velocity is unchanged, and **the acceleration is still $g$ downward**;
- **velocity at a given moment**, found by combining the components at that time.

The point about maximum height is worth repeating from 2.4: velocity is momentarily zero in the vertical direction only. The projectile is still moving horizontally and still accelerating downward.
