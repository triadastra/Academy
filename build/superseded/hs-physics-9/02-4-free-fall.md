# 2.4 Free Fall

Free fall is the most important special case of constant acceleration, and historically it is where the modern treatment of motion began. This lesson covers Galileo's result, the value of $g$, and how to handle objects thrown upward as well as dropped.

## What free fall means

**Free fall** is the motion of a falling object when air resistance is negligible and the motion can be considered due to gravity alone.

The qualification matters. Objects falling through air experience **fluid friction** (air resistance), and **air resistance affects lighter objects more than heavier ones** — a feather more than a rock. Free fall is what remains once that complication is removed.

## Aristotle and Galileo

**Aristotle** held that heavier objects fall faster. Everyday observation appears to support this, because everyday observation includes air resistance.

**Galileo's model** came from rolling objects down inclines: a **steeper incline produces faster acceleration**, and a vertical incline gives the **maximum acceleration** — free fall. Using inclines slowed the motion enough to measure it, which is why the result was obtainable at all.

**Galileo's hypothesis** is that at a given location on Earth, and in the absence of air resistance, **all objects fall with the same acceleration**:

- no matter what the falling object is made of;
- no matter how much it weighs;
- no matter what height it was dropped from;
- no matter whether it was dropped or thrown upward.

## Acceleration due to gravity

The acceleration due to gravity on Earth is:

$$g = 9.8\ \text{m/s}^2$$

directed downward at Earth's surface. There are **small variations in $g$ with latitude and elevation**.

**Defining the direction is essential** before solving any problem. If downward is chosen as positive, $g = +9.8\ \text{m/s}^2$; if upward is positive, $g = -9.8\ \text{m/s}^2$. Either convention works; mixing them mid-problem does not.

## Solving free-fall problems

The four equations of motion for constant acceleration apply unchanged, because $g$ is a constant acceleration. They can be used **regardless of whether there is an initial upward velocity, an initial downward velocity, or none at all**.

### Objects dropped from rest

The initial velocity is zero, and the object accelerates downward at $g$ throughout.

### Objects launched upward

The initial velocity must be non-zero and directed upward. The acceleration is **constant at $g$ for the whole flight** — this is the point students most often get wrong.

- **On the way up**, velocity decreases to zero at maximum height.
- **At the top**, velocity is zero but acceleration is still $g$. The object is not in equilibrium at its highest point; it is momentarily at rest while still accelerating.
- **On the way down**, velocity increases as the object falls.

Because the acceleration never changes, the entire flight can be treated as a single constant-acceleration problem rather than as two separate journeys.
