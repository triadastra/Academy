# 5.1 Work and Energy

Work is the link between force and energy: it is what a force does when it moves something. This lesson defines work precisely — including the cases where a force does none — introduces kinetic and potential energy, and establishes the work–energy theorem.

## Work

**Work** is the use of a force to move an object some distance — the transfer of energy when a force moves an object.

More precisely, work is the product of the magnitude of the displacement and the **component of the force parallel to the displacement**.

Its unit is the **joule (J)**, and it is a **scalar**: it has magnitude but no direction, though it may be positive or negative.

### The formulas

When the force is constant and in the same direction as the motion:

$$W = Fd$$

When the force acts at an angle, **at least part of the force must be parallel to the displacement**:

$$W = Fd\cos\theta$$

where $\theta$ is the angle between the directions of the force and the displacement.

For example, a sailor pulling a boat 0.30 m along a dock with a rope at 25.0° to the horizontal does work $W = Fd\cos 25.0°$ — only the horizontal component contributes.

### The sign of work

- **Positive work** — force and displacement are in the same direction, and energy increases.
- **Negative work** — force opposes displacement, as in braking, and energy decreases.
- **Zero work** — the force is **perpendicular** to the motion, as in circular motion.

The perpendicular case is worth dwelling on. A centripetal force does no work at all, however large it is, because it never has a component along the direction of travel. This is why an object in uniform circular motion keeps a constant speed.

## Energy

**Energy** is the ability of an object to produce a change in itself or the world around it.

### Kinetic energy

**Kinetic energy (KE)** is the energy an object has due to its motion:

$$KE = \tfrac{1}{2}mv^2$$

It depends on mass and speed — **faster or heavier objects have more kinetic energy** — and is measured in joules.

Because $v$ is squared, doubling the speed quadruples the kinetic energy while doubling the mass only doubles it. Speed matters disproportionately.

To find **speed from kinetic energy**, rearrange the formula and **take the square root at the end**. To find **mass**, rearrange similarly. In both cases check that units are consistent — kilograms for mass, metres per second for speed.

### Potential energy

**Potential energy (PE)** is the energy associated with forces that depend on the **position or configuration** of a system.

**Gravitational potential energy** is the stored energy in a system resulting from the gravitational force between Earth and an object:

$$PE = mgh$$

where $m$ is mass, $g$ the acceleration due to gravity, and $h$ the height above a **reference level**.

Two points that are easily missed:

- The work done by an external force to move an object from one point to another **with no acceleration** equals the change in potential energy.
- **Potential energy belongs to a system, not a single object.** Gravitational PE is a property of the object *and* Earth together, which is why the reference level can be chosen freely — only changes in PE are physically meaningful.

## The work–energy theorem

> **The net work done on an object is equal to the change in the object's kinetic energy.**

$$W_{\text{net}} = \Delta KE$$

The theorem is **valid only if $W$ is the net work** done on the object — the work of all forces combined, not of one force in isolation.

It is the practical tool for problems asking for a **final speed, a braking distance, or a required force**: identify the initial speed, applied force, and distance, relate the work to the change in kinetic energy, and solve.

If net work is positive, kinetic energy **increases**; if negative, it decreases.

## Work done by a varying force

When force changes with position, work is the **area under the force–displacement graph** — the area between the curve and the $x$-axis.

For a linear variation this is the area of a triangle or trapezoid. This is the same "area under the graph" principle used in 2.3, applied to a different pair of quantities.
