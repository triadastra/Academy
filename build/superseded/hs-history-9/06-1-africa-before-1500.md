# 6.1 Africa Before 1500

## Geography

Africa's environments include the **Sahara Desert**, rainforests, **savannas**, and highlands. The **Great Rift Valley** served as an important passageway.

**Resources and trade:** minerals such as **gold and salt** were the valuable commodities, and **camels revolutionised trade across the Sahara** by making the crossing survivable. Gold and salt seem an odd pair until you notice that salt was scarce in West Africa and essential for preserving food, while gold was abundant there and scarce elsewhere — each side had what the other could not get.

## The Bantu migrations

West African farmers and herders migrated **south and east between about 100 BCE and 1000 CE**, spanning from West to South Africa.

As Bantu speakers moved, they **spread farming, ironworking, and domestication**, and their **language and culture mixed** with the groups they encountered.

The migration is the reason a family of related languages is spoken across a third of a continent, and the reason iron-using agriculture reached most of sub-Saharan Africa. It is one of the largest population movements in world history.

## Nubia

**Nubia** lay along the Nile, south of Egypt, with a prosperous civilisation based on trade and agriculture. Trade led to contact with Egypt, and the two became **rivals for control of the area and its trade**. From about **1500 BCE Nubia was under Egyptian control for almost 500 years**, after which it reasserted itself and at one point ruled Egypt.

## Axum and Ethiopia

**Axum** lay southeast of Nubia, on the **Red Sea**, replacing Kush as the regional power. Its people descended from **African farmers and Middle Easterners**, producing a distinctive culture and the **Geez** language. Legend traces the founding of Axum and the Ethiopian royal dynasty to the son of **Solomon and the Queen of Sheba** — a dynasty that lasted into the twentieth century. The land was first mentioned in a Greek guidebook around 400 CE.

**Trade** made Axum wealthy. It controlled a **triangular trade network** linking Africa, India, and the Mediterranean, dealing in **ivory, animal hides, gold, iron, spices, and textiles** — and carrying ideas along with goods.

**Christianity** spread to Axum by the **300s** and became the official religion under **King Ezana**. Christian churches replaced older temples. Axum's ties with North Africa and the Mediterranean strengthened at first but **weakened with the rise of Islam**, and the resulting isolation led to civil war and economic decline.

**Ethiopia** maintained its independence through **geographic isolation — protected by mountains — and a strong Christian identity**. **King Lalibela's rock-hewn churches**, carved downward out of solid stone, exemplify Ethiopian architectural skill. Ethiopian Christians kept connections with the Holy Land while absorbing local customs, including the observance of **Jewish holidays and dietary laws**. Ethiopian kings claimed descent from Solomon and the Queen of Sheba, and some practised Judaism; the **Falasha** community lived in the mountains until the late 1900s.

Ethiopia is the significant exception in African history: an ancient Christian state that survived the expansion of Islam and, later, was never colonised.

## The East African city-states

As Axum declined, city-states such as **Kilwa, Mogadishu, and Mombasa** emerged along the coast. **Arab and Persian merchants** established Muslim communities from the **600s**.

Trade flourished thanks to the **monsoons** — alternating wind currents that blow reliably in one direction for part of the year and the reverse for the rest, making round trips to India and Arabia predictable rather than speculative. This is the same wind system that governs Indian agriculture (2.1), doing different work.

The result was a genuinely blended culture:

- The **Swahili** language emerged, incorporating many **Arabic words** and written in **Arabic script**
- **Architecture** blended East African and Arabic styles

**Ibn Battuta**, a Moroccan judge, documented his travels across Africa and Asia, describing the trading traditions of **Mogadishu** and the hospitality and merchant practices he found — a first-hand source for the wealth and cultural richness of these cities.

## Great Zimbabwe

**Great Zimbabwe** was a powerful **inland** empire, built by **Bantu-speaking peoples between 900 and 1500**, known for its impressive **stone enclosures and palaces** built without mortar.

It **tapped gold resources** and had links with the coastal cities; artefacts found there indicate connections to the coastal trade networks and skilled local artisans.

It declined by **1500** due to **overpopulation, civil war, and external pressure from Portuguese traders**.

## Kingdoms and trade in West Africa

The West African kingdoms — **Ghana**, then **Mali**, then Songhai — were built on control of the **trans-Saharan trade** in gold and salt.

**Islam** spread south across the Sahara into the **Niger River valley**, carried by **Muslim traders**, both Arabs and local converts, who brought Islamic values, culture, and **legal traditions**. This contributed directly to **state formation**, notably in the kingdom of **Mali**: a written legal tradition and a shared religion with one's trading partners are considerable administrative advantages.

**Timbuktu** became a major commercial hub for trans-Saharan trade, dealing in gold, salt, and other goods, with a well-developed social and economic system and a significant community of **Islamic scholars**. **Leo Africanus** described its wealth, including its trade in precious goods and its use of currency such as **cowrie shells and gold**.

## Focus question

*What influence did religion and trade have on the development of East Africa?*

The two are inseparable. Trade brought Muslim merchants, whose religion, language, and law then reshaped the coastal societies — producing Swahili, the mosque-and-coral architecture of Kilwa, and a legal framework that made further trade easier. Religion travelled as a by-product of commerce, and then made commerce work better.
