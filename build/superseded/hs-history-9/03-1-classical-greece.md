# 3.1 Classical Greece

## Geography and the polis

After the **Greek Dark Ages** (c. 1100–800 BCE), Greece was **divided by mountains** into scattered **poleis** — city-states. The terrain is the fundamental fact: mountains and sea fragmented Greece into hundreds of separate communities, which is why Greece produced city-states rather than an empire, and why those city-states spent so much of their history fighting each other.

## Athens: the road to democracy

Athens had serious social problems that forced change — poverty, debt, and debt-slavery among them. A sequence of reformers responded.

**Draco and the archons.** An **archon** was a leader of the aristocratic government and supreme judge. Draco created a **strict code of law** to curb crime — so severe that **"draconian"** still means excessively harsh. He extended voting rights to anyone who could afford armour.

**Solon**, elected archon in **594 BCE**, continued the work: he **cancelled or reduced debts**, **eliminated slavery for debt**, and abolished the "helot" class in Athens.

**Cleisthenes** finished what the earlier archons started and **empowered the common people**:

- **Redesigned electoral districts** so they were no longer divided by wealth
- Created the **Council of Five Hundred** — 500 people chosen **by lot** to help run the government
- Made it possible for most citizens to participate at least once in their lives, and for **any free man** to hold power
- Introduced **ostracism**: if 6,000 votes were cast against a man, he was exiled
- Established large **juries** — as many as 1,501 at once — to protect against corruption

The design principle behind selection by lot and enormous juries is worth noticing: both make **bribery impractical**. You cannot buy an official who was chosen at random the week before, or a jury of 1,501 people.

The limits matter too. "Any free man" excluded women, slaves, and foreign residents — the majority of Athens's population.

## Sparta

Sparta was founded by descendants of the **Dorians**, who invaded Greece around 1200 BCE, and was located on the **Peloponnese**. It invaded its neighbours and enslaved many of them.

**Between 65% and 85% of the population were enslaved helots**, who farmed the large estates. "Citizen" meant the land-owning leisure class, who by definition owned helots and did not work.

Everything about Spartan society follows from that ratio. A citizen minority ruling an enslaved majority several times its size must be permanently armed and permanently vigilant, so Sparta became a full-time military state — **behind on arts and science, deeply conservative, and afraid of change and outside influence**.

Athens and Sparta are therefore not merely different characters but different solutions to the problem of who does the work.

## The Persian Wars

**Start.** The Persians conquered the Greek city-states of **Ionia** in Asia Minor. In **499 BCE** Athens sent ships to help the Ionian Greeks rebel against Persian rule — giving Persia a reason to invade Greece itself.

**Marathon (490 BCE).** A Persian army landed near Marathon, and the Athenians defeated it in **hand-to-hand combat**.

**The Persian return.** The Spartans formed the first defensive army and were defeated. The Persians marched south and **burnt Athens**, whose people had already evacuated.

**Salamis.** The Athenians used a fleet to defeat the Persians by **luring the Persian navy into the narrow strait of Salamis**, where the larger Persian fleet could not manoeuvre. In **479 BCE** the Greeks defeated the Persians in Asia Minor.

Salamis is the model case of a smaller force choosing the ground: the strait converted Persia's numerical advantage into a liability.

## From alliance to empire

Athens was the most powerful city-state after the war, and organised the **Delian League** — an **alliance** in which two or more city-states agreed to cooperate and come to one another's aid.

Athens then **gradually converted its leadership of the League into an Athenian Empire**, treating allied contributions as tribute. This transformation — a defensive alliance becoming an instrument of domination — is the direct cause of the Peloponnesian War with Sparta that followed.

## Greek culture and its transmission

**Arts.** Sculptors used **idealised forms** rather than realistic ones. **Phidias** created the statues for the Parthenon and the statue of Zeus at Olympia.

**Influences on and of Greece.** The civilisations of the Near East and Egypt influenced Greek culture; Greek religion and its features were later adopted into Roman culture; and the **Greek alphabet**, itself derived from the **Phoenicians** (1.3), was adopted by many other civilisations.

Greece is thus a transmitter as much as an originator — a point worth holding against any account that treats Greek achievement as self-generated.
