# 7.3 The Medieval Church

The Church was the one institution that survived Rome's collapse intact, and in a fragmented Europe it was the only body with a presence in every village and authority across every border.

## The Church dominates medieval life

**Conversion and parish life.** Christianity spread across Europe, and the **parish priest** was most people's main contact with the institution. He **celebrated mass, administered the sacraments, preached, explained the Bible**, and later **ran schools**.

**The Church in society.** The church building was the **social centre of the village**. The Church **marked every stage of life** — baptism, marriage, and burial — and required **tithes**, a share of income. **Bishops** managed cathedrals and the wider dioceses.

Because it controlled the sacraments, the Church controlled access to salvation. That is the source of its authority over kings as well as peasants: excommunication was a genuine sanction against anyone who believed.

**Views on women** were mixed. Women were held to be **equal before God** but were also seen as **weak**; **Mary** was presented as the ideal. The Church **set a minimum marriage age** and **protected women** in some respects while **punishing them harshly** in others.

## Monasteries and convents

**Benedictine Rule.** Organised by **Benedict**, monks and nuns took vows of **obedience, poverty, and chastity**. Their days were divided among **worship, work, and study**, and they worked in **fields, workshops, and scriptoria**, producing food and goods and **copying manuscripts**.

The scriptorium is why classical texts survive in the West at all. Every ancient work that reached the Renaissance did so because a monk copied it out by hand, repeatedly, across centuries.

**Service and scholarship.** Monasteries **provided health and educational services**, looked after the **poor and sick**, **sent missionaries**, **preserved learning**, and **taught Latin**.

**Opportunities for women.** Convents allowed women to **escape societal limits**, and some achieved genuine distinction — **Hildegard of Bingen** wrote on theology, medicine, and music. These rights were **later restricted**.

## The growth of Church power

The **pope claimed supremacy** over all Christians. The **clergy held land** — the Church became the largest landowner in Europe — and **influenced politics** throughout the continent.

This produced the defining struggle of medieval Europe: the Church claimed an authority independent of, and superior to, secular rulers, while kings claimed authority over the churches in their territory. The conflict recurs from the eleventh century to the Reformation (8.3), and it did not exist in the same form in the Byzantine east (3.3), where the emperor controlled the church.

## The Crusades

### The world in 1050

Before the Crusades, Europe was far from the centre of things:

- **Islamic civilisation** thrived from Spain to India, with traders and scholars spreading ideas (4.3)
- **China's culture flourished** under the Tang and Song (5.1)
- **Ghana** was a trading empire in West Africa (6.1)
- **India** had prosperous cities
- The **Maya** and other American civilisations flourished (6.2)
- The **Byzantine Empire** faced the Turkish threat

### Causes

- The **Byzantine emperor's plea** for help against the Seljuk Turks
- **Pope Urban II's call** to arms
- **Knights' motives:** wealth, land, and adventure
- **The pope's motives:** increasing papal power, healing the schism with the Eastern church, and **redirecting the internal fighting** of European knights outward

The last of these is easy to miss and genuinely important: Europe had a surplus of armed men with nothing to do but fight each other, and the Crusades gave that violence an external target.

### Events, 1096–1291

- The **First Crusade captured Jerusalem**
- The **Fourth Crusade was diverted to Constantinople**, which the crusaders sacked — see 3.3

### Effects

| Effect | Detail |
|---|---|
| **Trade expansion** | Contact with the wealthier east created demand for its goods |
| **Growth of the money economy** | Financing expeditions required credit and coin |
| **Monarchs' power increased** | Nobles died or sold land to fund crusades; kings gained |
| **Papal power** | Rose, then declined as later crusades failed |
| **A wider worldview** | Europeans encountered a world they had not known |

Note the irony in the second and third rows: an enterprise launched by the papacy ultimately strengthened the kings and the merchants who would later challenge it.

## The Reconquista

The Christian reconquest of Iberia ran in parallel:

- **Toledo** captured in **1085**
- **Portugal** established
- By **1300** Christian kingdoms controlled most of Iberia
- **Granada fell in 1492**

**Aftermath.** **Ferdinand and Isabella expelled non-Christians** — Jews and then Muslims. **Many skilled people left.** Spain **achieved religious unity at a cost**, losing much of the merchant, medical, and scholarly expertise that had made Andalusia prosperous (4.2). 1492 is thus the year Spain both completed the Reconquista and dispatched Columbus — the two are connected, since victory freed resources and ambition for expansion overseas (8.1).
