# 2.2 Ancient China: Qin and Han

## The unification of China

**Shi Huangdi**, originally known as Zheng, unified China in **221 BCE** after defeating the rival states, beginning China's classical age and taking the title "First Emperor".

### Legalism

Shi Huangdi adopted **Legalism**, based on the teachings of **Hanfeizi**. Legalism holds that:

- **Human nature is inherently evil**, so strong governance is necessary to maintain order
- **Strict laws and harsh punishments** are the instruments of that governance

This contrasts directly with Confucianism, which holds that people can be led by moral example. The Qin regime followed its premises to their conclusion: severe measures against dissent, including **book burnings** and the persecution of Confucian scholars.

### Centralisation

To consolidate power, Shi Huangdi:

- **Abolished feudalism**, replacing it with **military districts** run by loyal officials
- Enforced **high taxes** on the peasantry
- **Standardised weights, measures, and currency** to promote unity
- Built **roads and canals** to improve transport
- Built the **Great Wall** against northern invasions

Standardisation is the durable achievement. A single currency and a single set of measures make an empire economically one thing rather than many, and every later dynasty kept the practice.

The **Great Wall** also symbolised the emperor's ability to mobilise resources on a vast scale — and the forced labour it required was among the causes of what followed.

### Collapse

After Shi Huangdi's death in **210 BCE**, discontent over heavy taxation and forced labour produced widespread revolt, and the **Qin dynasty fell in 206 BCE** — barely fifteen years after unifying China. Legalism proved capable of conquering an empire and incapable of holding one.

## The Han dynasty

**Gao Zu**, a **peasant** leader, established the Han dynasty. He restored order, **reduced taxes**, and incorporated **Confucian scholars** into government — reversing the Qin approach while keeping its unified structure. This combination, a centralised Qin-style state legitimated by Confucian ethics, defines Chinese imperial government for two thousand years.

### Emperor Wudi's reforms

**Wudi**, the most renowned Han emperor:

- **Expanded** the empire
- Improved the economy through **infrastructure and granary systems**
- Established a **government monopoly on iron and salt**, providing revenue without raising farm taxes
- Pursued expansion that led to the establishment of the **Silk Road**, linking China with the West and carrying goods, culture, and ideas in both directions

### The civil service system

The Han implemented a **merit-based civil service system**, promoting educated officials over hereditary appointees. **Confucian teachings** guided the training of scholars, emphasising moral integrity and knowledge.

This is one of the most consequential institutions in world history: it created a governing class defined by **examination** rather than birth, and it survived, with interruptions, until 1905. Its later development is covered in 4.1.

## The Han golden age

The Han period is recognised as a golden age of Chinese civilisation:

| Field | Achievements |
|---|---|
| **Science and medicine** | Advances in astronomy and chemistry; **acupuncture** |
| **Technology** | **Paper**, the **rudder**, advanced shipbuilding |
| **Arts** | Literature, poetry, and craftsmanship — including Ban Zhao's *Lessons for Women* |

## Buddhism arrives

By about **100 CE**, Buddhism had spread into China along the trade routes. It offered a spiritual path contrasting with traditional Chinese values, and its emphasis on **personal salvation** appealed particularly in periods of disorder — which is precisely when it grew fastest (see 4.1).

## Legacy

Shi Huangdi, Gao Zu, and Wudi between them established a unified China and a framework of governance and culture that endured for centuries: a centralised bureaucratic state, standardised across a vast territory, staffed by examined officials, and justified in Confucian terms. Later dynasties rose and fell, but they inherited this shape rather than inventing new ones.
