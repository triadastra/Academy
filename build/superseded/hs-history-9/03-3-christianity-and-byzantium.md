# 3.3 Christianity and the Byzantine Empire

## The origins of Christianity

Christianity began with a Jew named **Jesus** in **Judea**, born in Nazareth in the twenty-third year of Augustus and raised in Galilee — born, in Christian belief, to the virgin **Mary**, with God as his father.

Jesus travelled and preached in Judea — Bethlehem, Nazareth, Galilee, and Jerusalem — and performed miracles. His popularity became threatening to Roman leaders, and he was **crucified**. Christians believe he rose from the dead three days later and ascended to heaven.

His **apostles** spread the message, and **four Gospels** on the life of Jesus were written into the New Testament.

Christianity emerged inside the Roman Empire and used its infrastructure: the roads and sea routes built for legions and trade carried missionaries, and the common Greek of the eastern Mediterranean carried the Gospels.

## Constantine and Constantinople

**Constantine** rebuilt the Greek city of **Byzantium** and renamed it after himself — **Constantinople**. Roads fanned out from it, and the Eastern Roman Empire became known as the **Byzantine Empire**.

**Why Constantinople became rich and powerful:** it stood on the shore of the **Bosporus**, the strait linking the Mediterranean and Black Seas — a position that made it the hinge of trade between Europe and Asia. It had an **excellent harbour**, was **guarded on three sides by water**, and had **sea walls on high ground** to strengthen its defences.

Geography made the city both wealthy and nearly impregnable, which is why it survived the western empire by a thousand years.

## Justinian

The Byzantine Empire reached its peak under **Justinian**, who ruled from **527 to 565**.

His accomplishments:

- A vast **construction project** to restore the empire to its past glories — countless cities, buildings, and churches
- The **Hagia Sophia** ("Holy Wisdom"), topped with a spherical dome, serving as the cathedral of Eastern Christianity
- The **Code of Justinian**, which preserved and organised Roman law and unified the empire
- Establishing firm foundations for a **single faith**

The Code is the more consequential of the two great monuments. It transmitted Roman law to later Europe, and is the reason Roman legal concepts underlie most modern legal systems outside the English-speaking world.

## Eastern and Western churches

| | Western Church | Eastern Church |
|---|---|---|
| **Main city** | Rome | Constantinople |
| **Authority** | The **pope** claimed authority over all Christians | The **Byzantine Emperor** controlled church affairs and appointed the **patriarch** |
| **Language** | Latin | Greek |
| **Clergy** | Celibate | **Allowed to marry** |
| **Major holiday** | Christmas | Easter |

The decisive difference is the second row. In the West the church claimed authority independent of any ruler; in the East the emperor controlled the church. That single structural difference shapes the politics of both halves of Christendom for centuries — in the West it produces repeated conflicts between popes and kings (6.3), and in the East it does not.

## The Great Schism

Differences in beliefs, practices, and the use of **icons** provoked a **schism** — a split — between Eastern and Western Christianity: the **Great Schism**. The Eastern church became the **Greek Orthodox Church**, the Western the **Roman Catholic Church**, and the two treated each other as rivals.

## The fall of the Byzantine Empire

Several factors combined:

**Internal weakness.** Struggles over succession, court intrigues, and constant wars undermined the empire's strength.

**The Crusades — invited, then catastrophic.** The Byzantine emperor called for Western help against the **Seljuk Turks**, whose attacks had blocked the pilgrimage routes to Jerusalem; this produced the **First Crusade**. During later crusades, trade rivalry sparked violence between the Byzantines and **Venice**. Venetian merchants persuaded the soldiers of the **Fourth Crusade** to attack **Constantinople** itself: the crusaders **burned and plundered the city**, seized control of Byzantine trade, and drained the empire's wealth.

The empire never recovered. Byzantium was fatally wounded not by its Muslim enemies but by the Western Christians it had asked for help — one of the sharpest ironies in medieval history.
