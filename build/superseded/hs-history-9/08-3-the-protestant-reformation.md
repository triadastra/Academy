# 8.3 The Protestant Reformation

A religious movement of the **1500s** against problems in the Catholic Church, which produced new Christian branches including **Lutheranism** and **Calvinism**.

## Background

**Economic change.** Northern Europeans faced uncertainty as fixed medieval economies shifted to **urban, market-based** ones with **unequal distribution of wealth**.

**Humanist ideas.** Renaissance humanism spread via the **printing press**. Classical education and social reform took root, leading people to **question the Church**.

**Church abuses were rife.** Popes engaged in **political power struggles**, led **lavish lives**, and increased fees for services. Some clergy sold **indulgences** — originally granted for good deeds, but by the late 1400s purchasable with money.

**Earlier revolts had already occurred.** **John Wycliffe** in 1300s England and **Jan Hus** in what is now the Czech Republic protested Church practices. Both were suppressed — which raises the question below about why Luther succeeded where they failed.

## Martin Luther

In **1517** **Johann Tetzel's** sale of indulgences to fund the rebuilding of **St. Peter's** enraged Luther. Tetzel claimed indulgences **guaranteed heaven** for buyers and their dead relatives.

Luther wrote the **95 Theses**, arguing that indulgences had **no basis in the Bible**, that the **pope had no authority over purgatory**, and that **salvation comes through faith**. He nailed them to the church door at **Wittenberg**.

Copies spread rapidly across Europe, sparking debate. Luther **refused to recant** when the Church demanded it, and developed increasingly radical doctrines, urging Christians to **reject Rome's authority** and arguing the Church could be reformed by **secular authorities**.

In **1521 Pope Leo X excommunicated him**. Emperor **Charles V** summoned him to the **Diet of Worms**; Luther refused to give up his writings and was **declared an outlaw**.

### Luther's teachings

- **Salvation by faith alone** — not by Church rituals or purchased indulgences
- **All Christians have equal access to God** through faith and the Bible — the priesthood of all believers
- **The Bible is the sole source of religious truth**, not the pope. Luther **translated parts of the Bible into German** so everyone could read it
- **Rejected some sacraments** — rituals cannot erase sin; only God can
- **Simplified church practice** — banned indulgences, confession, pilgrimages, and prayers to saints; simplified the mass and emphasised **sermons**; **allowed clergy to marry**

Note how these fit together. If scripture is the sole authority and every believer can read it, then the Church's interpretive monopoly is broken — which is why translation into the vernacular was not a side issue but the centre of the programme.

### Why Luther's ideas spread

- **Printing presses** carried his writings across Germany and Scandinavia
- Many **clergy** saw his reforms as a genuine solution to corruption
- Some **German princes embraced Lutheranism for self-serving reasons**: to break free from Church and imperial authority, to **seize Church property**, or out of national feeling

That third reason answers the question about Hus. Luther had the press, and he had powerful princes with a material interest in his success.

### The Peasants' Revolt (1524)

Peasants inspired by Luther's ideas hoped for **social and economic change**, including an end to serfdom. As the revolt grew violent, **Luther denounced it**, and the nobles suppressed it.

*Was it a failure?* In its immediate aims, yes — it was crushed and the peasants gained nothing. But it exposed something important: Luther's revolution was **theological, not social**, and he needed the princes far more than he needed the peasants.

### The Peace of Augsburg (1555)

Allowed **German princes to choose between Catholicism and Lutheranism**. Most northern states chose Lutheranism; southern states remained Catholic. Note what the settlement does **not** grant: the choice belongs to the prince, not to the individual.

## Switzerland and Calvin

**Ulrich Zwingli** in Zurich stressed the importance of the Bible and rejected elaborate rituals.

**John Calvin**, born in France, published his beliefs in **1536**, including **predestination** — that God has long since determined who will be saved, dividing humanity into "saints" and "sinners".

In **1541 Calvin led Geneva**, setting up a **theocracy** run by church leaders. Calvinists emphasised **hard work, discipline, thrift, honesty, and morality**, and imposed **strict rules** — no dancing, no swearing.

*How did predestination change daily life in Geneva?* Logically, if salvation is already decided, behaviour cannot alter it. In practice the doctrine produced the opposite of fatalism: since no one could know their status, disciplined and prosperous living was treated as **evidence** of election — which is why Calvinism is associated with exactly the industriousness its theology seems to make pointless.

**Calvinism spread** to Scotland (**John Knox**), France (the **Huguenots**), the Netherlands, and later America (the **Puritans**), and led to religious wars in Germany, France, the Netherlands, England, and Scotland. Calvinists were widely unpopular, which is part of the background to the **St. Bartholomew's Day Massacre** of Huguenots in France.

## Comparing the three

| | **Catholicism** | **Lutheranism** | **Calvinism** |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Salvation** | Through faith **and good works** | Through **faith alone** | **God predetermines** who is saved |
| **Authority** | Pope and Church tradition | **Scripture alone** | Scripture alone |
| **Clergy** | Celibate | May marry | May marry |
| **Government** | — | Princes choose | **Theocracy** in Geneva |

## Key terms

| Term | Meaning |
|---|---|
| **Indulgences** | Payments to reduce punishment for sins |
| **Diet of Worms (1521)** | The assembly at which Luther refused to recant |
| **Peace of Augsburg (1555)** | Let German princes choose Catholicism or Lutheranism |
| **Predestination** | God has already determined who will be saved |
