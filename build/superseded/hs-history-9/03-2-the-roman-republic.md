# 3.2 The Roman Republic

## Origins

Rome's origin myths centre on **Aeneas**, son of the goddess Venus and a mortal man, and on **Romulus**, who killed his brother **Remus** and gave the city its name.

## The conquest of Italy

At the beginning of the Republic, Rome was surrounded by enemies — Latin communities and others.

| Date | Event |
|---|---|
| **338 BCE** | Rome crushed the Latin states in Latium |
| Next 50 years | Successful struggles with the hill peoples of central Italy; then contact with the Greek communities |
| **267 BCE** | Conquest of southern Italy completed by defeating the Greek cities |
| **264 BCE** | Rome crushed the Etruscan states in the north, and had conquered most of Italy |

### The Roman Confederation

In **338 BCE** the Romans devised the **Roman Confederation**. Some peoples — the Latins — were granted **full Roman citizenship**; the remaining communities were made **allies**, free to run their local affairs but **required to provide soldiers for Rome**. **Loyal allies could improve their status and even become Roman citizens.**

This is the key to Roman expansion, and the sharpest contrast with Athens (3.1). Athens converted its allies into subjects and made itself hated; Rome offered its defeated enemies a path into the Roman citizen body, and turned them into a manpower reserve. It is why Rome could absorb catastrophic defeats and keep fielding armies.

## The Roman state

After the overthrow of the monarchy, Roman nobles established a **republican** government designed to prevent any one man from holding power.

**Consuls and praetors** were the chief executive officers. **Two consuls** were chosen annually to administer government and lead the army — two, so that each could check the other, and annual, so that neither could entrench. The office of **praetor**, created in 336 BCE, handled civil law but could also lead armies and govern Rome when the consuls were away.

**The Roman Senate** could formally only *advise* the magistrates, but its advice was not taken lightly, and by the third century BCE it had virtually the force of law. It comprised about **300 men** drawn from the landed aristocracy, who **remained senators for life**.

**The Centuriate Assembly** was organised so that the **wealthiest citizens always had a majority** — a reminder that the Republic was never a democracy in the Athenian sense.

## The struggle of the orders

In the fifth century BCE the **plebeians** began to seek equality with the **patricians**.

- **471 BCE** — the **Council of the Plebs** was created, a popular assembly for plebeians only
- **Tribunes of the plebs** were given power to protect plebeians from arrest by patrician magistrates
- **Intermarriage** between the orders was permitted (it had been forbidden)
- **287 BCE** — plebeians gained the right to pass laws binding on the whole state

The outcome was not equality but a **new senatorial aristocracy**: because intermarriage was now allowed, a select number of patrician and plebeian families merged and came to dominate the political offices. The plebeian victory produced a wider elite, not the end of elite rule.

## The Punic Wars

**First Punic War (264–241 BCE).** Rome intervened in a struggle between two Sicilian cities; Carthage considered Sicily its territory, and war followed. **Rome developed a navy** — having had none — and won. Carthage gave up all rights to Sicily, which became the **first Roman province**; Corsica and Sardinia followed.

**Second Punic War (218–201 BCE).** Rome encouraged one of Carthage's Spanish allies to revolt. **Hannibal Barca**, a Carthaginian general who had sworn to restore his country's honour, **crossed the Alps** with 30,000–40,000 men and terrorised Italy for fifteen years.

- **Battle of Cannae (216 BCE)** — a catastrophic Roman defeat; Rome was on the brink of disaster.
- **Fabius Maximus**, appointed dictator, **avoided pitched battles and attacked supply lines** — the **Fabian strategy**, which trades territory and reputation for time. It is the classic response to an opponent who is better than you in open battle.
- Rome reconquered the Italian cities that had gone over to Hannibal.
- **Scipio Africanus the Elder** learned from Hannibal's own tactics and pushed the Carthaginians out of Spain by 206 BCE.
- Rome then struck directly at Carthage, forcing Hannibal's recall from Italy, and crushed him at the **Battle of Zama (202 BCE)**.

Carthage lost Spain, and **Rome became the dominant power in the western Mediterranean**.

**Third Punic War (149–146 BCE).** Carthage was destroyed entirely.

## The consequences of conquest

Rome won an empire and, in doing so, wrecked the society that had won it.

**The rich–poor gap widened.** More conquered land meant patricians could buy more land cheaply and staff it with war captives; plebeians got less land and drifted into the cities, unemployed.

**Country life changed.** The **latifundia** — massive farming estates worked by slaves — replaced the small farms that had been the backbone of the Roman state and army. Small farmers simply could not compete with slave labour.

**Culture changed.** An **influx of slaves** and a newly **Hellenised urban culture** followed: Roman literature imitated Greek models and Romans adopted Greek-style baths. Aristocrats gained enormous political and material opportunities.

**Patriotism declined.** With no serious external enemy left, the old Roman frugality and civic devotion faded.

Later wars deepened the pattern — the **Jugurthine War (112–106 BCE)**, the **Social War (91–87 BCE)** in which Italian cities revolted demanding the citizenship their manpower had earned, the **Mithridatic War (88–63 BCE)**, and three **Servile Wars** (135–132, 104–100, and 73–71 BCE), the last led by **Spartacus**.

## The decline of the Republic (133–31 BCE)

**The Gracchi.** **Tiberius and Gaius Gracchus** believed the underlying cause of Rome's problems was the **decline of the small farmer**, and attempted land reform. Both were killed — establishing that Roman political disputes could now be settled by violence.

**Gaius Marius (157–86 BCE).** Consul from 104 to 100 BCE, Marius recruited **volunteers from the urban and rural poor who owned no property**. These soldiers **swore loyalty to their general, not to Rome**, because it was the general who would promise them land — which in turn forced generals into politics to get the necessary laws passed.

This single change is the mechanism of the Republic's death. Marius created a system that **placed enormous power in the hands of individual generals rather than the state**, and every subsequent crisis runs through an army loyal to one man.

**Sulla.** Given command of a war in Asia Minor, he marched on Rome when the Council of the Plebs tried to transfer that command to Marius — the first civil war.

**The First Triumvirate.** **Crassus** (the richest man in Rome, who had suppressed a major slave rebellion), **Pompey** (a military hero from Spain), and **Julius Caesar** (commander in Gaul) formed a coalition. Crassus died in battle in **53 BCE**, leaving Caesar and Pompey in direct competition. The leading senators, judging Pompey less dangerous, voted that Caesar lay down his command and return as a private citizen. **Caesar refused, kept his army, and crossed the Rubicon** into Italy illegally, defeating Pompey and his allies.

Caesar was made **dictator in 47 BCE** and **dictator for life** three years later — after which he was assassinated.

**The Second Triumvirate.** **Octavian** (Caesar's grandnephew and adopted son) took the west; **Antony** took the east and allied himself closely with **Cleopatra VII** of Egypt, despite being married to Octavian's sister; **Lepidus** was the third and least. Octavian crushed Antony's forces at the **Battle of Actium** in Greece.

## Augustus

In **27 BCE Octavian proclaimed himself emperor** — and, simultaneously, announced the **restoration of the Republic**. The Senate gave him the title **Imperator**, commander-in-chief.

The contradiction was deliberate and it worked. Many Romans had believed **Caesar was a tyrant**; Augustus was careful to present himself as an **ordinary citizen** and to maintain the **façade of elected officials rather than dictators**, while holding the reality of power:

- An army of **28 legions** (about 150,000 men)
- The **Praetorian Guard** of roughly 9,000 men to guard the emperor
- **Consul for life**, with power to veto, to make laws, and to summon the Senate

Caesar took the title and was murdered; Augustus took the power and kept the forms, and died in his bed.

## The legacy of the Republic

- The spread of **Greco-Roman culture**
- **Roman law**, including *jus naturale* — the idea of a universal, natural law. Roman law was harmonised with the needs of a global empire and guided largely by **reason and logic**
- **Infrastructure** — well-built military roads radiating from Rome, and roads for civil use
