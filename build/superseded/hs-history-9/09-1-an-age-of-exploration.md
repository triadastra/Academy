# 9.1 An Age of Exploration and Expansion

## The world Europeans entered

**Europeans were not the first in Asian trade.** Indian Ocean trade had thrived for centuries, and Asian and African networks were already well established when Vasco da Gama arrived. His voyage is a key moment for **European** history, not for the trade itself.

**Muslim merchants had long dominated the spice trade**, sailing from ports across Asia. By the thirteenth century Islam had a presence in the Southeast Asian islands and was spreading inland, helped by new sultanates such as **Malacca**, founded in the early fifteenth century.

In **West Africa**, Muslim commercial and religious influence had spread south across the Sahara to the **Niger River valley**, contributing to state formation in kingdoms such as **Mali**, with **Timbuktu** as a major hub of trans-Saharan trade in gold and salt (6.1).

Keeping this in view is essential. The European voyages did not open world trade; they **inserted Europe into a system that already existed**, and much of what follows is about the violence of that insertion.

## Motives: "God, Gold, and Glory"

- **God** — missionary efforts to spread Christianity
- **Gold** — the desire for wealth, especially spices
- **Glory** — the pursuit of fame and national prestige

**Technological advances** made the voyages possible: better ships, and navigational tools including the **compass** and the **astrolabe**.

## The Portuguese maritime empire

**Beginnings.** Portugal began exploring the **west coast of Africa** under **Prince Henry the Navigator**, seeking gold. He established a **school for navigators** and built **forts** along the African coast.

**Voyages to India.** **Vasco da Gama's voyage (1497–99)** was the milestone. He reached **Calicut**, and the voyage was sponsored specifically to **break the Muslim monopoly** on the spice trade. Portugal followed up by establishing trading posts and forts — at **Malacca** among others — to control the routes. The Portuguese **kept the route secret** until the Dutch later discovered it.

**Why it succeeded initially:** a strong navy, advanced ships, and **cannon** technology. Portugal could not out-trade the established networks, but it could out-shoot them, and its strategy was to seize **chokepoints** rather than territory.

**Why it did not last:** over-extension, high costs, and competition from Muslim rivals and then from **Spain, England, and the Netherlands**.

## Dividing the spoils

Portugal and Spain signed the **Treaty of Tordesillas (1494)**, setting a **line of demarcation**: most of the Americas to **Spain**, and areas east — including Africa and Asia — to **Portugal**. Disputes over the exact boundaries arose later, and the treaty was violated repeatedly.

The treaty is worth pausing on: two European powers, with papal blessing, divided the inhabited world between themselves without reference to anyone living in it.

## The conquest of the Americas

**Spanish exploration and conquest.** **Christopher Columbus**, an Italian sailing for Spain, reached the Americas in **1492**. Spanish **conquistadors** — **Hernán Cortés** in Mexico, **Francisco Pizarro** in Peru — conquered the **Aztec and Inca** empires using **superior weapons, alliances with resentful subject peoples, and disease**.

All three factors are necessary to the explanation. Cortés had a few hundred men; he also had tens of thousands of indigenous allies who hated Aztec tribute demands (6.2), and he arrived in a population with no immunity to European disease. Pizarro arrived immediately after an Inca civil war (6.3).

Spain established a large colonial empire covering Mexico, Central America, and much of South America.

**Portuguese in Brazil.** **Pedro Cabral** reached Brazil in **1500**. Portugal established colonies focused first on extracting gold and silver, then on **plantation agriculture** — sugar and later coffee — and developed a multiracial society of European, indigenous, and African peoples.

## Governing the empires

Spain created **viceroyalties** — **New Spain**, **Peru** — to govern vast territories; Portugal developed its own colonial administration in Brazil.

**Latin America became a multiracial society**, with **mestizos** (European-indigenous) and other mixed groups emerging through intermarriage and African slavery. Social status was explicitly racialised: **the closer to pure European descent, the higher one's status**.

**The Catholic Church** played a major role — converting indigenous peoples, building missions, and shaping social and cultural life.

## Exploiting the riches

Europeans exploited the Americas' natural resources, above all **gold and silver**, from mines in **Mexico, Peru, and Bolivia**, shipping the metals to Europe and fuelling economic growth there.

**The encomienda system** granted European settlers land and indigenous **labour**. Its stated main purpose was to **convert indigenous people to Christianity**; in practice it produced **exploitation and abuse**.

**African slaves** were brought to the Americas to work plantations as indigenous populations were **decimated by disease and overwork**.

## Opposing viewpoints

Contemporaries disagreed sharply, and the disagreement is examinable:

- **Gonzalo Fernández de Oviedo** viewed European exploration as a **"march of civilisation"**, bringing Christianity and order
- **Bartolomé de Las Casas** **condemned the brutal treatment** of indigenous peoples and the atrocities committed by conquistadors

Las Casas matters because he demonstrates that the conquest was **contested at the time**, by Spaniards, on moral grounds — the criticism is not merely a modern imposition.

## Competition intensifies

The **English, Dutch, and French** entered the competition for American colonies and trade, establishing settlements in the Caribbean and North America.

## The Columbian Exchange

The transfer of **plants, animals, diseases, and cultures** between the Americas and the Old World.

**Positive effects:** new crops in both directions — potatoes and maize to Europe, Asia, and Africa; wheat, cattle, and horses to the Americas — supporting large population increases.

**Catastrophic effects:** epidemic disease in the Americas.

**Why the disease ran one way.** Europeans did not suffer comparably because indigenous Americans **had no prior exposure** to Old World epidemic diseases — the populations of the two hemispheres had been separated too long to share pathogens, and the Americas had far fewer domesticated herd animals from which such diseases arise. The result was mortality on a scale with few parallels in human history, and it is the single largest factor in the European conquest.
