# 7.1 The Early Middle Ages

## Western Europe in decline

The Roman Empire had included large parts of Western Europe, spread the **Latin language**, and made **Christianity** prominent. After its fall in the west:

- **Social order deteriorated**
- **Political fragmentation** occurred
- The **economy declined severely**
- The region was **cut off from more advanced regions**
- **Classical learning faded**

The period that followed became known as the **Middle Ages**, characterised by blended cultures — Germanic, Roman, and Christian.

It is worth keeping the scale in perspective: while Western Europe was at its lowest point, Byzantium, the Muslim world, Tang China, and Ghana were all flourishing. "The Dark Ages" describes one region, not the world.

## The Germanic kingdoms

Germanic culture, as it entered the former empire, **lacked cities and written laws**, and its **warriors pledged loyalty in exchange for weapons and loot**. Between **400 and 700** these peoples divided Western Europe among themselves.

**The Franks** became the key kingdom. **Clovis**:

- **Conquered Gaul**, later France
- Ruled by **Frankish customs** while **preserving the Roman heritage**
- **Converted to Christianity**, which gained him the support of the local population and an **alliance with the pope**

Clovis's conversion is the decisive political move of the era. It aligned the strongest Germanic kingdom with the institution that retained the most legitimacy among the conquered Roman population — and with the papacy, which needed a protector.

## The Muslim threat

Islam emerged as a new power, and the Church and the Christian kingdoms feared Muslim expansion out of Spain. **Charles Martel** rallied Frankish warriors and won a Christian victory at the **Battle of Tours (732)**. Muslim expansion in Western Europe was halted, though Muslim power in Spain remained a concern for centuries.

## Charlemagne

Charlemagne **united parts of Western Europe** — France, Germany, and Italy — reuniting much of the Western Roman Empire. He fought Muslims in Spain, confronted the Avars and Slavs in the east, and opposed the Lombards in Italy.

**Becoming Roman Emperor.** Pope **Leo III** sought his help in Rome; Charlemagne's delegates arrested the pope's opponents; and in **800** Charlemagne was **crowned Emperor of the Romans**. This **revived the idea of Christendom** — and **outraged the Eastern Roman Empire**, which considered itself the only legitimate Roman authority.

The coronation set a precedent with long consequences: an emperor crowned by a pope owes something to the papacy, and later medieval politics turns repeatedly on what exactly he owes.

**Unifying the Christian empire.** Charlemagne aimed to create a **united Christian Europe**. He sent **missionaries** to convert people, and dispatched the **missi dominici** — royal envoys — to **check the roads, listen to grievances, and ensure justice**, giving the crown eyes independent of the local nobility.

**Revival of learning.** He saw **education as a unifying tool**: he revived Latin learning, encouraged the creation of schools, brought scholars to the **Palace School**, and sought to revive Rome's glory at **Aachen**.

## Europe after Charlemagne

His son **Louis I** took the throne; Louis's sons fought for power, and in **843** his grandsons split the empire under the **Treaty of Verdun** — the division that lies behind the later separation of France and Germany.

Charlemagne's legacy was a blending of **Germanic, Roman, and Christian** cultures and an example of strong government. But the empire did not hold, and new invasions followed:

- **Magyars** settled in Hungary around **900**, raiding Eastern Europe and plundering Germany, France, and Italy before eventually being pushed back
- **Vikings** from Scandinavia were **expert sailors** who raided coasts and rivers from the **700s**. They were also **traders and explorers**, setting up colonies in **North America** and settling in England, Ireland, and elsewhere

The Viking case is a useful corrective: the same people appear in the sources as raiders, merchants, settlers, and explorers, and any account that keeps only the first is incomplete.

These renewed invasions, arriving on a fragmented and defenceless landscape, are the direct cause of the system described in 7.2.
