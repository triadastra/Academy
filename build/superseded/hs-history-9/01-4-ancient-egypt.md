# 1.4 Ancient Egypt: Kingdom on the Nile

## Geography's influence

The Greek historian **Herodotus** wrote that Egypt is the gift of the Nile — a judgement the geography supports completely.

The Nile provided a **narrow band of fertile land** known as the **"Black Land"**, surrounded by inhospitable **"Red Land"** desert. Farming villages thrived along the river, growing wheat and flax.

The Nile's **annual floods**, caused by rains in Ethiopia and central Africa, were crucial: they soaked the land and deposited rich silt. **Cooperation was necessary to manage the floods**, and early governments built dikes, reservoirs, and irrigation systems to control the water — the same dynamic that produced government in Sumer (1.2).

The decisive contrast with Mesopotamia is **predictability**. The Nile flooded on a regular annual schedule and the deserts on either side kept invaders out, whereas the Tigris and Euphrates flooded unpredictably in a region with no natural barriers. That difference shows up everywhere: in Egypt's political stability, in its long dynasties, and in its far more optimistic religion.

Egyptian merchants used the Nile for trade, exchanging goods across Africa, the Middle East, and the Mediterranean.

## Unification

Ancient Egypt was divided into **Upper Egypt** (south, upriver) and **Lower Egypt** (north, at the delta). Around **3100 BCE, King Menes** united the two, establishing **Memphis** as the first capital.

## The three kingdoms

| Period | Dates |
|---|---|
| **Old Kingdom** | 2575 – 2130 BCE |
| **Middle Kingdom** | 1938 – 1630 BCE |
| **New Kingdom** | 1539 – 1075 BCE |

### The Old Kingdom

Pharaohs established a **centralised government**. They were viewed as **gods** and held absolute power, supported by a **bureaucracy**:

- **Viziers** supervised government operations
- Departments managed tax collection, farming, and irrigation

Pharaohs built **necropolises and pyramids**, such as the **Great Pyramids at Giza**, as tombs for the afterlife — eternal homes for the deceased. Construction required thousands of workers and took decades.

The pyramids are the clearest possible evidence of the eight features from 1.1 working together: only a large agricultural surplus, an organised bureaucracy, job specialisation, and a religion worth that expense could produce them.

### The Middle Kingdom

A period of power struggles, crop failures, and corruption. Despite these, strong rulers initiated large **drainage projects** to create arable land and expanded trade contacts.

Around **1700 BCE the Hyksos invaded**, introducing new **military technologies**. Egyptians adopted some Hyksos customs — an instance of a conquest producing cultural exchange rather than only destruction.

### The New Kingdom

A period of **expansion and conquest**. **Hatshepsut** was notable as Egypt's first female pharaoh; **Thutmose III** extended Egypt's borders. **Ramses II** campaigned widely and signed a peace treaty with the Hittites. The empire reached its height around **1450 BCE**, encompassing parts of Syria and reaching the Euphrates.

### Decline

After **1100 BCE** Egyptian power declined under invasions by the Assyrians and Persians. The last Egyptian dynasty ended in **332 BCE** with Greek control under Alexander, followed by **Roman conquest in 30 BCE**.

## Religion

**Amon-Re** was the greatest of the gods, associated with the sun and the ram. Each city typically worshipped its own god, but Amon-Re rose to national prominence: the sun god **Re** was worshipped in the Old Kingdom, and by the Middle Kingdom Re had been combined with Amon.

**Osiris and Isis** were also significant, representing human emotions and the afterlife. Osiris became **god of the dead** after being killed by his brother **Set**; **Isis** was revered for her nurturing qualities.

In **1380 BCE**, Pharaoh **Amenhotep IV** — later **Akhenaton** — attempted to shift worship from Amon-Re to **Aton**, a lesser god. His reforms faced fierce resistance from the priests and did not survive him. The episode shows the limits of even a god-king's power: the priesthood controlled too much wealth and land to be overruled.

### The afterlife and mummification

Egyptians believed in an afterlife in which souls had to prove their worthiness to Osiris. The **heart of the deceased was weighed against the feather of truth**.

**Mummification** was a complex process aimed at preserving the body for the afterlife. Initially reserved for the elite, it eventually became accessible more widely.

The discovery of **Tutankhamen's tomb in 1922 by Howard Carter** provided invaluable insight into Egyptian civilisation, since it was found largely intact.

## Society

Egyptian society was hierarchical: **pharaoh**, then government officials, priests, merchants, and **peasants**. Most Egyptians were farmers.

**Women in ancient Egypt enjoyed more rights and independence** than their counterparts in most other ancient societies. They could **own property, conduct business, and participate in religious life** — a genuine and unusual distinction.

## Learning and the arts

**Hieroglyphics** were a complex writing system, later simplified into **hieratic** for sacred texts and **demotic** for everyday use. The **Rosetta Stone** was crucial to deciphering hieroglyphs, since it carried the same text in hieroglyphic, demotic, and Greek — allowing scholars to read Egyptian records for the first time in over a millennium.

Egyptians made significant contributions to **medicine, astronomy, and mathematics**, developing a **calendar** and using **geometry for land surveying** — the latter a direct consequence of the flood, which erased field boundaries every year and required them to be re-measured.

Art included statues, wall paintings, and carvings, usually on religious themes or daily life; literature included hymns, prayers, and stories.
