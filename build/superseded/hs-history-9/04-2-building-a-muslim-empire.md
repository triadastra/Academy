# 4.2 Building a Muslim Empire

## The succession crisis

Muhammad's death caused enormous grief and raised the question of the community's future. **There was no designated successor.**

**Abu Bakr**, Muhammad's father-in-law, was selected as the first **caliph** — successor to Muhammad and leader of the community. After battles with wavering tribes, he reunited the Muslims on the basis of their allegiance to Islam, converting the remaining Arab tribes and thereby ending warfare between Arabs and uniting them under one leader.

## The Sunni–Shi'a split

The division began after Muhammad's death in **632** over the rightful successor, and it hardened through a sequence of violent events:

- **656** — the third caliph, **Uthman**, was murdered; **Ali** was named caliph
- **657** — war erupted between Uthman's supporters and Ali's
- **660** — the Umayyads declared their own caliph in Jerusalem
- **661** — **Ali was assassinated**, and his son later killed in battle

| | **Sunni** (about 85%) | **Shi'a** (about 15%) |
|---|---|---|
| **Who may lead** | Any pious male Muslim from Muhammad's tribe, chosen by consensus | Only descendants of **Ali** and Muhammad's daughter |
| **Reasoning** | Muhammad cannot be succeeded **as prophet**; there are no prophets after him, so any good Muslim may lead the community | Muhammad **selected Ali** as his spiritual successor |
| **Leaders** | The **caliph**, a political leader | The **Imams**, divinely inspired leaders empowered to interpret the Quran and Muhammad's actions |

The split is fundamentally about **whether religious authority is inherited or conferred**, and it produced centuries of political and theological rivalry that continues to shape the region.

**Sufism** emerged separately as a **mystical** tradition, seeking personal communion with God through meditation, fasting, music, poetry, and dance — represented by poets such as **Rumi**.

## The conquests

Under the first four caliphs, Muslim armies achieved extraordinary victories against the Byzantine and Persian empires:

- Defeated the **Byzantines at the Yarmuk River (636)**, capturing Syria and Egypt by **640**
- **Overthrew the Sassanian (Persian) Empire by 650**
- Moved **west into North Africa and Spain**, and **east into Central Asia**
- The **Battle of Tours (732)** marked the high point of Arab expansion in Europe

### Why the conquests succeeded

Three reasons:

1. **Weakness of the Byzantine and Persian empires**, exhausted by prolonged conflict with each other
2. **Local populations often welcomed the Arabs as liberators** from harsh rule
3. **Bold, efficient Arab fighting methods**

The first reason is the most important and the most easily missed. The two great powers of the region had just spent decades fighting each other to a standstill; Islam expanded into a vacuum.

## Governing the conquered

**Non-Muslims** in conquered territories were **allowed to retain their religion** but paid a tax — the **jizya** — and were **exempt from military service**. **Conversion to Islam was officially voluntary.**

Muslim leaders **prohibited looting and destruction** of conquered lands, ensuring continued prosperity for the empire.

Many converted anyway, drawn by the **egalitarian principles of Islam** and by Arab administrative efficiency. Islam has **no religious hierarchy** — no priestly class — and emphasises the **equality of all believers regardless of race, gender, or wealth**. Many also read Islam's rapid triumph as a sign of God's favour.

As the civilisation developed, **Jews and Christians played key roles** as officials, doctors, and translators — which is part of why the Golden Age of 4.3 was possible.

## The Umayyad Dynasty (661–750)

- Established **hereditary rule**, with the capital at **Damascus**
- Expanded Islamic rule to **Spain (Andalusia)** and parts of the Mediterranean
- Built the **Dome of the Rock** in Jerusalem, marking the site where Muslims believe Muhammad ascended to heaven
- Faced internal divisions, including the deepening Sunni–Shi'a split
- **Overthrown in 750** by the Abbasids, amid accusations of corruption and favouritism

## The Abbasid Dynasty (750–1258)

The **Abbasid revolution** succeeded because the Umayyad caliphs had grown rich and luxurious, provoking revolts. In **747** frontier warriors of the Abbasid party allied with the **Shi'a and non-Arab Muslim converts**; in **750** they won the **Battle of Zab** and quickly conquered the rest of the empire.

The Abbasids:

- Moved the capital to **Baghdad**, shifting the cultural and political centre eastward
- **Integrated non-Arab Muslims** into the administration and military — the constituency that had brought them to power
- Achieved the great cultural and scientific advances of the golden age, particularly under **Harun al-Rashid** and **al-Ma'mun**
- Presided over trade networks spanning Europe, Asia, and Africa

## Córdoba

An Umayyad survivor, **Abd al-Rahman I**, fled to the Iberian Peninsula and created the **Córdoba Caliphate**. **Andalusia** became a hub for trade, agriculture, and intellectual achievement, and a notable period of **cultural and religious coexistence**.

Its decline began with internal divisions and the Christian **Reconquista**, culminating in the fall of **Granada in 1492**.

## Fragmentation and decline

**The Abbasids never ruled Spain**, and from about **850** their control over the rest of the empire fragmented. As caliphal power faded, independent states emerged — the **Fatimids** in Egypt, and Shi'ite rulers elsewhere.

**The Seljuk Turks**, originally military mercenaries, **seized Baghdad in 1055**, taking political control as **sultans**. They revitalised Sunni Islam but provoked the Byzantine appeals that produced the **Crusades**.

**The Crusades (1096–1291)** temporarily established Christian rule in parts of the Middle East but had **minimal long-term impact** on the region itself.

**The Mongols ended Abbasid rule.** From **1216 Genghis Khan** led the Mongols across southwest Asia; in **1258 Hulagu**, his great-grandson, **burned and looted Baghdad**, killing the last Abbasid caliph. In the late 1300s **Timur (Tamerlane)** campaigned through the Middle East, conquering Muslim and non-Muslim lands alike.

Over time **Mongol elites converted to Islam**, allowing cultural recovery under the **Mamluks** in Egypt — a pattern seen repeatedly in this course, in which conquerors are absorbed by the civilisation they conquered.

## Legacy

Islam spread across **three continents**, shaping the political, cultural, and religious landscape of each. The empire's eventual fragmentation came from **internal divisions** (Sunni versus Shi'a) combined with **external pressure** (Crusades, Mongols) — but the religion and the civilisation long outlasted the political unity that first carried them.
