# 8.5 The Scientific Revolution

## Changing views of the universe

Until the mid-1500s Europeans believed in the **Earth-centred** universe of **Ptolemy and Aristotle**, a view supported by the Church.

**Copernicus.** In **1543 Nicolaus Copernicus** published *On the Revolutions of the Heavenly Spheres*, proposing a **heliocentric — sun-centred — model**. **Most experts rejected it**, because it challenged both classical and religious teaching.

**Tycho Brahe** set up an observatory and made observations of unprecedented accuracy, providing evidence bearing on Copernicus's theory.

**Johannes Kepler** used Brahe's data to calculate that planets move in **elliptical orbits** — supporting heliocentrism and correcting it, since Copernicus had assumed circles.

**Galileo Galilei** built a **telescope** and observed **Jupiter's moons**, which supported the heliocentric view by showing that not everything orbits the Earth. His discoveries were opposed by scholars and **condemned by the Church**; he was tried and placed under **house arrest**.

The sequence is the point. Copernicus proposed, Brahe measured, Kepler calculated, Galileo observed — no single figure did it alone, and the theory prevailed because **independent lines of evidence converged** on it. That is the method, not just the result.

## A new scientific method

In the early 1600s a new method emerged, **based on observation and experimentation**.

**Francis Bacon** stressed **experimentation and observation**, and wanted science to **improve people's lives** through practical technologies — reasoning **from evidence to general conclusions**.

**René Descartes** emphasised **human reasoning**, beginning from systematic doubt: his famous statement is *"I think, therefore I am."*

The two are usually paired as opposites — Bacon the empiricist, Descartes the rationalist — and the mature scientific method uses both: **collecting and measuring data, proposing a hypothesis, testing it, and repeating the process**.

What is genuinely new is not curiosity but the insistence that a claim must be **testable** and that authority is **not evidence**. Aristotle had said heavy objects fall faster; the new method's answer was to go and check.

## Breakthroughs in medicine and chemistry

**Medicine:**

- **Andreas Vesalius** published *On the Structure of the Human Body* in **1543** — the **first accurate study of human anatomy**, based on dissection rather than on ancient texts
- **Ambroise Paré** developed new **surgical techniques** and an **anti-infection ointment**
- **William Harvey** described the **circulation of the blood** in the early 1600s
- **Anton van Leeuwenhoek** perfected the **microscope** and was the first to see **cells and microorganisms**

Note that Vesalius published in the same year as Copernicus. Both replaced an inherited authority with direct observation, in domains as different as astronomy and anatomy.

**Chemistry:** alchemy was transformed. **Robert Boyle** distinguished between **elements and compounds** and explained the **behaviour of gases**.

## Newton links the sciences

**Isaac Newton** saw an apple fall and wondered whether the same force controlled the motion of the planets. He developed the **theory of gravity**, showing that a **single force** keeps the planets in orbit.

In **1687** he published his account of the law of gravity, arguing that **nature follows uniform laws**.

This is the culmination of the whole movement. Aristotle's physics had treated the heavens and the Earth as fundamentally different realms governed by different principles; Newton showed that **one law describes both**. Newton's laws held for over 200 years, and the **calculus** he developed in part is still used today.
