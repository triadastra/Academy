# 5.3 Korea and Japan

Both Korea and Japan developed within China's cultural orbit and both selectively adapted what they took — this lesson is largely about what each chose to borrow and what each kept.

## Korea

### Early settlement

The Korean peninsula was probably first settled by **Altaic-speaking fishing and hunting people from Manchuria** during the Neolithic. Farming began around **2000 BCE**, and organised communities formed.

**A disagreement over the first kingdom:** Chinese sources claim **Koguryo** (37 BCE – 668 CE) was the first organised kingdom; Korean scholars assert that **Gojoseon**, founded by **Dangun in 2333 BCE**, was the first, with an ethnically Korean population. This is a live historiographical dispute, and a useful reminder that "when did this country begin?" is often a political question as much as an archaeological one.

### Chinese influence and the Three Kingdoms

In **109 BCE** the northern part of the peninsula came under **direct Chinese rule** during the Han dynasty.

The **Three Kingdoms** were **Koguryo, Paekche, and Silla**.

- **Koguryo** was the first to introduce **Buddhism** in the late fourth century CE and established **Confucian academies**
- **Silla**, initially less influenced by China, later became dominant. It **accepted tributary status under the Tang** to appease the Chinese and unify the country, and its rulers adopted Chinese political institutions to create a **centralised state**
- **Buddhism** grew in popularity, with many Koreans travelling to Buddhist sites in China

Silla's tributary status is a good illustration of how the Chinese tribute system actually worked: accepting a subordinate title bought Silla the political backing it needed to unify the peninsula. Formal submission was often a tool rather than a defeat.

### The Koryo dynasty

Silla collapsed in the early tenth century, and the **Koryo** dynasty emerged in the north, aiming to strengthen itself by introducing **Chinese political institutions**. It lasted about **400 years** and was a period of high achievement.

**Buddhist monasteries** were significant: they controlled large territories, provided **royal advisers**, and produced art — including the printing of the **Tripitaka** on wooden blocks.

The Koryo kingdom struggled throughout to overcome the power of the **nobility**.

### Mongol rule and Choson

In the **13th century** the Mongols seized northern Korea and made it a **tributary of the Yuan**. Korean peasants and artisans were compelled to provide **conscript labour** — including shipbuilding for **Kublai Khan's invasion of Japan**.

The military commander **Yi Song-gye** seized power and established the **Choson (Joseon) dynasty in 1392**. Choson actively adopted new technologies from China, such as **woodblock printing**.

## Japan

### Early Japan

Early inhabitants lived by **hunting, fishing, and food-gathering**. By around **800 BCE rice cultivation** had been introduced, possibly by Korean immigrants.

The **Yayoi** period (300 BCE – 250 CE) saw a mixture of the earlier **Jomon** people and the new arrivals from Korea. Settlement began on **Kyushu**, then spread to the **Yamato plain** near modern Osaka and Kyoto. Political organisation was **highly decentralised**. Japan was first mentioned in Chinese records in **57 CE**.

### Chinese influence and early culture

Japan absorbed the **three core philosophies** from China — Confucianism, Daoism, and Buddhism — alongside its own **Shintoism**, which concerns landscapes, history, and ancestral ties.

Writing came from China as **kanji**, supplemented by two Japanese syllabaries, **katakana** and **hiragana**. **Poetry, painting, and architecture were all heavily influenced by the Tang**.

Everyday culture included the **kimono**, sitting close to the floor, **paper-making**, wrestling and martial arts, music, and games such as **go**.

Japan's borrowing is characteristically **selective**: it took Chinese writing, Buddhism, and artistic models, but never adopted the civil service examination, which would have threatened the hereditary aristocracy. Compare Korea, which took the examination system wholesale.

### The Heian period

The **Heian** period is the **"golden age" of Japanese culture**. A notable feature of court life was linguistic segregation by gender: **men wrote in Chinese, women wrote in Japanese**.

The consequence is one of the great ironies in literary history. Because Japanese was regarded as the lesser vehicle, women writing in it produced the period's enduring literature — including the world's first novel — while the men's Chinese compositions are now read mainly by specialists.

### The samurai

**Samurai** were soldiers with a master; **ronin** were soldiers without one, and ranked lower.

They were **not always "good and faithful"** — a samurai could turn on his clan for a better offer, since service was often **stipendiary**. **Not all were men.**

**Bushido**, the warrior code, prescribed **justice, courage, mercy, politeness, honesty, honour, loyalty, and self-control**. But there is **no record of the code before the mid-19th century**, which means it is largely a **later idealisation** projected backwards onto the medieval warrior — an important corrective to the popular image.

### Buddhism and law

**Zen Buddhism** arrived in **1191**, and the **Lotus Sutra sect** — radical and intolerant — in **1253**.

The **Joei Shikimoku legal code of 1232** reflected **Confucian values**. Its significance is procedural: previously, disputes had been settled through **social connections**, and a written code substituted rules for influence.

The shogunate eventually **lost power to landlords**, and faced the **Mongol invasions of 1274 and 1281** — both of which failed.

### The Muromachi period: Japan's Renaissance

**Karamono** were artistic and cultural products of China, traditionally highly prized in Japan. Members of the **Ashikaga** clan, who ruled as shogun, amassed a huge collection at their mansion.

Japanese paintings incorporated **Zen** aspects — gratitude for beauty and nature — and Zen Buddhist monks travelled from Japan to **Ming China** to study authentic Chinese painting.

**Josetsu** is regarded as the father of Japanese ink painting. His *Catching a Fish With a Gourd* is a piece of Zen humour — a **koan**, or riddle, in visual form, designed to provoke the viewer into new ways of seeing.

The **Ryoanji temple** in north Kyoto, affiliated with a branch of Zen Buddhism, was a great centre for elite cultural activity under the patronage of the **Hosokawa** family. **Zen Buddhism pursued enlightenment or awakening through personal experience and meditation** — which is why its art aims at provocation rather than instruction.

The **haiku**, a short poetic form of 5-7-5 syllables, belongs to the same aesthetic: maximum compression, meaning left for the reader to complete.
