# 7.2 Feudalism and the Manor Economy

Two systems, easily confused, answering two different questions. **Feudalism is a political system** — who owes military service to whom. **The manor is an economic system** — how food is produced and who gets it.

## Feudalism: a political system

**Origins.** The invasions of 7.1 weakened central rule; with no king able to defend a whole kingdom, protection had to be organised locally.

**Structure.** Lords divided land — **fiefs** — among **vassals**, who **pledged loyalty and service** in return. The arrangement **varied greatly across regions and over time**; there was never a single uniform "feudal system".

**The exchange:**

| The lord provides | The vassal provides |
|---|---|
| A **fief**, varying in size and including peasants, towns, and buildings | **40 days of military service** annually |
| **Protection** | **Money payments** |
| | **Advice** |

Relationships became complex because a man could hold fiefs from **several lords**; a vassal therefore designated a **liege lord** to whom his primary loyalty was owed.

The complication is the system's central weakness. When two of a man's lords went to war with each other, feudal obligation gave no clean answer — which is why medieval politics generated so much litigation and so many broken oaths.

## Knights and nobles

**Training.** Boys were trained as knights from **age 7**, maintained their own armour and weapons, and underwent strict discipline.

**Warfare.** Knights fought **on horseback with swords, axes, and lances**, wearing armour and carrying shields, and practised in **tournaments**.

**Castles.** By the **1100s** monarchs and nobles had large **stone castles**. Knights lived in them; they provided **refuge for peasants in war**; and they were key to **controlling strategic locations**. Castles were captured by **siege or assault**.

**Noblewomen.** They **managed estates when the men were away**, supervised vassals, ran households, and performed agricultural and medical tasks. Some were significant political figures — **Eleanor of Aquitaine** most famously.

**Inheritance.** Land usually passed to the **eldest son**. Women received land as a **dowry**, and **widows retained land**. Girls learned household skills, some learned to read and write, and all were expected to bear children and be dutiful.

**Chivalry** was the code for knights, requiring **bravery, loyalty, fairness, and the protection of the weak**, and celebrated by **troubadours** who sang about knights. Like bushido in 5.3, chivalry describes an ideal rather than a reliable description of behaviour.

## The manor: an economic system

The manor was the lord's estate, where **peasants — mostly serfs — lived and worked**.

**What peasants owed:**

- Worked on the **lord's land several days a week**
- **Repaired infrastructure**
- Asked the **lord's permission to marry**
- **Paid fees** for inheritance and for using the mill
- **Paid in products**, since money was scarce

**What the lord owed:** **protection**.

Serfs were not slaves — they could not be bought and sold individually, and they had customary rights to their strips of land — but they were **bound to the land** and could not leave it.

**A self-sufficient world.** Peasants produced most necessities and **rarely travelled far from the village**. They **lacked schooling and knowledge of the outside world**. Living conditions were harsh: **long working hours**, a **simple diet**, and **punishment for poaching** on the lord's land.

There were breaks — **celebrations of marriages and births**, and holidays at **Christmas and Easter**.

## How the two systems connect

The link is straightforward and worth stating explicitly: the **manor produced the surplus** that allowed a knight to spend his life training and fighting rather than farming. A fief without peasants is worthless, because the fief's purpose is to fund a warrior. Feudalism is the military structure that the manorial economy pays for.
