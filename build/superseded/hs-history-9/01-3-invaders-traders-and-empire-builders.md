# 1.3 Invaders, Traders, and Empire Builders

Because Mesopotamia had **no natural barriers**, wave after wave of nomadic peoples and warriors invaded the Fertile Crescent. Each conquest left something behind, and the region's history is a sequence of empires each inheriting the last one's achievements.

## Key terms

| Term | Meaning |
|---|---|
| **Barter economy** | Goods and services traded directly |
| **Money economy** | Goods and services paid for with currency |
| **Civil law** | Governs private rights and matters |
| **Criminal law** | Deals with offences against others |
| **Alphabet** | A writing system in which symbols represent sounds |

## Sargon and the first empire

**Sargon of Akkad**, around **2300 BCE**, created the **first known empire** by conquering the Sumerian city-states. An empire, unlike a city-state, unites many peoples under one ruler — a form of organisation that dominates the next four thousand years.

## Hammurabi and Babylon

**Hammurabi**, king of Babylon around **1790 BCE**, revived the Sumerian city-states and imposed unity over the region.

**Hammurabi's Code**, carved on a stone pillar, is his most famous achievement — the first comprehensive written law code we possess.

- **Civil law** addressed private rights: business contracts, property inheritance, and marriage. It aimed to **protect the powerless**, including women and slaves.
- **Criminal law** detailed specific punishments to promote social order, often following the principle of **"an eye for an eye"**.

He also improved irrigation systems, organised a strong army, and promoted religious unity by elevating **Marduk**, Babylon's chief god.

The significance of a written code is that it is **public and fixed**. Before Hammurabi, justice depended on whichever official was deciding; afterwards, the standard was carved in stone where anyone could see it — the ancestor of the idea that rulers themselves are bound by law.

## The Hittites

Around **1400 BCE** the Hittites, in Asia Minor (Anatolia), introduced **ironworking**. Iron is harder and more common than bronze, so this transformed both warfare and agriculture. The Hittite kingdom ended around **1190 BCE**, after which iron technology spread widely.

## The Assyrians

The **Assyrians**, known for a fierce warrior culture, expanded their empire by **1100 BCE**. They emerged in upper Tigris, spoke a Semitic language, and built their power on **terror and warfare**, excelling militarily.

They established a **well-ordered society**, built impressive palaces, and created one of the **first libraries** at Nineveh, under **Ashurbanipal**. By his reign the empire was overextended; Babylon rebelled and Assyria disintegrated.

Note that Assyrian society defined identity by **language** rather than ethnicity, and used **religion as a cohesive force** — mechanisms for holding together an empire of many peoples.

## Nebuchadnezzar and the revival of Babylon

After the fall of Assyria, **Nebuchadnezzar** rebuilt Babylon, enhancing its defences and infrastructure. He constructed the famous **Ishtar Gate** and possibly the **Hanging Gardens**.

## The Persian Empire

In **539 BCE** Babylon fell to the Persians under **Cyrus the Great**, who established the largest empire the world had yet seen. Its defining characteristic was **tolerance**: Cyrus extended control over the Medes (the first **satrapy**, or province), made Babylonia a province under a **satrap** (governor), and generally allowed existing officials to keep their positions and peoples to keep their customs.

That tolerance was a deliberate governing strategy, not merely a virtue. An empire that lets conquered peoples keep their religion and their local administrators needs far fewer troops to hold it down.

**Cambyses**, Cyrus's son, invaded Egypt. **Darius I** built the empire to its greatest extent and unified it by:

- Dividing it into **satrapies** governed by satraps
- Implementing a **single code of laws**
- Improving communication through **roads**
- Introducing **standardised weights and measures**
- Encouraging the use of **coins**, moving from a **barter economy to a money economy**, which simplified trade

## Zoroastrianism

**Zoroaster**, around 660 BCE, introduced a **monotheistic** religion centred on **Ahura Mazda**. Its worldview is **dualistic**, emphasising the struggle between good (Ahura Mazda) and evil (Ahriman) — ideas that influenced later religions, including Judaism, Christianity, and Islam.

## The Phoenicians

While empires rose and fell, the **Phoenicians** built their power on **maritime trade** rather than conquest, establishing colonies and spreading their culture throughout the Mediterranean. They were skilled manufacturers, producing glass and the famous **Tyrian purple** dye.

Their most consequential invention was an early **alphabet** of **22 consonant sounds**. An alphabet requires a few dozen symbols where cuneiform required hundreds, which put literacy within reach of ordinary merchants rather than a trained scribal class. The Greeks adopted it (see 2.1), the Romans adapted it from the Greeks, and it is the ancestor of the letters on this page. The Phoenicians were eventually defeated by the Assyrians and then the Persians — but the alphabet outlasted every empire in this lesson.
