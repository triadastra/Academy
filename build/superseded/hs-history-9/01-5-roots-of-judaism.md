# 1.5 The Roots of Judaism

Judaism originated around **4,000 years ago** in the region that is now Israel, at the western end of the Fertile Crescent. Its central innovation was **monotheism** — the worship of one God — in a world where every neighbouring society was polytheistic.

## Key terms

| Term | Meaning |
|---|---|
| **Monotheistic** | Believing in one God |
| **Torah** | The most sacred text of Judaism — the first five books of the Hebrew Bible |
| **Abraham** | Considered the father of the Israelite people |
| **Covenant** | A binding agreement between God and the Israelites |
| **Moses** | The figure who renewed God's covenant with the Israelites |
| **Patriarchal** | A society in which men hold most power and authority |
| **Sabbath** | A holy day of rest and worship |
| **Prophet** | A spiritual leader who interprets God's will |
| **Ethics** | Moral principles guiding behaviour |
| **Diaspora** | The dispersal of Jews beyond their homeland |

## Origins

The ancient Israelites believed in an **all-knowing, all-powerful, omnipresent God**. Their history and their faith were intertwined: events were understood as reflections of God's will.

They originated from **Mesopotamia**, a region dominated by polytheism. Nomadic tribes wandered into Palestine from the east around **1900 BCE**.

**Abraham**, living around **2000 BCE**, is recognised as the father of the Israelites. God — **Yahweh** — made a **covenant** with him, promising that his descendants would become a great nation.

**Moses** renewed the covenant after the Israelites were enslaved in Egypt, leading them in the **Exodus**, a journey of forty years, ultimately toward the promised land of **Canaan**.

## The kingdom of Israel

By **1000 BCE** the Israelites established the kingdom of Israel, uniting the **twelve tribes** under **King David**. His son **Solomon** expanded the kingdom and strengthened royal power, building the Temple in **Jerusalem**.

After Solomon's death around **922 BCE**, the kingdom split into **Israel** in the north and **Judah** in the south. Israel was conquered by the **Assyrians in 722 BCE**; Judah was forced to pay tribute to Assyria, regained independence after Assyria's decline, and was then conquered by the **Chaldeans**, who **destroyed Jerusalem in 586 BCE** and carried the upper class to Babylon in captivity.

When the **Persians** destroyed the Chaldeans, the Jews were allowed to return — an instance of the Persian tolerance described in 1.3. The region remained under Persian control until Alexander the Great.

## Law, prophets, and ethics

The **Torah** contains many laws — the Books of the Law — governing moral conduct and social justice. The core commitment is **obedience to God**.

**Prophets** such as **Isaiah** and **Jeremiah** interpreted God's will and urged devotion to the law, emphasising **social justice** and the ethical treatment of others.

This ethical emphasis is what distinguishes Judaism most sharply from the surrounding religions. Mesopotamian and Egyptian gods demanded correct ritual; the Israelite God demanded **righteous conduct**, and the prophets insisted that ritual without justice was worthless.

## The Diaspora

During the Babylonian Captivity many Jews dispersed across the world — the **Diaspora**. Despite being scattered, they maintained their identity through the Torah, the Sabbath, and shared law. Jews experienced **persecution over many centuries**; in the 1800s many came to support **Zionism**, a movement for a Jewish country.

The **Dead Sea Scrolls**, discovered in **1947** and written around 2,000 years ago, provide direct insight into early Jewish beliefs and practices.

## Influence

Judaism significantly influenced **Christianity and Islam**, which share its monotheism, many of its scriptures, and descent from Abraham. Ancient Israel is the birthplace of two monotheistic religions — Judaism and Christianity — and the third, Islam, traces itself to the same tradition. The idea of one God who makes ethical demands on human beings begins here and reaches roughly half the world's population today.
