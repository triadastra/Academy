# 1.2 Mesopotamia and the City-States of Sumer

## Geography

The **Fertile Crescent** is the arc of rich land extending from the **Persian Gulf to the Mediterranean Sea**, encompassing **Mesopotamia** — the region **between the Tigris and Euphrates rivers**. **Sumer**, the world's first civilisation, emerged in southeastern Mesopotamia around **3300 BCE**.

The rivers gave and took away:

| Advantage | Disadvantage |
|---|---|
| Plenty of fresh water for irrigation | **Unpredictable flooding** |
| Silt deposited by floods enriched the soil | **No natural barriers to invasion** |
| Access to trade, travel, and cultural diffusion | Limited natural resources — little stone, wood, or metal |

Sumerians grew mainly **barley and wheat**, traded grain, cloth, and tools, and imported stone, wood, and metal. Lacking stone, they used **clay**, making sun-dried bricks to build cities such as **Ur** and **Uruk** — and mud-brick walls to defend them.

The **Epic of Gilgamesh**, first told orally in Sumer, describes a great flood that destroyed the world — literature that records the environmental anxiety of a society living beside unpredictable rivers.

## Survival and cooperation

Managing the floods required **collaboration** on a scale no single family could manage. Leadership came from **temple priests or royal officials** who organised the construction of dikes and irrigation systems.

This is the origin of organised government in Mesopotamia: the rivers created a problem that could only be solved collectively, and solving it required someone with authority to direct the work.

## The city-states

By **2500 BCE** Sumer consisted of at least **12 city-states**, frequently in conflict over resources. Governance evolved from **military leaders** to **hereditary rulers** responsible for defence, public works, and religious ceremonies.

A ruler was expected to:

- **Protect** the city
- **Build** walls and irrigation works
- **Hire scribes** for taxes and records
- **Perform religious rituals**

## Social hierarchy

| Level | Members |
|---|---|
| **Top** | Ruling family, leading officials, high priests |
| **Middle** | Lesser priests, scribes, merchants, artisans |
| **Bottom** | Peasant farmers |
| **Below** | Slaves — often war captives or debtors |

Women had fewer rights than men.

## Religion

Sumerians were **polytheistic**, worshipping many gods who influenced every aspect of life. They built **ziggurats** — stepped temples — for religious ceremonies, and believed in a **grim underworld** afterlife. This pessimistic view of death contrasts sharply with Egypt's, which is one of the clearest cultural differences between the two river civilisations (see 1.3).

## Writing

**Cuneiform**, developed by **3200 BCE**, is the **earliest known writing system**. It evolved from **pictographs** — picture-symbols — into wedge-shaped marks pressed into clay, and eventually into signs representing sounds rather than things.

Writing allowed the recording of **economic transactions, myths, and laws**. **Scribes** underwent rigorous training and became essential to administration.

Note the order of events: writing was invented for **accounting**, not for literature. The need to record who owed how much grain came first; epic poetry came later.

## Legacy

Sumerian innovations in writing, mathematics, and astronomy shaped every later civilisation in the region. Their mathematics was based on **60**, which is why we still divide:

- **time into 60 minutes**
- **circles into 360 degrees**

Other contributions include the **plough**, the **wheel** (which the Sumerians possibly invented), sailing, bronze-working, and the ziggurat.
