# 9.2 Africa and Southeast Asia in Transition

## The Portuguese in Africa

The Portuguese initially sought a **sea route to the Spice Islands** but also engaged in trade along the African coast, focusing on **gold**.

They **seized East African port cities** — **Kilwa, Sofala, Mombasa** — and built forts to control the trade, especially in gold. These were the prosperous Swahili city-states of 6.1; Portuguese control disrupted the Indian Ocean networks that had sustained them for centuries.

## The Atlantic slave trade

European exploration of the African coast led to a **significant increase in the slave trade from the sixteenth to the eighteenth centuries**. Enslaved Africans were **forcibly removed from their homes and exported to plantations in the Americas**, usually cash-crop plantations growing **cotton, tobacco, and sugarcane**.

**Slavery existed in Africa before European involvement**, but the transatlantic trade **expanded its scale and changed its character** — turning it into a mass, racialised, permanent, and hereditary system geared to plantation production.

The **Dutch, English, and French** joined the West African trade, competing with the Portuguese.

**Impact on Africa.** The trade **devastated African societies**, displacing millions and disrupting economies. **European merchants, local elites, and African intermediaries** all participated, often **destabilising kingdoms** such as **Kongo** — states could be drawn into raiding their neighbours to obtain the captives European traders would buy, which in turn required more weapons, bought with more captives.

**The Middle Passage** — the voyage from Africa to the Americas — was characterised by **high mortality**, and conditions in the Americas were brutal.

## European settlement in Africa

The **Dutch** were the first Europeans to settle in **South Africa**, establishing a colony at the **Cape of Good Hope** as a supply station on the route to Asia. The colony evolved, with Dutch farmers — **Boers** — and other European settlers, and came into conflict with indigenous groups, including **Bantu-speaking peoples**, over land.

The distinction matters: most European activity in Africa before 1800 was **coastal trade**, not settlement. The Cape is the exception, and it became a settler colony precisely because it was a way-station rather than a trading destination.

## Southeast Asia in the era of the spice trade

**European competition.** The Portuguese, Dutch, English, and French competed for control of the spice trade. The **Dutch East India Company (VOC)** emerged dominant, pushing out rivals and controlling the routes.

The VOC is a significant institutional innovation: a **joint-stock company** granted state powers to make war, sign treaties, and govern territory. It is a commercial organisation acting as a state, and the model for much of what follows in European imperialism.

**Society and culture before colonisation.** Southeast Asian societies such as **Siam** and **Malacca** balanced local traditions with newer influences — **Islam and Buddhism** — which shaped political structures, while trade in spices and textiles drove economic growth.

**European presence remained limited initially** but grew, increasingly affecting local politics and culture.

## Turbulent centuries

The period from 1500 to 1800 in Africa is often summarised as one of disruption, but the more accurate description is **uneven**: some regions were devastated by the slave trade, some states were strengthened by their role as intermediaries in it, and much of the interior was little affected until much later. Generalising across a continent is exactly the error to avoid here.
