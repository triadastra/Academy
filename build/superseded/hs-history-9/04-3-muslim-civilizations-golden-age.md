# 4.3 Muslim Civilization's Golden Age

Between roughly **750 and 1350** the Muslim world was the most intellectually productive region on earth. This lesson covers why, and what it produced.

## Trade

Merchants built a **vast trading network** across Muslim lands and beyond. **Camel caravans** — called the **"ships of the desert"** — crossed the Sahara, and Muslim traders reached East Asia and Europe along the **Silk Road**, spreading products, technologies, knowledge, and culture.

**Baghdad** became the centre of world trade and education, alongside **Cairo** and **Córdoba**.

**The merchant was honoured in Muslim culture** — Muhammad himself had been a merchant. That cultural respect for commerce is a real difference from societies where trade was looked down on, and it helps explain the scale of the network.

## Business innovations

Extensive trade and a money economy led Muslims to **pioneer new business practices**:

- **Partnerships**
- Buying and selling **on credit**
- **Banks** in all major cities, so that **a check written in Baghdad might be cashed in Cairo**
- **Joint ventures with Christians and Jews**
- Guidelines for artisans and their guilds

The check is the emblematic invention: it makes long-distance trade possible without transporting gold across bandit country. (The English word "check" derives, through several languages, from the Arabic *sakk*.)

## Manufacturing

Muslim artisans produced a wealth of fine goods — **glassware, jewellery, tapestries, carpets, swords, and water clocks**.

## Society

Islamic society was **relatively egalitarian for its time**, with genuine **social mobility** — the ability to move up in social class. It nevertheless practised **slavery** and maintained **gender hierarchies**. Women had rights such as **property ownership** but were often subject to restrictive customs including **veiling and seclusion**.

## Literature and language

- **Persian** was the language of high culture
- **Arabic** was the language of religion, law, and natural science, and the foundation of a great literary tradition

**The Quran is a very important source for Muslim literature**, especially poetry. Common themes were **desert journeys, the joys of battle, chivalry, and romance**.

Major works include **Ferdowsi's *Book of Kings***, **Omar Khayyam's *Rubaiyat***, and **The Thousand and One Nights**.

## Art and architecture

**Artists were forbidden to use human or animal figures in religious art**, on the grounds that depicting living beings risks idolatry and encroaches on God's role as creator.

The restriction is worth understanding as a **generative** constraint rather than merely a prohibition. Denied representation, Muslim artists developed **abstract geometric patterns (arabesque)** and **calligraphy** to an extraordinary level of sophistication — turning the written word itself into the highest visual art.

Religious buildings were decorated with arabesque and calligraphy. **Domes, mosques, and high minarets** dominated the cities; the dome and arch forms were adapted from **Byzantine** models.

## Science and scholarship

**Muslim scholars preserved and expanded upon the learning of earlier civilisations.** They collected the classical works of the Greeks, Romans, Persians, and Indians into **libraries**, **translated them into Arabic**, and built upon them.

This is why the twelfth century saw schools, libraries, and institutes flourishing in Cairo, Baghdad, and Córdoba, drawing on Greek, Roman, Persian, and Indian knowledge — and why many Greek texts survive at all. Works lost in Europe were preserved in Arabic and later translated back into Latin, which is one of the preconditions of the European Renaissance (7.1).

**Mathematics.** **Al-Khwarizmi** pioneered the study of **algebra** — the word derives from the title of his book, and "algorithm" from his name. Muslim scholars also spread **Arabic numerals**, which had been invented in India (2.1), including the concept of zero.

**Medicine and public health.** Remarkable advances were made:

- **Muhammad al-Razi**, head physician at Baghdad's chief hospital, wrote extensively on medicine
- **Ibn Sina** (Avicenna), a Persian physician, wrote the *Canon of Medicine*, an encyclopedia of diseases and treatments that remained a standard text in Europe for centuries

**History.** Historians such as **al-Mas'udi** and **Ibn Khaldun** advanced historical method, emphasising **critical analysis of sources** and the understanding of historical dynamics — Ibn Khaldun in particular is often described as a founder of sociology.

## Integration and legacy

The essential point about this civilisation is that it was **integrative**. It absorbed Greek, Persian, and Indian traditions rather than displacing them, and combined them with Arabic and Islamic contributions to produce something none of the sources had alone. The transmission of Indian numerals through Arabic into Europe is a single small example of a very large pattern.
