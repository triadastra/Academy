# 8.1 The Renaissance in Italy

## What the Renaissance was

People in Italy between **1350 and 1550** saw themselves as reviving Classical antiquity, and called the period the **Renaissance** — French for "rebirth".

## Humanism

**Humanism** was the intellectual movement at its centre: the study of the **liberal arts** based on Greek and Roman authors, emphasising **rhetoric, poetry, moral philosophy, grammar, and history**. It focused on **human abilities, learning, and the reading of old texts**.

**Petrarch** is called the "father of the Italian Renaissance". Humanism promoted **Latin** and generated interest in **Classical Greece**.

In **Florence**, humanists emphasised **leading an active life**: city council members believed intellectual life was properly the business of **citizens**, not only of scholars withdrawn from the world. This civic humanism is what distinguishes the Italian movement from purely academic scholarship.

**Women's participation varied.** Some upper-class women received an education and contributed to intellectual life.

## Why Italy?

Several conditions coincided:

- **Italian city-states** grew rich on **trade**, providing wealth to spend on art and learning
- Italy contained the **physical remains of Rome**, making the classical past visible daily
- Competing city-states and wealthy families used **patronage** to display status

## Important people

**The Medici family** were powerful bankers in **Florence** who paid for the work of artists including Leonardo and Michelangelo. **Cosimo de' Medici (1389–1464)** took control of Florence's merchant oligarchy in **1434**, and through **patronage and political alliances** his family dominated the city and stood at the centre of the cultural Renaissance.

**Leonardo da Vinci** — painter (*Mona Lisa*), inventor, and scientist; the model "Renaissance Man". *The Last Supper* represents the High Renaissance concern for **idealising nature**, using **gesture and movement to depict character and inner nature**; his experimental technique caused the fresco to deteriorate.

**Michelangelo** — sculptor (*David*) and painter (the **Sistine Chapel** ceiling). He **dissected bodies** to understand human anatomy, aiming at perfection of design, and his ceiling figures are famously muscular. Influenced by **Neoplatonism**, he believed beautiful forms were reflections of divine beauty.

**Raphael** — *The School of Athens*, painted in 1510–11 in the Vatican, depicts an imaginary gathering of ancient philosophers with **Plato and Aristotle at the centre**.

**Masaccio** — his *Tribute Money* in the Brancacci Chapel is considered the **first great work of Early Renaissance art**, introducing a realistic style with **rational perspective**.

**Machiavelli (1469–1527)** — wrote *The Prince* (1513), arguing that rulers should be strong rather than always moral, and doing whatever works to stay in power. His concerns were the **acquisition, maintenance, and expansion of political power**.

*The Prince* is best understood as the political counterpart of Renaissance realism in art: it describes rulers **as they behave** rather than as moral treatises said they ought to behave. That descriptive turn is what made it shocking.

**Castiglione** — *The Book of the Courtier* taught how to be the ideal Renaissance noble: **smart, athletic, and artistic**.

## Art and innovation

| Technique | Effect |
|---|---|
| **Perspective** | Made paintings look three-dimensional |
| **Realism** | Study of **human anatomy** made figures lifelike |
| **Oil paints** | Brighter colours and subtler light effects |

**Renaissance architects** were inspired by **Roman models**, designing interiors with **classical columns, rounded arches, and coffered ceilings** balanced in proportion.

## Europe's political landscape in the fifteenth century

**State-building.** In the second half of the century, monarchies in **France, Spain, and England** were building modern states, reducing the power of the Church and the nobles and enhancing their own.

**Italy** was different — five major powers, none able to dominate:

| State | Form |
|---|---|
| **Milan** | A duchy |
| **Florence** | A republic |
| **Venice** | A republic, a stable maritime state governed by a **merchant oligarchy** |
| **The Papal States** | Ruled by the pope |
| **The Kingdom of Naples** | A centralised territorial state |

Milan, Florence, and Venice were skilled at building alliances and exercised real political influence.

Political boundaries help explain the frequent **wars between France and the Holy Roman Empire** — bordering territories with competing interests.

**Despite their wealth, the Italian states were not strong.** From **1494** Italy was a battlefield in the struggle between the French and Spanish monarchies, leading to **Spanish domination in the sixteenth century**. Wealth without military scale is not power — which is precisely the situation Machiavelli was writing about.

**France.** The Hundred Years' War left France prosperous and with a sense of **national feeling**. **Louis XI (1461–83)** strengthened the monarchy by subduing the great lords and creating a strong financial base through taxation.

**England.** The Hundred Years' War caused financial strain and losses, followed by the civil **Wars of the Roses**. **Henry Tudor** established a new dynasty in **1485**; **Henry VII (1485–1509)** built a strong monarchical government and managed finances well.

**Spain.** The marriage of **Isabella of Castile** and **Ferdinand of Aragon** in 1469 united the two crowns, and by the sixteenth century the new Spanish army was among the best in Europe.

**Central and Eastern Europe.** The **Holy Roman Empire** failed to develop strong monarchical authority; Germany became a land of hundreds of virtually independent states. The **Habsburg** dynasty gradually acquired possessions along the Danube and became a major power, with Austria central to European affairs. The emperor was **effectively hereditary but still had to be elected**. In Eastern Europe rulers struggled to centralise, often facing divisions among Catholics, Eastern Orthodox, and other groups.

Around 1500, then: the **Reconquista** was complete under Aragon and Castile, **France** was unified and stable under the House of Valois, **England** was stabilised under the Tudors, the **Holy Roman Empire** was decentralised under Maximilian I, and the **Ottoman Empire** was rising in the east. This is the Europe into which the Reformation (8.3) and the voyages of exploration (9.1) arrive.

## Other renaissances

The Italian Renaissance was not unique, and the comparison is worth making:

**The Timurid Renaissance.** The Timurid Empire, a Turko-Mongol state founded by **Timur (Tamerlane)** in **1370**, saw a revival of arts and sciences that spread across the Muslim world and rivalled European achievements.

**Japan's Muromachi period** produced its own cultural flowering (5.3).

**The Benin Renaissance.** The **Benin Bronzes** are a group of more than a thousand metal plaques and sculptures that decorated the royal palaces of the Kingdom of Benin, in modern Nigeria — warrior figures, masks, and animal forms made to celebrate Benin's kings and military victories, at a technical standard equal to anything cast in Europe at the time.
