# 2.1 Early Civilizations of India

## Geography

The **Indian subcontinent** is a large landmass jutting out from Asia into the Indian Ocean, comprising India, Pakistan, Bangladesh, Sri Lanka, Nepal, and Bhutan. Its northern border is the **Himalayas**.

Three major zones:

- The fertile **Gangetic Plain** in the north, watered by the Indus, Ganges, and Brahmaputra
- The dry **Deccan plateau** — partly arid, agriculturally unproductive, sparsely populated
- The **coastal plains** on either side of the Deccan

**Monsoons** are seasonal winds that blow from a consistent direction for part of the year and dominate agriculture. Winter monsoons bring hot, dry air from the north; summer monsoons bring rain. Too little rain means famine; too much means flood. The whole agricultural year depends on their arrival, which is why they recur throughout Indian history — and, later, why they governed Indian Ocean trade (see 4.2 and 5.2).

## The Indus Valley civilisation

Archaeologists in the early 1900s discovered a civilisation in the Indus River valley that had flourished 4,500 years earlier and had been entirely unknown. It emerged around **2600 BCE** and lasted about 700 years.

Two major cities: **Mohenjo-Daro** and **Harappa**.

What the ruins show:

- **Carefully planned, organised layout** — long wide main streets and large rectangular blocks
- Building materials of **mud and unbaked brick**
- **Complex plumbing** — baths, drains, and water chutes leading into sewers
- A **uniform system of weights and measures**

The grid plan and standardised weights are the striking findings. They imply a **central authority** capable of imposing a single design across cities hundreds of miles apart — yet no palaces, no royal tombs, and no decipherable script have been found to tell us who that authority was. The Indus script remains unread, which is why we know less about this civilisation than about Sumer or Egypt despite having more of its buildings.

## The Vedic Age

Around **1500–500 BCE** the **Aryan** civilisation dominated — the "Vedic Age", named for the **Vedas**, its sacred texts. The period produced **Sanskrit** as a written language and an **acculturation**, or blending, of Aryan and Dravidian traditions.

By around **500 BCE** classical India consisted of rival kingdoms.

The **Mahabharata** is one of the two great Sanskrit epics of ancient India (the **Ramayana** is the other). Revered in Hinduism and containing philosophical and devotional material — the Bhagavad-Gita is part of it — it was compiled between the 3rd century BCE and the 3rd century CE, running to some 200,000 verse lines and 1.8 million words. It is comparable to the Bible or the Quran in its importance to world civilisation.

## Hinduism

Hinduism **evolved** from Aryan and Indus beliefs. It has **no single founder and no single sacred text** — an unusual feature among world religions, and the reason it is better understood as a tradition than as a doctrine. Its principal texts are the **Vedas**, the **Upanishads**, and the **Bhagavad-Gita**.

| Concept | Meaning |
|---|---|
| **Brahman** | The single spiritual force underlying everything |
| **Atman** | The individual soul |
| **Moksha** | Union with Brahman — release from the cycle of rebirth |
| **Karma** | The consequences of one's actions, carried into future lives |
| **Dharma** | One's moral duties, according to one's station |
| **Ahimsa** | Nonviolence |
| **Reincarnation** | Rebirth of the soul into a new life |

**Caste** organised society into a hierarchy with limited mobility, including those outside the system entirely — the **"untouchables"** or **dalits**. Caste and dharma reinforced each other: one's duties were defined by one's caste position, and fulfilling them well affected one's next life.

## Buddhism

Founded by **Siddhartha Gautama**, the Buddha. Its core teachings are the **Four Noble Truths** and the **Eightfold Path**, leading toward **nirvana**.

Buddhism and Hinduism **share** the concepts of karma, dharma, and reincarnation — Buddhism grew out of the same religious world. But Buddhism **rejects caste** and focuses on **personal enlightenment**, which is available to anyone regardless of birth.

That rejection explains much of Buddhism's history. It **spread widely through Asia** — into China (2.3), Korea, and Japan (4.3) — while **declining in India** as Hinduism absorbed some of its teachings and reasserted the social structure Buddhism had challenged.

## The Maurya Empire (321 – 185 BCE)

In **321 BCE Chandragupta Maurya** established the first Indian empire amid conflicts among rival rajahs in northern India. His court was documented by **Megasthenes**, a Greek ambassador. He expanded from the Ganges valley across northern India and into the Deccan.

The Maurya state was a **structured bureaucracy with strict governance**, including a **secret police**.

**Asoka**, Chandragupta's grandson and the third Mauryan emperor, is the most revered. After a devastating war in **Kalinga** he **converted to Buddhism**, renouncing violence and committing himself to moral governance and religious tolerance. He:

- Erected **stone pillars** throughout India offering moral guidance and promising just rule
- Built **roads and hospitals**
- Sent **missionaries** to spread Buddhism

Asoka is the rare case of a ruler who conquered successfully and then concluded that conquest was wrong. His pillars are the earliest surviving statements of a policy of tolerance by an Indian state.

After Asoka's death the empire **fragmented** under internal strife and the sheer diversity of the subcontinent. The Deccan divided into numerous kingdoms; **Tamil kingdoms** in the south thrived on trade, exporting spices, and developed a rich literary tradition.

## The Gupta Empire (320 – 540 CE) — a golden age

About 500 years after the Mauryas, the **Gupta** dynasty unified much of India. Gupta government was **less centralised** than Maurya government, leaving more authority with local rulers.

**Achievements:**

- **Trade and agriculture** thrived; artisans produced goods for local and international markets
- **Art** — sculpture, murals, temple carvings, and statues of Hindu gods
- **Literature** — including Kalidasa's play *Shakuntala*
- **Mathematics** — Indian mathematicians formalised the **decimal system** and introduced the concept of **zero**, which transformed mathematics worldwide (see 3.3 — these numerals reached Europe through Muslim scholars)
- **Medicine** — surgical techniques, early inoculation practices, medical texts, and the development of **Ayurvedic** medicine

Under the Mauryas Buddhism spread; under the Guptas **Hinduism flourished**, becoming the foundation of later Indian culture.

The Gupta Empire declined through ineffective leadership, civil unrest, and invasions by the **White Huns**, after which India fragmented into regional kingdoms again.

## Family and village life

Most Indians lived in **villages**, governed autonomously by a **headman and council** responsible for local projects, relying on agriculture and trade.

The ideal family was a **joint family**, with several generations living together and making decisions collectively.

**Women's status varied over time and generally narrowed.** Early Aryan women enjoyed more freedom than their later counterparts; by the Gupta period upper-class women faced increasing restrictions, and **dowries** became common in marriage. This trajectory — from relative freedom toward restriction as a society becomes wealthier and more stratified — recurs in several civilisations in this course.
