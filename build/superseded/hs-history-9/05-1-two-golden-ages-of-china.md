# 5.1 Two Golden Ages of China: Tang and Song

## The Era of Division

After the Han, China fragmented into the **"Era of Six Dynasties"**, marked by a **rejection of Confucian principles** — hard work, community interest, rational order — in favour of **emotional satisfaction**, hedonistic pursuits, and philosophy. The **"Seven Sages of the Bamboo Forest"**, a group of writers including the poet **Liu Ling**, exemplify the mood.

**Daoism could not satisfy deeper emotional needs, and Buddhism filled the gap** in the first and second centuries CE. This is the general pattern: Buddhism grew fastest in periods of disorder, when its emphasis on personal salvation answered questions Confucianism did not address.

In the south, **Chinese rule was maintained**.

## The Sui dynasty

After four years of internal division, China was **reunified in 581** by **Yang Jian (Sui Wendi)**, who founded the **Sui** with its capital at **Chang'an**.

He created a **unified ideology** to enhance efficiency, combining **Confucianism with Daoism and Buddhism** — founding monasteries for both doctrines in the capital and appointing Buddhist monks as political advisers. He also launched vast construction projects, including the Grand Canal.

The dynasty ended at the death of Sui Yangdi, Wendi's son, after a short reign — the Sui, like the Qin (2.2), reunified China at enormous human cost and was destroyed by the resentment that cost produced. **Li Yuan**, one of Yangdi's generals, took advantage of the instability and founded the **Tang**.

## The Tang dynasty

**Chang'an became the seat of empire.** The Tang:

- **Pacified the northwest**, giving it the name **Xinjiang**
- Fought a **long conflict with Tibet**, extending Chinese control over the plateau north of the Himalayas
- **Assimilated the southern provinces** into the empire, and established commercial and diplomatic relations with the states of Southeast Asia

China became the foremost power in East Asia. The emperor **demanded tribute** from beyond the frontier: **Korea accepted tributary status** and attempted to adapt the Chinese model, and **Japan dispatched official missions to China to learn** (see 5.3).

### Flowering of culture

The Tang saw the **apogee of Chinese creativity in sculpture and the arts**, driven substantially by **Buddhism**, which affected art, literature, philosophy, religion, and politics. Monasteries proliferated and **Buddhist monks served as advisers at court**.

### Decline

Court intrigues, official corruption, and droughts weakened the dynasty. In **755** rebellious forces seized Chang'an; the rebellion was suppressed but the Tang **never fully recovered**. Loss of government power meant the rise of **great landed families** and **chronic instability** along the northern and western frontiers, where local military commanders ruled without government oversight. The **Kirghiz** defeated the Uighurs and helped overthrow the Tang, leaving chaos, foreign invasion, and division.

## The Song dynasty (960–1279)

The Song was **established in 960**, but from the start its rulers had difficulty defending their territory. **Song Taizu**, the founding emperor, was unable to reconquer the northwest from the **Khitan**.

The capital was **Kaifeng**; under pressure from northern nomads it moved to **Hangzhou**, and the seven emperors who ruled from there are known as the **Southern Song**. In its final decades the dynasty paid tribute to the **Jurchen** of Manchuria.

The Song is the paradox of Chinese history: **economic expansion, prosperity, and cultural achievement combined with political and military weakness**. It could not surmount the external challenges from the north, and that is what brought it down (see 5.2).

## Government

Confucian government, based on principles established under the Qin and Han, matured from the Sui to the end of the Song.

At the apex was the **Grand Council**, assisted by the secretariat and chancellery, with representatives from three authorities: **military, civil, and censorate**. Below sat the **Department of Affairs**, composed of ministries responsible for justice, military affairs, personnel, public works, revenue, and rites.

### The examination system

The **Tang** adopted the practice of selecting some officials through periodic **service examinations**, but its effectiveness was limited: exams were administered only in the capital, and questions covered Buddhist, Daoist, and Confucian texts.

The **Song** transformed the system. It **limited aristocratic control of the bureaucracy** and strengthened central administration by making the examination the **primary route to an official career**, basing questions on **Confucianism**, and **setting up training academies at provincial and district level** to give the poor and the affluent a more equal opportunity.

This is one of the great institutional innovations in world history — a state staffed by competitive examination six centuries before anything comparable in Europe.

**Local government** was retained from earlier dynasties: a **magistrate** handled law and order; village government rested with a **council of elders** and a chief who maintained the militia and collected taxes.

## Economy

**Land reform** was a recurring theme. In the early Tang, land lay in the hands of noble families and peasants were effectively enserfed. Under **Empress Wu**, policy aimed to **reduce the power of the landed nobility** and maximise tax revenue. The Song returned to the same problem: **Wang Anshi (1021–1086)** attempted to **limit the size of landholdings** through progressive land taxes and provided **cheap credit to poor farmers** to help them avoid bankruptcy.

**The Silk Road.** Chinese military forts were established at strategic points from the edge of the Taklamakan Desert to the borders of Central Asia. Goods were traded by **Uighurs** (Turkic-speaking) and **Sogdians** (Iranian-speaking), carried by **Bactrian camel caravans**. Routes had to be chosen carefully — the southern Taklamakan route had bandits, the direct Uighur route was avoided, and mountain routes were arduous because of dryness. **Dunhuang** served as a secondary Tang capital on the route.

**The maritime route** became appealing after the Tang fell in the tenth century. China had long-standing sea trade with neighbouring countries, initially dominated by other groups. The **Song** saw far more maritime activity, spurred by the **compass** and shipbuilding advances; **Canton** had large government-owned ships.

**Gunpowder** was developed under the Tang.

## Religion and philosophy

In the later Tang, **Buddhism and Daoism lost court favour** — resented for their accumulated **wealth and land** and envied by other groups. The state destroyed many temples and forced monks back into secular life. Note the material motive: monasteries had become major landholders, and dissolving them transferred that land to the state.

**Neo-Confucianism** emerged to fill the ideological void and to **strengthen the state's power**. **Zhu Xi** combined the Confucian classics with Buddhist and Daoist metaphysics, emphasising the **"investigation of things"**. His ideas became central to Confucian orthodoxy, though scholars such as **Wang Yangming** criticised them. Neo-Confucianism emphasised the **inner search of the self** and incorporated Buddhist concepts such as karma, influencing Confucian views of hierarchy.

Sects such as the **White Lotus** carried political aspirations alongside religious ones.

## Society

From the end of the Han to the Ming, Chinese society became more complex: **urban populations grew** and the social structure diversified with more merchants and artisans.

**The rise of the gentry.** In the early Tang, powerful noble families held sway; the examination system produced the **scholar-gentry**, who replaced the aristocracy in influence. Gentry status depended on **examination success** and family circumstances — a genuinely different basis for elite membership from inherited land.

**Village and family life.** Most Chinese lived in villages with limited outside contact. The ideal family was **multi-generational**, with the eldest male presiding over ancestral rites.

**Women.** In the **Tang**, some women were politically active. In the **Song**, **foot-binding** became common among the gentry. Women also had economic roles — in silk production and in running businesses. The Tang-to-Song trajectory is again one of narrowing.

## Technology, literature, and art

**Paper** was invented in China, and **woodblock printing** emerged by the first century BCE; by the seventh century CE the Chinese had advanced wood carving and printing techniques. **Movable type** and block printing were used into modern times, popularising literature.

**Poetry** reached its height in the Tang. **Li Bo**'s work is carefree and imaginative; **Du Fu**'s poems reflect the social and political turmoil of the late Tang — the two are usually studied together precisely because they represent opposite responses to the same age.

**Prose and history.** Each dynasty produced an official history of its predecessor; local gazetteers and encyclopedias added regional knowledge. Novels such as **The Romance of the Three Kingdoms** and **The Water Margin** appeared later.

**Popular entertainment** thrived with the growth of cities: comedians, musicians, boxers, acrobats, marionettes, shadow plays, and storytellers.

**Painting** reached a high level under the Tang. Influenced by Buddhism and Daoism, which emphasised nature, Chinese painters developed **monochrome ink painting**, aiming to convey the **essence** of nature rather than a realistic depiction.

**Porcelain**, made of fine clay fired at high temperature, was first produced after the fall of the Han and became popular under the Tang. The **Song** was known for **celadons**; the **Ming** produced multicoloured porcelain with exotic designs. Tang tombs contain ceramic sculptures of horses and camels — the Silk Road rendered in clay.
