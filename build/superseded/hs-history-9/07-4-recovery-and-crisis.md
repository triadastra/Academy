# 7.4 Economic Recovery and the Crisis of the 1300s

## An agricultural revolution

Growth began with technology in the fields:

- **Iron ploughs** came into use in the **800s**, capable of turning the heavy soils of northern Europe that lighter Mediterranean ploughs could not
- A **new harness** made **horse ploughing** possible — horses work faster than oxen
- Lords **cleared land** for more farming
- The **three-field system** was adopted, leaving one field fallow while two were cropped

**Population nearly tripled between 1000 and 1300.**

Everything else in this lesson follows from that tripling. More people than the land needs means people available for towns, trades, and markets.

## The revival of trade and travel

**Trade routes expanded.** **Merchant companies** formed, and routes connected Europe, the Middle East, and Asia. The **Hanseatic League** protected trade in northern Europe.

**Towns and cities grew.** **Trade fairs** drew merchants and generated permanent settlements, and **lords allowed peasants to move to towns**.

That last point is quietly revolutionary. A serf who reached a town and stayed became free, and lords who needed cash more than labour let it happen — which is how the manorial system began dissolving from within.

## The commercial revolution

**Banking and credit.** Banking houses grew; **credit and insurance** systems developed; **letters of credit and bills of exchange** came into use — the same instruments the Muslim world had pioneered (4.3), now serving European long-distance trade.

**Social and economic change.**

- **Serfdom was undermined**
- A **middle class emerged** — merchants and artisans who were neither noble nor peasant, and who fitted nowhere in the feudal scheme of 7.2
- **Jews played a role in lending**, partly because Christians were restricted from charging interest

**Guilds** protected their members' interests, regulated quality and prices, and **provided social services** to members and their families.

## Town and city life

Towns were **surrounded by walls**, **crowded and dirty**, with **busy streets**, constant **risk of fire**, and **no sanitation**. Growth outran infrastructure everywhere — which is part of why the disease of the next section spread as it did.

## The Black Death

In **autumn 1347** a fleet of Genoese trading ships loaded with grain left the Black Sea port of **Caffa** for **Messina, Sicily**. Sailors began falling sick during the voyage, and after the crew arrived the townspeople sickened too. The disease spread from Italy to Spain and France and was called the **Black Death**.

**What it was:** **bubonic plague**, spread by fleas carried by rats. The disease had appeared before and subsided, but a strain survived in **Mongolia**; the **Mongol conquests of the 1200s** created the trade routes along which it travelled.

This is the dark side of the **Pax Mongolica** in 5.2. The same integration of Eurasia that carried silk and gunpowder carried the plague.

**Normal life broke down.** The plague brought terror. People turned to **magic and witchcraft** for cures; some sought **wild pleasure**, others **beat themselves with nailed whips** (flagellation). **Christians blamed Jews** for the plague, and **thousands of Jews were slaughtered**.

**The economy suffered.** With workers and employers dead, **production declined** and **inflation** broke out. Landowners **converted cropland to sheep pasture**, which needed fewer hands. Peasants **revolted** through the 1300s and 1400s — including the **English Peasants' Revolt of 1381** — as work became scarce and lords tried to hold wages down.

## Upheaval in the Church

**The Church splits.** In **1309 Pope Clement V moved the papal court to Avignon**, just outside the southern border of France. The popes there kept a **lavish court**, and criticism grew. In **1378 reformers elected their own pope in Rome**; the French church responded by choosing a rival. The result was a **schism**, with two popes each claiming legitimacy.

**New heresies.** People dissatisfied with the Church began arguing that Christians should follow **the Bible rather than the Church** — the idea that would drive the Reformation (8.3). The schism made that argument far easier to make: when two popes excommunicate each other, papal authority is difficult to treat as self-evident.

## The Hundred Years' War (1337–1453)

A destructive war between **France and England**, rivals for control of territory and of the **English Channel**.

**England won early victories**, largely through the **longbow**, which allowed archers to break armoured cavalry charges at a distance.

**Joan of Arc**, a seventeen-year-old peasant woman, led the French to several victories. She was **captured by English allies, convicted, and burned at the stake** — and the French regarded her as a **martyr**. In **1453** the French secured final victory using **cannon**.

The technology bookends the war neatly: it opens with the longbow ending the dominance of the mounted knight, and closes with gunpowder ending the dominance of the castle. The military basis of feudalism (7.2) does not survive it.

**Impact:**

- **National feeling** was created on both sides — people began to think of themselves as French or English rather than as subjects of a particular lord
- The **French king expanded his power**
- The **English Parliament gained power**, because it funded the ruler — the origin of the principle that the power to tax is the power to bargain
