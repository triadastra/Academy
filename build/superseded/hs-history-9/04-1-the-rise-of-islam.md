# 4.1 The Rise of Islam

## The Arabian Peninsula before Islam

Islam emerged in the **Arabian Peninsula** in the **A.D. 500s**, in a desert environment inhabited by various Arab tribes. The region was characterised by **Bedouins** — nomadic herders who moved through the desert seeking seasonal pasture for their herds.

**Mecca** was a market town at the crossroads of several caravan routes and a **pilgrimage centre**, housing the **Kaaba**, an ancient temple used for pagan worship.

## Muhammad

**Muhammad** was born in Mecca around **A.D. 570**. He was known for **honesty in business** and was a devoted husband and father, but was troubled by the moral ills of his society — especially greed.

At around **40** he experienced a spiritual awakening while meditating in a cave, hearing the angel **Gabriel** calling him to be God's messenger. He was confused and terrified; his wife encouraged him to accept the call. He then devoted his life to spreading Islam.

**The Hijra.** In **622**, facing threats to their lives, Muhammad and his followers fled Mecca for **Yathrib**, later called **Medina**. The hijra marks the **beginning of the Muslim calendar** — a striking choice: the calendar begins not with Muhammad's birth or his first revelation but with the founding of a **community**.

He later dedicated the Kaaba to **Allah** and established monotheistic worship there.

## Teachings

Islam is a **monotheistic** faith sharing much with Judaism and Christianity. The **Quran** is its sacred text, regarded as the **direct word of God**, emphasising God's power and compassion. It consists of **114 suras** (chapters).

The Quran provides guidance on ethical standards and on life, emphasising **honesty, generosity, and social justice**, and defining righteousness as belief in God combined with good deeds, including charity and prayer.

## The Five Pillars

Observant Muslims perform five essential duties — remembered as **CPFCP**:

| Pillar | Duty |
|---|---|
| **Confession of faith** | The **shahada**: there is no god but God, and Muhammad is his messenger |
| **Prayer** | Five times daily, facing Mecca, in a house of worship called a **mosque** |
| **Fasting** | Between dawn and sunset during the month of **Ramadan** |
| **Charity** | Almsgiving to the poor — **zakat** |
| **Pilgrimage** | The **hajj** to Mecca, to worship at the Kaaba, at least once in a lifetime |

## Jihad and the People of the Book

**Jihad** means **struggle in God's service**, and may be personal or communal.

Muslims, Jews, and Christians are considered **"People of the Book"**, sharing a belief in one God. This category has practical consequences: it is the basis on which conquered Jewish and Christian populations were permitted to keep their religion (see 4.2).

## Sharia

**Islamic law, or Sharia**, integrates religious principles into a legal framework, regulating **moral conduct, family life, and business practices**. It is a body of law that includes interpretations of the Quran.

Because Islam makes no separation between religious and civil authority, Sharia governs areas that other traditions divide between church and state — which is why the question of who may interpret it became a political question immediately after Muhammad's death (4.2).

## Islam and women

**Arab women had limited rights** before Islam. Islam **extended rights and protection to women** by affirming the **spiritual equality of all Muslims**. The Quran:

- Prohibited the **killing of daughters**
- Granted women an **inheritance**
- Allowed women to **reject a marriage offer**
- Gave women **equal right to study the Quran**

Still, women's lives and dress varied by region and class, and women continued to hold **less status than men**. The accurate summary is that Islam improved the position of women **relative to pre-Islamic Arabia** without establishing equality — and that later practice in many regions diverged from the Quran's provisions.

## Sources

Beyond the Quran, the **Sira** is the biography of Muhammad, and together with the recorded sayings and actions of the Prophet it supplements scripture as a source of guidance.
