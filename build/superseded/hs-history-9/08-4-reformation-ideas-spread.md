# 8.4 Reformation Ideas Spread

## An explosion of Protestant sects

As the Reformation continued, numerous new sects emerged — the predictable consequence of the principle that scripture alone is authoritative and every believer may read it. Once the Church's interpretive monopoly is broken, there is no mechanism for settling disagreements.

**Anabaptists** were a radical group who **rejected infant baptism**, arguing infants were too young to understand the faith. Some Anabaptist sects sought social change, wanting to abolish private property or hasten judgement day. **Most Anabaptists were peaceful**, calling for **religious toleration and the separation of church and state** — ideas far ahead of their time, and for which they were persecuted by Catholics and Protestants alike. **Baptists, Mennonites, and the Amish** trace their roots to them.

## The English Reformation

In the 1300s **John Wycliffe** had called for reform in England, and by the 1520s some clergy were exploring Protestant ideas.

**Henry VIII initially opposed Protestantism** — the pope gave him the title **"Defender of the Faith"** for denouncing Luther.

In **1527** Henry wanted an **annulment** of his marriage to **Catherine of Aragon** so he could marry **Anne Boleyn** and produce a male heir. **The pope refused**, not wanting to offend **Charles V**, Catherine's nephew.

*Why did Henry's personal life trigger England's Reformation?* Because the papal refusal was itself political — the pope was constrained by the Emperor — and because Henry's remedy required denying papal authority entirely. A dynastic problem could only be solved by a constitutional one.

**Henry broke with the Church.** Guided by **Thomas Cromwell**, Parliament passed laws removing the English church from papal control, and Henry appointed **Thomas Cranmer** archbishop. In **1534** Parliament passed the **Act of Supremacy**, making the monarch **head of the Church of England**. Loyal Catholics who refused to accept it — including **Sir Thomas More** — were executed.

Between **1536 and 1540** Henry **closed the Catholic convents and monasteries**, confiscating their lands and wealth and granting some to nobles to secure their support for the Anglican Church. That redistribution created a class of landowners with a permanent material stake in the Reformation's success — one reason it was not reversed.

**The Anglican Church kept Catholic rituals while rejecting papal authority.**

**After Henry:**

- **Edward VI** and his advisers were Protestants; Parliament passed Protestant reforms and Cranmer drew up the **Book of Common Prayer**
- **Mary Tudor** tried to restore Catholicism, and hundreds of Protestants — Cranmer among them — were **burned at the stake**
- **Elizabeth I** became queen in **1558**. The **Elizabethan Settlement (1559)** was a **compromise** between Protestant doctrine and Catholic tradition: it **preserved Catholic rituals**, **reaffirmed the monarch's spiritual authority**, and restored a version of the Book of Common Prayer

Elizabeth's settlement was deliberately ambiguous, and that was its strength: it was designed to be acceptable to as many of her subjects as possible after three violent reversals in twenty-five years.

## The Catholic Reformation

Led by **Pope Paul III**, the Catholic Reformation — or Counter-Reformation — aimed to **revive the Church's moral authority** and counter the Protestant tide.

**The Council of Trent (1545–1563)** met for almost twenty years and:

- **Reaffirmed** salvation through **faith and good works**, against Luther's "faith alone"
- Affirmed **both the Bible and Church tradition** as sources of truth
- **Reformed abuses** — penalised corrupt clergy and improved clerical education

Note that Trent conceded the **abuses** while conceding none of the **doctrine**. It made the Church harder to attack on the grounds Luther had actually used, without moving toward Protestantism.

**Pope Paul strengthened the Inquisition**, which used secret testimony, torture, and execution against Protestantism, and created the **Index of Forbidden Books**, banning Protestant and humanist works.

**The Jesuits (Society of Jesus)** were founded in **1540** by **Ignatius of Loyola** to combat heresy. They maintained strict spiritual and moral discipline, and their methods were distinctive:

- **Schools** — education rather than confrontation
- **Advisers to Catholic rulers**
- **Missions** worldwide, spreading Catholicism to Asia and the Americas

*How did the Jesuits differ from Protestant reformers?* The Protestants attacked the institution; the Jesuits worked **through** it, and won ground by teaching the next generation rather than by disputing with the present one.

**Teresa of Avila**, a Spanish nun, **reformed convents and monasteries** and emphasised prayer and meditation. Her spiritual writings were influential and she was canonised.

**By 1600** the Catholic Reformation had reduced Church abuses and won back some converts, but **Protestantism retained a major foothold** in Europe.

## Widespread persecution

During the Reformation both Catholics and Protestants persecuted radical sects, accused witches, and Jews.

**Witch hunts (1450–1750).** Tens of thousands, **mostly women** — midwives and social outcasts among them — were accused of devil worship and of being anti-Christian. Persecution **peaked in Germany and Switzerland during the religious conflicts** and **declined when the religious wars ended**.

*Were witch hunts more about religion or social control?* The timing is the strongest evidence. Accusations rose and fell with religious conflict and social stress rather than with any change in doctrine, and they concentrated on women with little social protection — which points toward social control operating through religious language.

**Jewish persecution.** **Venice ordered Jews into ghettos in 1516**. **Luther turned against the Jews** and called for their expulsion when they did not convert. Popes and emperors imposed further restrictions, and Jews were **expelled from Spain and various German states**, leading to widespread migration.
