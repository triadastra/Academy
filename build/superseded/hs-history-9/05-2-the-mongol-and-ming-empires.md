# 5.2 The Mongol and Ming Empires

## The Mongols

The Mongols were a **nomadic** people of the steppe. **Temujin** united the Mongol tribes and in **1206** was elected **Genghis Khan** — "Universal Ruler" — beginning the Mongol expansion.

Neighbouring peoples included the **Keraites** (Turkic-Mongol Nestorian Christians), the **Tatars** northeast of the Gobi Desert, who initially opposed Temujin, and the **Merkits**, who kidnapped Temujin's wife and were later defeated and absorbed.

Mongol forces eventually conquered an empire stretching **from the Pacific to Eastern Europe**, held together by **strict military discipline** and demands of **absolute loyalty**.

### Military system

The Mongol army was extraordinarily effective:

- **Accomplished horsemen**, excellent with the **short bow**, which can be fired from horseback
- Tactics including **tireless flying columns, ambushes, and feigned retreats**
- Later, **battering rams, cannons, catapults, flaming arrows, and gunpowder** — learned from the Chinese and Turks
- The **yam system** of relay stations and messengers, enabling rapid communication across vast distances
- **Spies and informants** who mapped territories before invasion, combined with cavalry

The organisation was decimal: the **arban** was the base unit of 10 warriors; the **tumen** the largest, of 10,000.

The feigned retreat deserves attention because it is the tactic that most consistently destroyed larger armies. A pursuing enemy breaks formation; a disciplined force that can turn and reform at speed then destroys it piecemeal. This required exactly the discipline the Mongols had and their opponents did not.

### Conquest of China

After defeating the **Jurchen**, the Mongols turned on the **Song**, which was already weakened by **internal factionalism and loss of tax revenue**. Genghis Khan died in **1227**; Mongol warriors completed the conquest of China by **1279** — using **gunpowder** — and established the **Yuan dynasty**. **Kublai Khan** completed the conquest of the Song and moved the capital to **Dadu**, modern Beijing, turning **Khanbaliq** into a Chinese walled city.

### The Golden Horde and Russia

**Batu**, Genghis Khan's grandson, led the invasion of Russia — **the only successful winter invasion of Russia in history**, since the Mongols used frozen rivers as roads where others were stopped by mud. In **1237–1238** Mongol forces destroyed the cities that resisted, including Kiev. Russia was divided into many small kingdoms and could not defend itself. They also conquered **Hungary**, which had the most developed military of the time; the eventual goal was all of Europe.

**Consequences for Russia:**

- **Religious toleration of the Orthodox Church** — the Mongols knew that suppressing it would cause problems
- **Increased Eurasian trade**, acting as a conduit between Europe, the Middle East, and Asia — honey, glassware, slaves, gunpowder, firearms, gems, textiles, and rugs
- **High taxes on peasants**, who gave up land and crops to the aristocracy in exchange for protection — an origin of **serfdom**
- **Russia isolated** from Western European influence, commercially and culturally

The conquest of Europe was **interrupted by the death of Ögedei** and the succession crisis that followed — the empire's chief structural weakness, since every khan's death recalled the armies for a leadership contest.

### Administration and division

Genghis Khan set up a capital at **Karakorum** and initially **prohibited his fellow Mongols from sedentary occupations** — an attempt to preserve the nomadic way of life that made them formidable. His successors adapted to the regions they conquered, and Mongol aristocrats began taking administrative positions.

After his death the unified empire **divided into khanates**:

- The **Khanate of Kipchak** (the Golden Horde)
- The **Khanate of Chaghadai**
- The **Khanate of Persia** (the Il-Khans)
- The **Great Khanate**

Power was increasingly decentralised to sub-khanates.

### Trade and diplomacy

Chinese ports exported tea, silk, porcelain, precious stones, cotton, and tropical products, with **Canton** a major port. In **1245 Pope Innocent IV** sent **John of Plano Carpini** to the Mongol headquarters; **Güyük Khan's reply demanded the Pope's submission**.

### Mongol rule in China

Yuan rulers under Kublai Khan sent armies into **Vietnam** and launched expeditions against Malay kingdoms in **Java and Sumatra** — but the conquests of **Vietnam and Japan failed**.

The Mongols initially **struggled to govern China**. They adopted Chinese political systems, used local talent, and retained some existing administrative structures. **Culturally, Mongols and Chinese remained separate**: Mongols followed their own laws, and while some converted to Buddhism, most commoners kept their traditional religions.

### Decline of the Yuan

From the **1340s**: excessive spending on conquests, tax problems, factionalism, corruption, and natural disasters. The Yuan was replaced by the **Ming** in 1368.

### Assessment

Genghis Khan created an effective military system and used innovative tactics against enemies on the steppe. But after gaining power the Mongols **had trouble transitioning from nomadic to sedentary rule**, causing leadership instability. Their expansion **brought death and suffering on an enormous scale**, but also created the **Pax Mongolica** — a period of relative peace that made trade across Eurasia safe and rapid for the first time.

Both halves of that judgement are needed. The same empire that depopulated cities also made it possible for goods, technologies, and ideas — and, in the 1340s, the plague (6.4) — to cross Eurasia in months.

## The Ming dynasty

The Ming expanded into **Mongolia and Central Asia** and briefly **reconquered Vietnam**. It **fortified the northern frontier with the Great Wall** and maintained tributary relationships with neighbouring states.

Ming rulers **reinstated traditional Confucian institutions**, using **civil service examinations** to select bureaucrats and dividing the empire for administration — deliberately restoring what the Yuan had displaced.

### The Great Wall and internal security

The Ming built major sections of the **Great Wall** for protection against northern invasion. The **Jinyiwei** — the "embroidered uniform guard", founded by the **Hongwu** emperor — evolved into a military organisation and **spy network**.

The **Yongle emperor** rebelled against his nephew the Jianwen emperor, **moved the capital to Beijing**, built the **Forbidden City**, expanded the **palace eunuch secret police**, and expanded the **civil service examination** system.

### Zheng He's voyages

In **1405** the Yongle emperor sent **Zheng He** on voyages with an enormous fleet, reaching the **east coast of Africa**. The purposes were **trade expansion, alliance building, and the projection of power**, and the voyages continued after Yongle's death.

The scale is worth registering: Zheng He's fleets were far larger than anything Europe would send out for another century (7.1 and 8.1). The voyages were then **discontinued** — a decision that makes the European expansion of the next century look less like inevitability and more like a choice China declined to make.
