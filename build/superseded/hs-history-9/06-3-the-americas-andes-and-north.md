# 6.3 The Andes and North America

## Early Andean cultures

**Caral (Peru)** was one of the **earliest urban centres, c. 2500 BCE**, with pyramids, plazas, and astronomical structures — arguably an early civilisation contemporary with Mesopotamia. Its people practised **irrigation farming** and traded with coastal communities in cotton, fish, and reeds. There is evidence of **social stratification** and of cultural activity in music and crafts.

**Chavín culture** emerged in the first millennium BCE, known for **stone temples** and **metallurgy** in copper and gold. It declined by 200 BCE, leaving artistic and religious influences behind.

**The Moche (Peru, first millennium CE)** built **adobe pyramids** — including the Pyramid of the Sun — and produced intricate ceramics and metalwork. Their culture focused on **warfare, sacrifice, and astronomy**, with deities such as **Ai Apaec**, the "Decapitator God". Moche rituals included **human sacrifices to invoke rain or prevent disasters**.

The Moche **collapsed by the eighth century** due to **environmental disruption** — El Niño floods followed by drought. Little is known of their culture directly; it survives mainly through artefacts.

**The Wari** expanded into Moche territory as the Moche declined, **adopting and repurposing Moche religious symbols and buildings** — a common pattern of successor states borrowing legitimacy from their predecessors.

**The Chimú** built **Chan Chan**, a large adobe city with advanced irrigation. Chimú civilisation collapsed by the fifteenth century when **floods and earthquakes damaged the irrigation systems** on which it depended.

### Environmental impact

Climate change is a recurring cause of collapse in this region and beyond: droughts and floods contributed to the fall of the **Moche**, the **Maya city-states**, and possibly the **Roman Empire**.

**El Niño** caused alternating **droughts and floods**, damaging both agriculture and fishing; **La Niña** follows it, bringing hurricanes.

The Andean pattern is instructive. These were societies whose wealth rested on **irrigation infrastructure**, which is precisely what a flood destroys — the same engineering that made them prosperous made them fragile.

## The Inca Empire

**Geography.** The Inca ruled the **Andes**, where **mountains made farming difficult**; the solution was **terracing** — cutting flat steps into steep slopes.

**Society.** The emperor, the **Sapa Inca**, ruled everything.

**Religion.** They worshipped the sun god **Inti**.

### Achievements

| Field | Achievement |
|---|---|
| **Architecture** | Precision stone buildings, including **Machu Picchu** |
| **Roads** | A **14,000-mile road system** through the mountains, with **suspension bridges** |
| **Record-keeping** | The **quipu** — knotted strings recording numbers and records |
| **Agriculture** | **Terrace farming** and irrigation; potatoes, maize, and quinoa |
| **Astronomy** | Used to plan farming; **solar temples** built |

**Why the quipu mattered.** The Inca had **no written language**. The quipu, a system of knotted cords, carried the numerical records — census figures, tribute owed, storehouse contents — that a state of ten million people required. It is the answer to the puzzle raised in 1.1: the Inca met every criterion of civilisation except writing, and the quipu is how they managed without it.

**Why roads and farming systems mattered so much.** In terrain where an army or a message might take weeks to cross a valley, the road system was the empire's nervous system, carrying runners, soldiers, and goods. Terracing and irrigation were what made a large population possible on mountainsides at all. Both were state projects, and both are why Inca rule could reach so far.

**Fall.** **Civil war weakened the empire** immediately before the Spanish arrival, and it was defeated by the conquistador **Pizarro** (see 8.2). The civil war is the essential part of the explanation — the question "how did a few hundred Spaniards defeat an empire of millions?" cannot be answered by weapons alone.

## North America

### Eastern Woodlands

**Agriculture began in the third millennium BCE**, focused on **maize, squash, beans, and grasses**. **Sedentary villages** emerged, with **councils** to resolve disputes, and some regions uniting under chiefs.

**Trade networks** spanned from **Lake Superior to the Gulf of Mexico**, exchanging meat, shells, and tools.

**Cahokia** was a major urban centre near modern East St. Louis, Illinois, with large **earthen mounds**. It served as an administrative and trade hub, with evidence of Mesoamerican influence such as **ball courts**. It declined by the twelfth century, possibly for environmental or social reasons.

**The Iroquois League** was a group of tribes that formed a **peace alliance** — a confederation with an agreed procedure for resolving disputes among its members.

**Potlatch** was a **gift-giving ceremony** used to display wealth and gain respect — a system in which status comes from what you give away rather than what you keep.

### The Southwest

The **Ancestral Pueblo peoples** developed agriculture in the arid Southwest — Colorado, New Mexico, and Arizona — with **advanced irrigation** and trade networks.

| Term | Meaning |
|---|---|
| **Pueblo** | Adobe or stone houses used in Southwest villages |
| **Pueblo Bonito** | A large pueblo in Chaco Canyon with over 600 rooms |
| **Mesa Verde** | Cliff homes in Colorado built by the Ancestral Puebloans |
| **Kiva** | An underground room for religious meetings |
| **Earthwork** | Large mounds of earth used for burials or ceremonies |

**Migration in the thirteenth century** may have been caused by **drought, warfare, or external pressure**. Descendants include the **Hopi and Zuni** — note that all Hopi are Pueblo, but not all Pueblo are Hopi.
