# 5.4 Electron Configurations

**Electron configurations** are the ways in which electrons are arranged in the various orbitals around the nuclei of atoms. They **determine the chemical properties of elements** and are the origin of the periodic trends in Chapter 7 — which is why this is the most consequential section of the atomic-structure unit.

## The three filling rules

The most stable arrangement of the electrons indicates the **ground state** of the atom, and is found by applying three rules in order.

**I. The Aufbau principle** — electrons occupy **orbitals of lowest energy first**, following the order established in 5.3:

$$1s < 2s < 2p < 3s < 3p < 4s < 3d < 4p < 5s < 4d < 5p < 6s < 4f < 5d < 6p < 7s < 5f < 6d < 7p$$

**II. The Pauli exclusion principle** — an atomic orbital may describe **at most two electrons, with opposite spins**. **Spin** is a quantum mechanical property of electrons and may be thought of as clockwise or counterclockwise.

- **Paired** — two opposite-spin electrons occupying one orbital.
- **Unpaired** — a single electron in one orbital.

**III. Hund's rule** — for **degenerate** orbitals, the lowest energy is attained when the **number of electrons with the same spin is maximised**. In practice: put one electron in each orbital of a sublevel before pairing any of them.

Hund's rule follows from repulsion — two electrons in the same orbital are closer together and repel more strongly than two in different orbitals of the same energy.

## Orbital diagrams

Each **box** in an orbital diagram represents one orbital, **half-arrows** represent electrons, and the **direction of the arrow** represents the spin. Orbital diagrams show unpaired electrons explicitly, which configurations alone do not.

## Writing electron configurations

A configuration is written as a sequence of terms: a **number** denoting the energy level, a **letter** denoting the orbital type, and a **superscript** giving the number of electrons in that sublevel.

Carbon in its ground state:

$$1s^2\,2s^2\,2p^2$$

An **excited state** shows one or more electrons promoted to a higher level than the ground state permits — for carbon, for instance, $1s^2\,2s^2\,2p^1\,3s^1$.

**Using the periodic table** to write a configuration:

1. Locate the element — which **block** and **group** it is in, and the **nearest preceding noble gas**.
2. Put together all the orbitals before that block.
3. Count the group of the element to determine the number of electrons in the unfilled orbital.

## Condensed configurations

An **abbreviated** (condensed, or noble-gas notation) configuration replaces the inner electrons with the symbol of the **nearest preceding noble gas** in brackets. Noble gases — He, Ne, Ar, Kr — are the most stable elements, and their configurations correspond to filled levels.

Three terms for the resulting groups:

- **Core electrons** — the inner-shell electrons, represented by the noble-gas symbol.
- **Outer-shell electrons** — those written after the noble-gas core.
- **Valence electrons** — the electrons involved in chemical bonding.

For elements with **atomic number 30 or less**, all of the outer-shell electrons are valence electrons. Beyond that the two are not the same, because filled $d$ sublevels count as outer-shell but not as valence.

## Exceptions

**Some actual electron configurations differ from those assigned using the Aufbau principle**, because **half-filled and fully-filled sublevels are unusually stable**. The standard examples:

$$\mathrm{Cr}: 1s^2 2s^2 2p^6 3s^2 3p^6 3d^5 4s^1$$
$$\mathrm{Cu}: 1s^2 2s^2 2p^6 3s^2 3p^6 3d^{10} 4s^1$$

rather than the predicted $3d^4 4s^2$ and $3d^9 4s^2$. The same exception occurs for the elements directly below them — **Mo, Ag, and Au**.

## Configurations for ions

First write the configuration of the element's **neutral** state, then add or remove electrons:

- **Metal atoms lose electrons** to form **cations**.
- **Nonmetal atoms gain electrons** to form **anions**.
- **Transition metals lose outermost-shell electrons first, then inner $d$-level electrons.**

The transition-metal rule is the one most often got wrong. Iron is $[\mathrm{Ar}]3d^6 4s^2$, so $\mathrm{Fe}^{2+}$ is $[\mathrm{Ar}]3d^6$ — the $4s$ electrons leave first, even though $4s$ was filled before $3d$. Filling order and removal order are not the same thing.
