# 6.1 Organizing and Classifying the Elements

This lesson traces how the periodic table reached its modern form, then classifies the elements by block and by metallic character — the two classification schemes that make the trends in 6.2 predictable.

## Searching for an organising principle

In the late 1790s **Antoine Lavoisier** compiled a list of 23 known elements. By 1870 approximately **70 elements** were recognised. As chemists began to use scientific methods to search for elements, the rate of discovery increased sharply, and the need for an organising principle became pressing.

Two early attempts:

- **The law of triads** — groups of three elements whose members have similar chemical properties.
- **The law of octaves** — **John Newlands**, 1864, observed that when elements are arranged in order of increasing atomic mass, the properties repeat every **eighth** element. This is a genuine periodic change, since the pattern repeats at a fixed interval.

**1860:** chemists finally agreed on a method for determining atomic masses — a prerequisite for any mass-based ordering.

## Mendeleev's periodic table

In **1869 Dmitri Mendeleev** and **Lothar Meyer** independently connected atomic mass with elemental properties. Mendeleev is credited because he **published first and demonstrated the usefulness of his table**.

What Mendeleev did:

- Arranged the elements in order of **increasing atomic mass**.
- Placed elements with **similar properties in the same column**, showing that properties repeat in an orderly way from row to row.
- **Corrected the atomic masses** of several elements (Be, In, U, Os, Ir, Pt, Y, Ti).
- Sometimes **broke the pattern of increasing atomic mass** in order to group similar properties together.
- **Left blank spaces** for unknown elements and predicted their existence and properties — successfully, for **scandium, gallium, and germanium** (his "eka-silicon").

The successful predictions are what elevated the table from a filing system to a scientific theory: it made testable claims about elements nobody had seen.

Element number 101, **mendelevium**, is named after him.

## The modern periodic table

In **1913 Henry Moseley** determined an atomic number for each known element and arranged them by **atomic number instead of atomic mass**, which revealed a clear periodic pattern with no exceptions.

**The periodic law:** when elements are arranged in order of **increasing atomic number**, there is a **periodic repetition of their physical and chemical properties**.

This explains Mendeleev's anomalies. **He found the correct pattern even though he arranged the elements according to the wrong property** — atomic mass generally increases with atomic number, so the two orderings almost always agree, and where they disagree Mendeleev's chemical instinct overrode the mass ordering.

The modern table has **7 periods and 18 groups**:

- **Periods 1–3:** shorter periods — 2, 8, 8 elements.
- **Periods 4–5:** longer periods — 18, 18 elements.
- **Periods 6–7:** 32 elements each.

The **International Union of Pure and Applied Chemistry (IUPAC)** sets standards for chemistry, including a 1–18 group numbering that has not been widely adopted alongside the older A/B system.

## Electron configurations and the blocks

Electrons that occupy the **highest principal energy level** are the atom's outermost electrons. **Valence electrons are involved in chemical bonding** and are largely responsible for an atom's chemical behaviour. For lighter elements (atomic number < 30), all outermost electrons are valence electrons.

The periodic table is structured so that **elements with the same pattern of outermost-level electron configuration are arranged in columns** — this is the actual reason for the periodic law, and it connects 5.4 directly to the table's shape.

The table divides into four blocks:

| Block | Columns | Elements | Classification |
|---|---|---|---|
| **s** | 2 | H, He, Groups 1A–2A | Representative |
| **p** | 6 | Groups 3A–8A (except He) | Representative |
| **d** | 10 | Groups 1B–8B (groups 3–12) | Transition elements |
| **f** | 14 | Lanthanides and actinides | Inner transition elements |

Classification by the electron configuration of the highest occupied energy level:

- **Representative elements** — s or p sublevel only partially filled: $ns^1$–$ns^2$, $ns^2np^1$–$ns^2np^6$.
- **Transition metals** — the s sublevel and the nearby d sublevel contain electrons.
- **Inner transition metals** — the f sublevel is filling.

For representative elements, the **group number equals the number of electrons in the highest occupied energy level** — the fastest way to find an element's valence count.

## Metals, nonmetals, and metalloids

**Across a period, the properties of elements become less metallic and more nonmetallic.**

**Metals:**

- **Lustre** — caused by the metal's ability to reflect light.
- Good conductors of heat and electricity. **Copper is second only to silver** as a conductor of electric current.
- **Solid at room temperature** (except mercury, Hg).
- **Malleable** — can be hammered into thin sheets without breaking. **Ductile** — can be drawn into wires. Aluminium is one of the metals that can be shaped into a thin sheet, or foil.

**Nonmetals:**

- There is **greater variation in physical properties among nonmetals** than among metals.
- Most are **gases** at room temperature, including nitrogen and oxygen; a few are solids, such as sulfur and phosphorus; **bromine is the only liquid**.
- Usually gases or **brittle** solids.
- **Poor conductors** of heat and electricity — **graphite is an exception**.

**Metalloids (semimetals):**

- Slight lustre, brittle.
- Their behaviour can often be **controlled by changing the conditions** — the property that makes silicon and germanium the basis of semiconductor electronics.
