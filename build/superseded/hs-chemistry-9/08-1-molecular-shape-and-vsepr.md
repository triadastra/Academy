# 8.1 Molecular Shape and VSEPR Theory

A Lewis structure says which atoms are bonded; it does not say where they sit in space. **Valence-shell electron-pair repulsion (VSEPR) theory** supplies the three-dimensional shape, from a single principle: **the repulsion between electron pairs causes molecular shapes to adjust so that the valence-electron pairs stay as far apart as possible.**

## Electron domains

- **Shared pair** — a bond between two atoms counts as **one shared pair, regardless of how many electrons are actually shared**.
- **Nonbonding (lone) pair** — a lone pair of electrons on the central atom.
- **Bond angle** — the geometric angle between two adjacent bonds.

Both kinds of pair are called **electron domains**. **A double or triple bond counts as one domain**, because all the electrons shared between those two atoms lie on the same side of the central atom. This is why $\mathrm{CO_2}$, with two double bonds, has 2 domains and is linear.

Two repulsion rules matter:

- **Lone pairs repel more strongly than bonding pairs**, so they compress bond angles. A lone pair is held by only one nucleus, so it spreads out and "takes up more space", squeezing the bonding pairs together.
- **Double and triple bonds** place greater electron density on one side of the central atom than single bonds do, so they exert slightly more repulsion.

## Predicting a shape

1. **Draw the Lewis structure** — identify the central atom and count valence electrons.
2. **Count electron domains** — bonding domains = number of bonds (single, double, or triple each count as one); nonbonding domains = number of lone pairs.
3. **Determine the electron-domain geometry** — 2 domains linear, 3 trigonal planar, 4 tetrahedral, 5 trigonal bipyramidal, 6 octahedral.
4. **Predict the molecular shape** — **ignore the lone pairs** when naming the shape. Only the positions of the atoms count.

$\mathrm{NH_3}$ has $5 + 3\times1 = 8$ electrons: 3 bonding pairs + 1 lone pair, so 4 domains, tetrahedral domain geometry, **trigonal pyramidal** molecular shape.

Step 4 is where most errors occur. Electron-domain geometry and molecular shape are different answers to different questions, and a question asking for "the shape" wants the second.

## The shapes

| Domains | Domain geometry | Bonding | Nonbonding | Molecular shape | Bond angle | Example |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| 2 | Linear | 2 | 0 | Linear | 180° | $\mathrm{CO_2}$, $\mathrm{BeF_2}$ |
| 3 | Trigonal planar | 3 | 0 | Trigonal planar | 120° | $\mathrm{BCl_3}$ |
| 3 | Trigonal planar | 2 | 1 | Bent | <120° | $\mathrm{SO_2}$ |
| 4 | Tetrahedral | 4 | 0 | Tetrahedral | 109.5° | $\mathrm{CH_4}$ |
| 4 | Tetrahedral | 3 | 1 | Trigonal pyramidal | 107° | $\mathrm{NH_3}$, $\mathrm{PCl_3}$ |
| 4 | Tetrahedral | 2 | 2 | Bent | 104.5° | $\mathrm{H_2O}$, $\mathrm{SCl_2}$ |
| 5 | Trigonal bipyramidal | 5 | 0 | Trigonal bipyramidal | 90°/120° | $\mathrm{PF_5}$, $\mathrm{PCl_5}$ |
| 6 | Octahedral | 6 | 0 | Octahedral | 90° | $\mathrm{SF_6}$ |
| 6 | Octahedral | 4 | 2 | Square planar | 90° | $\mathrm{XeF_4}$ |

Notes on the individual shapes:

- **Linear** — the atoms can be connected by a straight line.
- **Trigonal planar** — a flat, triangular shape; the three pairs are as far apart as possible at 120°.
- **Tetrahedral** — the four hydrogens in methane sit at the corners of a regular tetrahedron, with every H–C–H angle 109.5°.
- **Trigonal pyramidal** — nitrogen in $\mathrm{NH_3}$ is surrounded by four pairs, one unshared. The unshared pair strongly repels the bonding pairs, pushing them together to 107°.
- **Bent** — two bonding pairs and two lone pairs, as in water, at roughly 104.5°.

The 109.5° → 107° → 104.5° sequence across $\mathrm{CH_4}$, $\mathrm{NH_3}$, $\mathrm{H_2O}$ is the cleanest illustration of lone-pair repulsion: each lone pair added shaves a few degrees off the angle.

## Larger molecules

For larger molecules, the geometry of a **particular atom** is discussed rather than the molecule as a whole. Each central atom is assigned its own shape and bond angle — one carbon may be tetrahedral at 109.5° while an oxygen further along the chain is bent at about 105°.

## Molecular polarity

**Polarity** refers to a separation of positive and negative charge.

- A **polar molecule (dipole)** has one end with a positive charge and another end with a negative charge.
- A **nonpolar molecule** has no positive or negative ends.

**A molecule containing polar bonds is NOT necessarily a polar molecule.** The **shape of the molecule and the polarity of its bonds together** determine the result: bond dipoles are vectors, and symmetric arrangements cancel.

The rules:

- A molecule containing **only nonpolar bonds** is usually nonpolar (ozone, $\mathrm{O_3}$, is an exception).
- A molecule with polar bonds and a **symmetrical shape** — where the positive and negative charge centres coincide — is **nonpolar**.
- A **polar molecule** usually contains polar bonds **and an asymmetrical structure**.

| Shape | All outside atoms the same? | Polarity |
|---|---|---|
| $\mathrm{AX_2}$ linear | Yes | Nonpolar |
| $\mathrm{AX_2}$ linear | No | Polar |
| Bent | — | Polar |
| $\mathrm{AX_3}$ trigonal planar | Yes | Nonpolar |
| $\mathrm{AX_3}$ trigonal planar | No | Polar |
| Trigonal pyramidal | — | Polar |
| $\mathrm{AX_4}$ tetrahedral | Yes | Nonpolar |
| $\mathrm{AX_4}$ tetrahedral | No | Polar |

The shapes containing lone pairs — bent and pyramidal — are **always polar**, because a lone pair can never be cancelled by anything opposite it.

**Water is polar, carbon dioxide is not**, despite both having polar bonds. $\mathrm{CO_2}$ is linear, so the two C=O dipoles point in exactly opposite directions and cancel. Water is bent, so its two O–H dipoles add to a net dipole pointing away from the oxygen — which is the origin of nearly every unusual property of water.

*Practice:* classify $\mathrm{BrCl}$, $\mathrm{SO_2}$, and $\mathrm{SF_6}$ as polar or nonpolar. ($\mathrm{SF_6}$ is octahedral and symmetric with identical outer atoms — nonpolar. $\mathrm{XeF_4}$ is square planar and symmetric with identical F atoms — also nonpolar, despite the two lone pairs, because they sit opposite each other.)
