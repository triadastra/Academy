# 7.2 Covalent Bonding and Lewis Structures

A **covalent bond** is formed when two atoms **share** one or more pairs of electrons, so that both can achieve an octet. It commonly occurs between **nonmetals or metalloids** — atoms with fairly high electronegativities, where neither is willing to surrender an electron outright.

The general rule for predicting compound type:

| Combination | Compound |
|---|---|
| Metal + nonmetal | Ionic |
| Nonmetal + nonmetal | Molecular (covalent) |

## Why sharing works

Covalent bonds form through the sharing of electrons by adjacent atoms, so the **orbitals of the two atoms must overlap**.

Increased overlap brings the electrons and nuclei together while simultaneously decreasing electron–electron repulsion — the energy falls. But **if the atoms get too close, the internuclear repulsion sharply raises the energy**. The bond length is the separation at which these two effects balance, giving the minimum energy.

## Single, double, and triple bonds

- **Single bond** — only one pair of electrons is shared. In $\mathrm{H_2}$ each hydrogen atom shares one electron.
- **Double bond** — two shared pairs, as in $\mathrm{CO_2}$.
- **Triple bond** — three shared pairs, as in $\mathrm{N_2}$.

Multiple bonds form when the octet cannot otherwise be satisfied.

**Bond energy trends:**

- Stronger covalent bonds have **shorter** bond lengths.
- **Multiple bonds are shorter and stronger** than single bonds; triple is shorter than double.

## Molecular and structural formulas

- **Molecular formula** — shows all the atoms in one molecule, e.g. $\mathrm{NH_3(g)}$.
- **Structural formula** — uses element symbols and bonds to show the relative positions of atoms. Lewis structures are structural formulas.

## Drawing Lewis structures

1. **Sum the valence electrons**, including any charge — add one electron per negative charge, subtract one per positive charge.
2. **Identify the central atom** — usually the **least electronegative** atom.
3. **Connect the atoms with single bonds.**
4. **Complete the octets of the surrounding atoms.**
5. **Place any leftover electrons on the central atom.**
6. **If the central atom's octet is not fulfilled, form double or triple bonds** by moving lone pairs from the surrounding atoms into bonding positions.
7. **Add the charge** if the species is an ion, and bracket the structure.

*Worked electron counts:*

- $\mathrm{ClO_4^-}$: $7 + 6\times 4 + 1 = 32$ electrons = 16 pairs
- $\mathrm{CO_3^{2-}}$: $4 + 3\times 6 + 2 = 24$ electrons = 12 pairs
- $\mathrm{OCl_2}$: $6 + 7\times 2 = 20$ electrons = 10 pairs
- $\mathrm{BrCl_3}$: $7 + 7\times 3 = 28$ electrons = 14 pairs

Note the $+1$ for the negative charge on perchlorate and $+2$ on carbonate — a dropped charge is the most common source of a wrong structure.

## Formal charge

**Formal charge** identifies which of several valid Lewis structures is best:

$$\text{formal charge} = \text{valence e}^- - \left(\frac{\text{bonding e}^-}{2} + \text{nonbonding e}^-\right)$$

The best structure is the one that:

- has the **fewest charges**, as close to zero as possible, and
- puts any **negative charge on the most electronegative atom**.

For $\mathrm{CO_2}$ drawn as $\mathrm{O{=}C{=}O}$, every formal charge is zero, which is why that structure is correct.

## Coordinate covalent bonds

A **coordinate covalent bond** is a covalent bond in which **one atom provides both electrons** for the shared pair. Once formed, it is **identical to a regular covalent bond** — the label describes its origin, not its nature.

It usually forms when one atom already has a lone pair and another atom needs two electrons to complete its octet, as $\mathrm{H^+}$ does. **Polyatomic ions often contain both regular and coordinate covalent bonds** — the ammonium ion $\mathrm{NH_4^+}$ is the standard example.

## Resonance

**Resonance structures** are multiple valid Lewis structures for a molecule or ion, arising from **delocalised electrons**. The actual structure is a **resonance hybrid** — an average of all the resonance forms, and **lower in energy (more stable) than any single one of them**.

**Delocalisation** means the $\pi$ electrons are spread over three or more atoms, which stabilises the molecule.

**Evidence:** bond lengths and bond energies are **intermediate** between single and double bonds. In ozone, $\mathrm{O{=}O{-}O \leftrightarrow O{-}O{=}O}$, both O–O bonds measure 1.28 Å — shorter than a single bond, longer than a double.

$\mathrm{NO_3^-}$ has three resonance structures, with its $\pi$ electrons delocalised over the three N–O bonds. $\mathrm{CO_3^{2-}}$ and $\mathrm{SO_2}$ behave the same way.

The molecule does not flicker between forms. It exists permanently in a single state that the individual drawings can only approximate — a limitation of Lewis notation, not of the molecule.

## Exceptions to the octet rule

**1. Odd number of electrons.** Molecules with an unpaired electron cannot satisfy the octet rule. $\mathrm{NO}$ has $5 + 6 = 11$ electrons; $\mathrm{NO_2}$ and $\mathrm{ClO_2}$ are likewise odd.

**2. Fewer than eight (incomplete octet).** Elements such as **B and Be** form compounds with fewer than eight electrons. In $\mathrm{BF_3}$, boron has only 6 electrons from three single bonds — which is exactly why $\mathrm{BF_3}$ readily accepts a lone pair, forming a **coordinate covalent bond** with $\mathrm{NH_3}$ or becoming $\mathrm{BF_4^-}$.

**3. Expanded octet.** Elements in **period 3 or higher** (P, S, Cl, Xe) can use $d$ orbitals in their energy level to hold more than eight electrons:

- $\mathrm{PCl_5}$ — P has 10 electrons, five single bonds.
- $\mathrm{SF_6}$ — S has 12 electrons, six single bonds.
- $\mathrm{XeF_4}$ — likewise expanded.

Second-period elements can never expand their octet, because level 2 has no $d$ sublevel. That is the structural reason $\mathrm{NF_5}$ does not exist while $\mathrm{PF_5}$ does.
