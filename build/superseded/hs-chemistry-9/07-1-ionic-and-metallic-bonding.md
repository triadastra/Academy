# 7.1 Ionic and Metallic Bonding

A **chemical bond** is the electrostatic attraction force that holds two atoms together. There are three kinds, distinguished by what is attracted to what:

| Bond | Attraction between |
|---|---|
| **Ionic** | Cations and anions |
| **Covalent** | Nuclei and shared electrons |
| **Metallic** | Metal cations and delocalised electrons |

This lesson covers the first and the third; covalent bonding is 7.2.

## Lewis symbols

**Lewis symbols** are a drawing method that serves as an alternative to electron configuration. They are used to express chemical bonding and molecular geometry.

To draw one: write the element symbol, determine its number of valence electrons from the group number, and draw one dot per valence electron around the symbol. Hydrogen has one valence electron, so it gets one dot.

## Ionic bonding

Because of the **octet rule**, atoms want to gain or lose electrons to reach a noble-gas configuration:

- **Losing** electrons gives a **cation** (+).
- **Gaining** electrons gives an **anion** (−).

The halogens (Group 17) have 7 valence electrons and need one more for an octet, so they usually appear as 1− anions.

> **Test tip:** check the periodic table before you do bonding questions. The group number gives the valence count, and the valence count gives the likely charge.

**Pseudo-noble-gas configurations.** For elements in Groups 1B–4A in periods 4–6 — Zn, Ag, Pb and similar — the ion formed has a filled $d$ sublevel rather than a true octet. This configuration is relatively stable, which is why these elements form the ions they do.

## Ionic compounds

An ionic compound is the bond between cations and anions: the cations give electrons to the anions, and the resulting opposite charges create an electrostatic force of attraction. **Binary ionic compounds contain a metallic cation and a nonmetallic anion.**

The **electrostatic forces that hold ions together are called ionic bonds**.

**Ionic compounds must be electrically neutral**, so the charges of the cations and anions must balance.

A **chemical formula** shows the numbers of atoms of each element in the smallest representative unit of a substance. For an ionic compound this unit is a **formula unit** — the **lowest whole-number ratio of ions**. For sodium chloride the ratio is 1:1, one $\mathrm{Na^+}$ per $\mathrm{Cl^-}$, so the formula unit is $\mathrm{NaCl}$.

Ionic charges are used to *derive* the correct formula but are **not shown** when the formula unit is written.

> **Test tip:** when asked to explain trends in ionic bonding, refer to **Coulomb's law** — the force between charges is proportional to the product of the charges and inversely proportional to the square of the distance between them. Every ionic trend reduces to "bigger charges, closer together, stronger bond."

## Crystal lattices

Most ionic compounds are **crystalline solids** at room temperature, with the component ions arranged in repeating three-dimensional patterns. When grouped, the pattern always alternates $+ - + - + -\ldots$, forming a **lattice**. **Each ion is attracted strongly to each of its neighbours, and repulsions are minimised.**

The **coordination number** of an ion is the **number of ions of opposite charge that surround it** in the crystal. In $\mathrm{NaCl}$ each ion has a coordination number of 6, because each $\mathrm{Na^+}$ is surrounded by six $\mathrm{Cl^-}$.

**Lattice energy** is the energy required to completely separate the ions of one mole of an ionic solid into gaseous ions. It increases with **larger ionic charges** and **smaller ionic radii** — Coulomb's law again.

## Properties of ionic compounds

1. **Very stable structure**, because of the large attractive forces.
2. **High melting point and hardness** — the network of attractions extends throughout the entire crystal, so melting requires breaking an enormous number of strong bonds at once.
3. **Strong but brittle.** When an ionic crystal is struck, the cations and anions shift from their fixed positions; repulsions between ions of like charge then cause the crystal to shatter.
4. **Poor conductor as a solid, good conductor when molten or aqueous.** Ions have fixed positions in the solid state with no freedom to move. When melted, the orderly crystal structure breaks down; when dissolved, the ions become free to move about in solution. Conduction requires **mobile charge carriers**, and only the liquid and aqueous states provide them.

## Metallic bonding

Metals consist of **closely packed cations and loosely held valence electrons** rather than neutral atoms — the **"sea of electrons"** model.

- **Cations** — positively charged metal ions formed by losing valence electrons, e.g. $\mathrm{Na^+}$, $\mathrm{Mg^{2+}}$.
- **Electron sea** — delocalised valence electrons moving freely throughout the metal lattice.

A **metallic bond** is the attractive force between the free-floating valence electrons and the cations. The useful analogy: metal cations are "islands" submerged in a "sea" of electrons that holds them together.

### Properties explained by the electron sea

| Property | Explanation |
|---|---|
| **Metallic lustre** | Free electrons absorb and re-emit light |
| **Conductivity** | Mobile electrons carry electric current and thermal energy |
| **Malleability** | When force is applied, electrons shift and the cations stay bonded |
| **Ductility** | Cations can be drawn into wires without breaking bonds, supported by the electron sea |
| **High density** | Close-packed crystal structures, e.g. face-centred cubic |

The malleability contrast with ionic solids is the clearest demonstration of the model. In an **ionic crystal**, applying force shifts a layer of ions so that like charges align and repel, and the crystal shatters. In a **metal**, the electron sea is not directional — the cations can slide past each other and the sea simply flows with them.

### Strength of metallic bonding

Two primary factors:

1. **Ionic radius** — a smaller radius means stronger attraction to the electron sea. $\mathrm{Li^+ < Na^+ < K^+}$ in size, so melting point falls: Li 180 °C > Na 98 °C > K 63 °C.
2. **Number of valence electrons** — more electrons in the sea means stronger bonding. Na (1e⁻) < Mg (2e⁻) < Al (3e⁻), so Al 660 °C > Mg 650 °C > Na 98 °C.

A **secondary factor** is crystal structure — close-packed structures enhance bonding.

*Worked example:* Mg melts at 650 °C and Na at 98 °C because $\mathrm{Mg^{2+}}$ has a smaller radius **and** contributes two valence electrons rather than one, so both factors point the same way.

*Worked example:* ranked by decreasing metallic bond strength, Li > Na > K — smallest to largest ionic radius.

### Metallic versus ionic bonds

| Feature | Metallic bond | Ionic bond |
|---|---|---|
| Bonding particles | Cations + electron sea | Cations + anions |
| Electron behaviour | Delocalised, mobile | Transferred, localised |
| Conducts as a solid | Yes | No |
| Response to force | Malleable, ductile | Brittle, shatters |

**Alloys** are mixtures of metals — steel is Fe + C — that enhance strength by **disrupting cation packing**, making it harder for layers to slide.
