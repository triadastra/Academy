# 4.1 Atomic Structure

This lesson follows the atom from a philosophical guess to the nuclear model, then defines the quantities — atomic number, mass number, isotopes, and atomic mass — that every later calculation depends on.

## From philosophy to theory

An **atom** is the smallest particle of an element that retains the chemical identity of that element. Modern instruments such as the **scanning electron microscope** produce colour-enhanced images of atoms.

**Democritus's atomic philosophy** held that all matter is made of atoms. But Greek philosophers could only reason, debate, and discuss — they had no evidence or proof. That distinction matters: Democritus produced a philosophy, not a theory.

**Dalton's atomic theory** was the first evidence-based account:

1. All elements are composed of tiny indivisible particles called **atoms**.
2. Atoms of the **same element are identical**; atoms of any element are different from those of every other element.
3. Atoms of different elements can physically mix or chemically combine in **simple whole-number ratios** to form compounds.
4. Chemical reactions occur when atoms are **separated, joined, or rearranged** — atoms of one element are never changed into atoms of another element by a chemical reaction.

Dalton pictured the atom as a **hard sphere that was the same throughout**. Point 4 restates the Law of Conservation of Mass from 2.1 in atomic terms, and points 1 and 2 were both later revised.

## Subatomic particles

Atoms are now known to be **divisible**. Three kinds of subatomic particles are **electrons, protons, and neutrons**.

### Electrons

In 1897 the English physicist **J.J. Thomson** discovered the electron using a **cathode ray tube (CRT)**:

- **Cathode:** negatively charged electrode.
- **Anode:** positively charged electrode.
- The result was a glowing beam — a **cathode ray** — that travelled from the cathode to the anode.

Because the beam moved away from the negative electrode, the particles carried negative charge. Thomson obtained the electron's **charge-to-mass ratio**, but not either quantity separately.

**Robert A. Millikan** found the quantity of an electron's charge with the **oil-drop experiment**, measuring how varying the voltage between charged plates affected the motion of suspended oil droplets. The charge on any drop was always an **integral multiple** of $1.60 \times 10^{-19}$ coulombs, so Millikan deduced this value to be the charge of a single electron.

**Thomson's model of the atom** followed: a sphere of positive charge with negative charges distributed evenly throughout the interior.

### The nucleus

**Rutherford's gold-foil experiment** directed a narrow beam of **alpha particles** (helium nuclei) at a fragile sheet of gold.

- **Expected:** alpha particles should pass through with little or no deflection, as Thomson's diffuse-charge model predicts.
- **Observed:** most went straight through or were slightly deflected, but **a small fraction bounced off at very large angles**, and some bounced straight back toward the source.

The conclusion: all of an atom's positive charge and most of its mass is concentrated in a very small core at the atom's centre — the **nucleus**. This is the **nuclear model**.

**Protons** are the positively charged subatomic particles in the nucleus. **Neutrons** are subatomic particles with no charge but a mass nearly equal to that of a proton; **James Chadwick** confirmed their existence.

For a **neutral atom, the number of electrons equals the number of protons**.

### Summary of contributions

| Scientist | Contribution |
|---|---|
| **Dalton** | Atomic theory — atoms as indivisible spheres |
| **J.J. Thomson** | Found the electron using the CRT; obtained the charge-to-mass ratio |
| **Millikan** | Found the quantity of an electron's charge |
| **Rutherford** | Proved the existence of the nucleus; proposed the nuclear model |
| **Chadwick** | Confirmed the existence of neutrons |

## Atomic number and mass number

The **atomic number** is the number of protons in the nucleus of an atom of that element. It **identifies the element** — change it and you have a different element.

For an individual atom, which is electrically neutral, the **number of electrons equals the number of protons**.

$$\text{mass number} = n_{\text{proton}} + n_{\text{neutron}}$$

## Isotopes

**Isotopes** are atoms that have the **same number of protons but different numbers of neutrons**.

- Elements are always found as a **mixture with a fixed percentage of isotopes**.
- Isotopes of the same element have **essentially identical chemical properties**, because chemistry is determined by electrons, and the electron count is unchanged.
- The **major difference between isotopes is their mass**.

**Notation** — either add the mass number after the element's name (carbon-12), or place the mass number to the **upper left** of the element symbol:

$$^{12}_{\ 6}\mathrm{C}$$

## Ions

When an atom **loses or gains one or more electrons**, it acquires a net electrical charge and is called an **ion**.

- **Losing** electrons gives a **positive** ion (a cation).
- **Gaining** electrons gives a **negative** ion (an anion).

The proton count never changes when an ion forms — otherwise the element itself would change.

## Atomic mass

**Atomic mass** is the **average mass of an element's isotopes, weighted by their relative abundance**. **Abundance** is the natural prevalence of the different isotopes of an element on Earth.

Masses in grams are inconveniently small, so the relative scale of atomic masses uses $\tfrac{1}{12}$ of the mass of the **carbon-12** isotope as the standard, giving the **atomic mass unit (amu)**:

- The mass of a proton or a neutron is approximately **one amu**.
- The mass of an electron is negligible by comparison.

$$\text{atomic mass} = \sum (\text{mass of isotope} \times \text{abundance})$$

The atomic mass of an element is close to the mass number of the isotope with the **highest abundance** — a useful check on any weighted-average calculation. If your answer sits far from the dominant isotope's mass, you have made an arithmetic error.
