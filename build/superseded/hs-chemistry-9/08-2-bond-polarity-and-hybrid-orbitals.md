# 8.2 Bond Polarity and Hybrid Orbitals

This lesson covers how unequal sharing produces polar bonds, and how orbital mixing accounts for the shapes predicted by VSEPR in 8.1.

## Bond polarity

**Bond polarity** is a measure of how **equally or unequally the electrons in a covalent bond are shared**.

- In a **nonpolar covalent bond**, the electrons are shared **equally** — as in $\mathrm{Cl_2}$ and $\mathrm{N_2}$, and any diatomic molecule of one element.
- In a **polar covalent bond**, electrons are shared **unequally**: one atom exerts a greater attraction for the bonding electrons than the other.

The element with the **greater electronegativity** has the greater attractive force in the bond. **If the difference in electronegativity is large enough, an ionic bond is formed** instead — the electron is not merely pulled but transferred.

## Classifying a bond by ΔEN

Using the Pauling scale difference:

| $\Delta \text{EN}$ | Bond type |
|---|---|
| $< 0.4$ | Nonpolar covalent |
| $0.4 – 2.0$ | Polar covalent |
| $\ge 2.0$ | Ionic |

The boundaries are conventions, not physical thresholds: bonding is a continuum from equal sharing to complete transfer, and these numbers just mark useful places along it.

## Describing polar bonds

The lowercase Greek letter **delta ($\delta$)** denotes that the atoms in a covalent bond acquire only **partial** charges, less than $1+$ or $1-$. These partial charges may be shown as clouds of electron density.

In $\mathrm{HCl}$, the chlorine atom attracts the electron cloud more than the hydrogen does, giving $\mathrm{H^{\delta+}}$–$\mathrm{Cl^{\delta-}}$.

The polar nature of a bond may also be represented by an **arrow pointing toward the more electronegative atom**.

A separation of positive from negative partial charge is measured as the bond's **dipole moment**. Molecular polarity — how bond dipoles combine — is covered in 8.1.

## Sigma and pi bonds

Covalent bonds are classified by the **shape of the orbital overlap**:

**Sigma ($\sigma$) bonds** form by **head-on overlap** of orbitals (s–s, s–p, p–p), with the electron density concentrated on the internuclear axis, in the middle between the two nuclei. They are the **strongest** type and permit **free rotation** about the bond.

**Pi ($\pi$) bonds** occur only between **p orbitals**, overlapping **sideways** to form two sausage-shaped clouds of electron density parallel to the internuclear axis. They are **weaker**, because the sideways overlap is less effective and there is more repulsion, and they **prevent rotation**.

Every bond combination follows from this:

| Bond | Composition |
|---|---|
| Single | 1 σ |
| Double | 1 σ + 1 π |
| Triple | 1 σ + 2 π |

A C=C double bond is 1 σ from $sp^2$–$sp^2$ overlap plus 1 π from p–p side overlap. A C≡C triple bond is 1 σ from $sp$–$sp$ overlap plus 2 π from p–p side overlaps.

The restricted rotation of π bonds is why alkenes have distinct *cis* and *trans* isomers while alkanes do not — a fact that carries directly into organic chemistry.

## Hybrid orbitals

When an atom approaches another atom to form a bond, its **atomic orbitals mix together, forming hybrid orbitals**. In hybridisation, several atomic orbitals mix to form the **same total number** of equivalent hybrid orbitals, all with equal energy and a specific geometry.

| Hybridisation | Orbitals mixed | Domains | Geometry | Bond angle | Examples |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| $sp$ | 1 s + 1 p | 2 | Linear | 180° | $\mathrm{BeF_2}$, $\mathrm{C_2H_2}$ |
| $sp^2$ | 1 s + 2 p | 3 | Trigonal planar | 120° | $\mathrm{BF_3}$, $\mathrm{C_2H_4}$ |
| $sp^3$ | 1 s + 3 p | 4 | Tetrahedral, pyramidal, bent | 109.5° and below | $\mathrm{CH_4}$, $\mathrm{NH_3}$, $\mathrm{H_2O}$ |
| $sp^3d$ | 1 s + 3 p + 1 d | 5 | Trigonal bipyramidal | 90°/120° | $\mathrm{PCl_5}$ |
| $sp^3d^2$ | 1 s + 3 p + 2 d | 6 | Octahedral | 90° | $\mathrm{SF_6}$ |

**Shortcut:** the number of **electron domains equals the number of hybrid orbitals**. Count domains as in 8.1, and the hybridisation follows immediately — 5 domains means $sp^3d$.

*Worked example:* the central atom in $\mathrm{CO_2}$ — linear geometry → 2 electron domains → **$sp$** hybridisation.

*Worked example:* $\mathrm{NH_4^+}$ — tetrahedral shape → 4 electron domains → **$sp^3$** hybridisation.

Note that the count uses **domains, not bonds**, so lone pairs are included. Water has two bonds but four domains, and is therefore $sp^3$, not $sp$.

## Key formulas

$$\Delta \text{EN} > 2.0 \approx \text{ionic}; \quad 0.4\text{–}2.0 \approx \text{polar covalent}; \quad < 0.4 \approx \text{nonpolar covalent}$$

$$\text{electron domains} = \text{number of hybrid orbitals}$$
