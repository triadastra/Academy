# 6.2 Periodic Trends

Every trend in this lesson follows from two competing quantities: how strongly the nucleus pulls on the outer electrons, and how far away those electrons are. Get **effective nuclear charge** right and the rest follows.

> **Test tip:** do not explain questions by quoting the direction of a trend. Explain the **reason behind** the trend — that is what the question is actually testing.

## Effective nuclear charge

In multi-electron atoms, electrons are **attracted to the nucleus but also repelled by other electrons**. The nuclear charge an electron actually experiences depends on both.

**Shielding effect:** inner (core) electrons repel outer (valence) electrons, decreasing the attraction from the nuclear charge $Z$.

**Effective nuclear charge** $Z_{\text{eff}}$ is the **net positive charge experienced by the valence electrons** after accounting for shielding:

$$Z_{\text{eff}} = Z - S$$

where $S$ is the shielding constant.

Details worth holding onto:

- $Z_{\text{eff}}$ for outermost electrons is **smaller** than for inner electrons, because of screening.
- **Inner electrons have a greater screening impact** than other outermost electrons — electrons in the same shell shield each other only weakly.
- On the same principal energy level, $Z_{\text{eff}}$ **decreases in the order s, p, d, f** — an s electron penetrates closest to the nucleus.

**Trends:**

- **Across a period: increases.** Protons are added while shielding stays roughly constant, because the added electrons go into the same shell.
- **Down a group: increases slightly**, because the diffuse core electron clouds screen less effectively than a simple count would suggest.

## Atomic and ionic size

**Atomic radius:**

- **Down a group: increases.** More energy levels are added, so valence electrons sit further from the nucleus.
- **Across a period: decreases.** $Z_{\text{eff}}$ increases, pulling the same shell in more tightly.

The second is the counterintuitive one: adding electrons across a period makes atoms *smaller*, because the added protons pull harder than the added electrons push apart.

**Ionic radius:**

- **Cations are smaller than their parent atoms** — an entire outer shell is often lost, and the remaining electrons feel a higher $Z_{\text{eff}}$.
- **Anions are larger than their parent atoms** — added electrons increase repulsion without any change in nuclear charge.

## Ionisation energy

An atom's **ionisation energy** ($I$) is the energy required to remove an electron (the valence electron first) from the **ground state of a gaseous atom or ion**. It is **always positive** — removing an electron from an attractive field always costs energy.

Atoms with **high $I$ hold onto their electrons tightly**; atoms with **low $I$ are more likely to lose their outermost electrons**, which is why low-$I$ elements are the reactive metals.

**Trend 1 — down a group, ionisation energy decreases.** More energy levels, so the valence electron is further from the nucleus while $Z_{\text{eff}}$ is nearly unchanged.

**Trend 2 — across a period, ionisation energy increases.** $Z_{\text{eff}}$ increases and the atoms get smaller, so the electron is held nearer to the nucleus and more energy is required to remove it.

**Ionisation energy trends are exactly opposite to the trends for atomic radius.** Radius is inversely related to attraction, and ionisation energy is directly related to it.

### Exceptions

Two discontinuities interrupt the across-a-period increase:

- **Group 2A to 3A** (e.g. Be > B) — the removed electron comes from a **p orbital** rather than the lower-energy, more strongly attracted **s orbital**.
- **Group 5A to 6A** (e.g. N > O) — the removed electron comes from a **doubly occupied orbital**, and the pairing repulsion makes it easier to remove.

Both exceptions come from the filling rules in 5.4 — the Aufbau ordering and Hund's rule respectively.

### Successive ionisation energies

**Successive ionisation energies** are the energies required to remove electrons beyond the first. The **first ionisation energy** removes the first electron, the second removes the next, and so on.

**Trend 3 — ionisation energy increases for every electron removed**, because each removal leaves a more positive ion holding the remaining electrons more tightly.

**When all valence electrons have been removed, the ionisation energy takes a quantum leap.** An atom holds the electrons of its noble-gas inner core much more strongly than its valence electrons, so there is a large jump the moment the next electron must come from the core.

This jump is diagnostic: counting the electrons removed before the large increase tells you how many valence electrons the element has, and therefore its group.

## Electron affinity

**Electron affinity** is the **energy change when an electron is added to a gaseous atom**.

- **The more negative the electron affinity, the greater the attraction** for the added electron.
- Electron affinities become **more exothermic (more negative) across a period** — it is easier for the atom to gain an electron.

**Exceptions**, from the same orbital reasoning:

- **Group 2A** — the added electron must go into a **p orbital**, a higher energy level, so it takes more energy.
- **Group 5A** — there is **no empty orbital**, so the extra electron must enter an already-occupied orbital, creating repulsion.

## Electronegativity

**Electronegativity** is the **ability of an atom to attract electrons when the atom is in a compound**. It is measured on the **Pauling scale**, roughly 0.7 to 4.0.

- **Down a group: decreases.** Valence shells are held less strongly by the nucleus, so it is harder for the atom to attract an electron.
- **Across a period: increases.** Attraction from the nucleus is greater, so it is easier for the atom to attract an electron.

In general, electronegativity values **decrease from top to bottom and increase from left to right**.

**Fluorine is the most electronegative element at 3.98**; **francium is the least at 0.7**.

*Worked example:* of Cl, Se, and Br, the most electronegative is **Cl** — Group 7A, Period 3, the furthest right and highest up.

## Valence electrons and the octet rule

**Valence electrons** are the electrons in the **highest occupied energy level** of an element's atoms. For a representative element, simply look at the **group number** to find the count. The exception: **helium has two** valence electrons, while the other noble gases have eight.

**Electron dot structures** are diagrams showing an atom's valence electrons as dots. All elements within a group (helium excepted) have the same number of dots — a direct visual of why groups share chemical behaviour.

**The octet rule:** in forming compounds, atoms tend to achieve the electron configuration of a noble gas. Noble gases such as neon and argon are non-reactive precisely because they already have eight electrons in their highest occupied energy level, $ns^2np^6$.

- **Metals tend to lose their valence electrons**, leaving a complete octet in the next-lowest energy level.
- **Some nonmetals tend to gain or share electrons** with another nonmetal atom to achieve a complete octet.

The octet rule applies to atoms in most compounds — the exceptions are covered in 7.2.

## Formation of ions

An **ion** is an atom that is no longer neutral because it has lost or gained electrons.

- A **cation** (positive) is produced when an atom **loses** one or more valence electrons.
- An **anion** (negative) is produced when an atom **gains** electrons. The name of an anion typically ends in **-ide**; those formed when chlorine and the other halogens gain an electron are called **halide ions**.
- **The charges of transition metal cations may vary**, which is why their names require a Roman numeral.

Every part of this section is the ionisation-energy and electron-affinity trends restated chemically: elements with low ionisation energy form cations, elements with strongly negative electron affinity form anions, and the octet is where both processes stop.
