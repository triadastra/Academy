# 8.3 Intermolecular Forces and Molecular Properties

The bonds in 7.2 hold atoms together **within** a molecule. This lesson covers the much weaker attractions **between** molecules — which nevertheless determine most of the physical properties you can measure.

## Van der Waals forces

**Van der Waals forces** are the two weakest attractions between molecules, also known collectively as **intermolecular forces**.

### Dispersion forces

**Dispersion forces** are caused by the **motion of electrons**. A momentary imbalance in an electron cloud creates a temporary dipole, which induces one in a neighbour.

- They are the **weakest of all molecular interactions**.
- They **operate between all molecules** — they occur even between nonpolar molecules, which have no other attraction available.
- Their strength **generally increases as the number of electrons in a molecule increases**, because a larger, more diffuse cloud is more easily distorted.

The electron-count trend is why the halogens go from gas ($\mathrm{F_2}$, $\mathrm{Cl_2}$) to liquid ($\mathrm{Br_2}$) to solid ($\mathrm{I_2}$) down the group, with no change in bond type at all.

### Dipole interactions

**Dipole interactions** occur when **polar molecules are attracted to one another**: the slightly negative region of one polar molecule is weakly attracted to the slightly positive region of another. They are **similar to, but much weaker than, ionic bonds** — partial charges rather than full ones.

## Hydrogen bonding

**Hydrogen bonding** is an especially strong dipole–dipole force between the **hydrogen atom of one molecule ($\delta+$)** and an **F, O, or N atom of another** molecule.

- Hydrogen bonds are the **strongest of the intermolecular forces**.
- They are **extremely important in determining the properties of water and of biological molecules** — they hold the two strands of DNA together and give proteins their folded shapes.
- The positive region of one water molecule attracts the negative region of another. A **snowflake's shape** is determined by the interactions of hydrogen bonds during its formation.

Hydrogen bonding requires H bonded directly to a **small, highly electronegative** atom. That combination concentrates a large partial positive charge on an atom with no inner electrons to shield it, which is why the effect is so much stronger than an ordinary dipole interaction — and why water boils at 100 °C when comparable-sized molecules boil far below zero.

## Intermolecular attractions and physical properties

**The diversity of physical properties among covalent compounds is mainly due to widely varying intermolecular attractions.**

The **melting and boiling points of most molecular compounds are low** compared with those of ionic compounds, because in most molecular solids **only the weak attractions between molecules need to be broken** — the covalent bonds inside each molecule stay intact.

At room temperature, molecular covalent substances may be:

- **Solid** — $\mathrm{I_2}$
- **Liquid** — $\mathrm{H_2O}$, $\mathrm{C_2H_5OH}$, $\mathrm{Br_2}$
- **Gas** — $\mathrm{CO_2}$, $\mathrm{O_2}$, $\mathrm{N_2}$

This is the point students most often miss: boiling water breaks hydrogen bonds between molecules, not the O–H bonds within them. Steam is still $\mathrm{H_2O}$, exactly as 2.1 said.

## Network solids

A few solids consisting of atoms bonded covalently **do not melt until 1000 °C or higher**. Most of these very stable substances are **network solids** (network crystals) — solids in which **all of the atoms are covalently bonded to each other** throughout the crystal, with no separate molecules at all.

**Diamond.** Each carbon atom is covalently bonded to **four other carbons**, interconnecting carbon atoms throughout the entire crystal in a tetrahedral structure. Diamond does not melt; it **vaporises to a gas at 3500 °C and above**. Uses: drill tips, abrasive coatings.

**Graphite.** Carbon atoms arranged in **layers held together by weak attractive forces**. It is therefore **soft** and, unusually for a nonmetal, a **conductor of electricity**. Uses: pencil cores, lubricant, battery electrodes.

**Quartz ($\mathrm{SiO_2}$).** A giant covalent structure similar to diamond: each **silicon atom is bonded to four oxygen atoms**, and each **oxygen atom is bonded to two silicon atoms**.

Diamond and graphite are both pure carbon, and the entire difference in their properties comes from how the covalent network is arranged — a three-dimensional lattice versus weakly stacked sheets. Structure, not composition, is what determines properties here.
