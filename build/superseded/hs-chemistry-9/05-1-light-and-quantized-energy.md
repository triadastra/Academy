# 5.1 Light and Quantized Energy

Rutherford's model in 4.1 left a question unanswered: where are the electrons? The answer came from studying light, and it required abandoning the idea that energy is continuous. This lesson covers the wave nature of light, quantisation, the photoelectric effect, and atomic emission spectra.

## The wave nature of light

Light is a kind of **electromagnetic radiation**. An electromagnetic wave consists of **electric and magnetic fields oscillating at right angles** to each other.

- **Wavelength** ($\lambda$) — the distance between corresponding points on adjacent waves.
- **Frequency** ($\nu$) — the number of waves passing a given point per unit of time. The SI unit is the **hertz (Hz)**.

All electromagnetic radiation travels at the same velocity — the **speed of light**, $c = 3.00 \times 10^8\ \text{m/s}$ in a vacuum:

$$c = \lambda\nu$$

Because $c$ is fixed, $\lambda$ and $\nu$ are **inversely proportional**: a shorter wavelength means a higher frequency.

The **electromagnetic spectrum** separates waves into levels by wavelength. Within the visible spectrum, ordered by increasing frequency:

$$\text{violet} < \text{indigo} < \text{blue} < \text{green} < \text{yellow} < \text{orange} < \text{red}$$

read as *decreasing* frequency from violet to red — violet has the shortest wavelength and highest frequency, red the longest and lowest.

**Higher-frequency electromagnetic radiation has higher energy.**

## Where the wave model fails

The wave model does not explain how light forms when temperature increases — the glow of a heated object, or of a neon light. A purely continuous wave picture predicts the wrong distribution of emitted energy.

## Planck's quantum theory

**Planck** resolved this by assuming energy comes in packets called **quanta**: energy can be either released or absorbed by atoms only in **discrete "chunks" of some minimum size**.

A **quantum** is the smallest quantity of energy that can be emitted or absorbed:

$$E = h\nu$$

where $h$ is **Planck's constant**, $6.626 \times 10^{-34}\ \text{J}\cdot\text{s}$.

Substituting $\nu = c/\lambda$ gives the equally useful form $E = hc/\lambda$.

## The photoelectric effect

Einstein used Planck's idea to explain the **photoelectric effect**: **electrons are ejected when light shines on a metal**.

Light consists of quanta of energy that behave like tiny particles, which Einstein called **photons**. A **photon** is a particle of electromagnetic radiation with no mass that carries a quantum of energy, $E = h\nu$.

When a photon strikes the surface of a metal, it transfers its energy to an electron in a metal atom. There is a minimum energy required to eject an electron — the **threshold energy** (work function). **Only when the photon's energy exceeds the threshold can an electron be emitted:**

$$KE_{\text{electron}} = E_{\text{photon}} - E_{\text{threshold}}$$

This is the decisive evidence for the particle nature of light. If light were purely a wave, a dim beam of any colour would eventually accumulate enough energy to eject an electron. It does not: below the threshold **frequency**, no electrons are emitted no matter how intense or how prolonged the light. Intensity controls *how many* photons arrive, not how much energy each one carries.

## Atomic emission spectra

Two kinds of spectrum:

- A **continuous spectrum** — one colour fades gradually into the next, as in the visible spectrum from a white light source.
- An **atomic emission spectrum** — the set of frequencies of the electromagnetic waves emitted by atoms of an element, appearing as **discrete lines** rather than a continuous band.

The atomic emission spectrum **is unique to each element**. This is what makes flame tests and astronomical spectroscopy possible: the line pattern is a fingerprint.

Discrete lines are exactly what a quantised model predicts. If an atom can only hold certain energies, it can only release certain energy differences, and therefore only certain frequencies of light — which is the subject of 5.2.
