# 2.1 Matter and Change

This lesson classifies matter — into states, mixtures, elements, and compounds — and distinguishes physical from chemical change. The organising question throughout is whether the **composition** stays the same.

## Properties of matter

**Properties** are characteristics that help identify types of matter.

- **Physical properties** can be observed **without changing** the substance.
- **Chemical properties** can only be observed **during a chemical change**.

Flammability is a chemical property: the only way to observe it is to burn the substance, which changes it.

## Physical and chemical change

- A **physical change** may change the **state** of matter, but **not the composition**:
  $$\mathrm{H_2O(l) \rightarrow H_2O(g)}$$
  Steam is still water.

- A **chemical change** changes the **composition**:
  $$\mathrm{NH_4^+(aq) + NO_2^-(aq) \rightarrow N_2(g) + 2H_2O(l)}$$
  Different substances exist afterwards.

**The Law of Conservation of Mass:** matter cannot be created or destroyed in a chemical reaction, so $m_{\text{reactant}} = m_{\text{product}}$.

The atoms are rearranged, not created or destroyed — which is why chemical equations must balance.

## States of matter

| State | Shape | Volume |
|---|---|---|
| **Gas** | Takes its container's shape | No definite volume |
| **Liquid** | Takes its container's shape | Definite volume |
| **Solid** | Definite shape | Definite volume |

## Mixtures

A **mixture** is a **physical blend** of two or more components.

- **Homogeneous** — uniform composition throughout.
- **Heterogeneous** — non-uniform composition.

Because a mixture is only a physical blend, its components can be separated by **physical** means:

- **Filtration** — uses a difference in particle size or mass. Sand water → water (l) + sand (s).
- **Distillation** — uses a difference in **boiling point**. Alcoholic water → alcohol (g) + water (l).
- **Crystallisation** — evaporating or boiling a liquid to leave the solid solute. Salt water → water (g) + salt (s).

Choosing a technique means asking which property differs between the components.

## Elements and compounds

- **Elements** are pure substances made of **one type of atom**.
- **Compounds** consist of **two or more elements chemically combined**, and can be broken down into simpler substances by **chemical** reactions.

Examples:

- **Mixture:** sand and water, $\mathrm{SiO_2(s) + H_2O(l)}$
- **Element:** pure oxygen gas, $\mathrm{O_2(g)}$
- **Compound:** carbon dioxide, $\mathrm{CO_2(g)}$

Note that $\mathrm{O_2}$ is an element despite containing two atoms — they are the same kind of atom.

## The diatomic elements

Seven elements occur naturally as **diatomic** molecules, because of their tendency to form covalent bonds:

$$\mathrm{H,\ N,\ O,\ F,\ Cl,\ Br,\ I}$$

They form a "7" shape on the periodic table (excepting hydrogen), which is the usual way to remember them.

## The periodic table

The periodic table organises elements by **atomic number** and groups them by similar properties.

- Properties of elements **within a period change gradually and with a pattern**.
- Elements **within a group have similar properties**.

Elements are classified as:

- **Metals** — typically shiny, good conductors, solid at room temperature (**except mercury, Hg**).
- **Nonmetals** — often dull, poor conductors, typically gases at room temperature (**bromine, Br, is a liquid**).
- **Metalloids** — properties in between.
