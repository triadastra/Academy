# 5.3 Quantum Mechanics and Atomic Orbitals

The quantum mechanical model replaces the orbit with the **orbital** — a region of probability. This lesson covers the model's foundations, the shapes and counts of orbitals, and how energy levels are structured.

## The quantum mechanical model

**Schrödinger** (German, 1887–1961) developed the quantum mechanical model, which determines **the allowed energies an electron can have and the probability of finding the electron in various locations around the nucleus**.

Three properties are built into it, one from each predecessor:

1. The energy of an electron is **quantised** (Bohr).
2. Electrons exhibit **wavelike behaviour** (de Broglie).
3. The exact position *and* momentum of an electron at any given point is **impossible to know simultaneously** (Heisenberg).

## Atomic orbitals

Solutions to the Schrödinger equation lead to a mathematical expression called an **atomic orbital**, represented pictorially as a **region of space in which there is a high probability of finding an electron**.

The picture is often drawn as an electron cloud: **the cloud is denser where the probability of finding the electron is higher**. Because the probability never reaches exactly zero at any finite distance, chemists define an orbital's surface to contain **90% of the electron's total probability distribution**.

Different atomic orbitals are denoted by the letters **s, p, d, and f** — also called **sublevels**.

**Shapes:**

- **s** orbitals — spherical.
- **p** orbitals — dumbbell-shaped.
- **d** orbitals — mostly four-leaf-clover-shaped.
- **f** orbitals — more complex still.

## Counting orbitals and electrons

Let $n$ be the **principal quantum number** — the energy level.

| Quantity | Rule |
|---|---|
| Number of sublevels in level $n$ | $n$ |
| Orbitals per sublevel | s: 1, p: 3, d: 5, f: 7 |
| Total orbitals in level $n$ | $n^2$ |
| Maximum electrons per orbital | 2 |
| Maximum electrons in level $n$ | $2n^2$ |

The **principal quantum number always equals the number of sublevels within that principal energy level** — level 1 has only s, level 2 has s and p, level 3 has s, p, and d, and so on.

The multiple p, d, and f orbitals in a sublevel differ in **orientation**, not in energy. Orbitals with the same energy are called **degenerate orbitals**.

## How orbitals change with $n$

As the principal quantum number increases, orbitals of the same type keep their **basic shape** but grow **larger**, with the region of high probability further from the nucleus. A $3s$ orbital is a bigger sphere than a $2s$ orbital.

## Energy ordering

In a **hydrogen atom** (one electron) the sublevels within a level are degenerate — $2s$ and $2p$ have the same energy. In a **multi-electron atom** they are not, because of electron–electron repulsion. Within one principal level:

$$s < p < d < f$$

Because the levels overlap, the overall filling order is not simply $1, 2, 3, \ldots$:

$$1s < 2s < 2p < 3s < 3p < 4s < 3d < 4p < 5s < 4d < 5p < 6s < 4f < 5d < 6p < 7s < 5f < 6d < 7p$$

Note that $4s$ comes **before** $3d$. This overlap is the origin of the transition metals' position on the periodic table, and of several exceptions covered in 5.4.
