# 5.2 The Bohr Model and the Wave Nature of Matter

This lesson explains the hydrogen line spectrum with Bohr's quantised orbits, then shows why that model had to be replaced — de Broglie's matter waves and Heisenberg's uncertainty principle together make the idea of an electron following a definite orbit untenable.

## The Bohr model

Bohr assumed that **electrons move in certain circular orbits with an allowed energy state around the nucleus**, designated by a **quantum number** $n = 1, 2, 3, \ldots$

Energy is only absorbed or emitted in such a way as to move an electron from one **"allowed" energy (quantised energy level) to another**.

- **Ground state** — the lowest energy state, $n = 1$.
- **Excited states** — the higher energy levels, $n = 2, 3, \ldots$

## Hydrogen's line spectrum

Electrons can be **excited to a higher energy level when they absorb energy**. When the atom is in an excited state, the electron can **drop from the higher level to a lower one**, and the atom **emits a photon** corresponding to the difference between the two levels:

$$\Delta E = E_{\text{higher orbit}} - E_{\text{lower orbit}} = h\nu$$

Because only certain energy differences exist, **only certain frequencies of radiation can be emitted** — which is precisely why the spectrum shows discrete lines rather than a continuous band. This is the explanation the discrete lines of 5.1 were waiting for.

## Evaluating the Bohr model

**What it succeeded at:**

- Explaining **single-electron atoms (hydrogen)** and single-electron ions.
- Introducing **quantised energy levels**, which lead directly to the current model of electronic structure.
- Establishing that electrons exist only in certain **discrete energy levels**, and that **energy is involved in the transition** of an electron from one level to another.

**What it failed at:**

- It **failed to explain the spectrum of any other element** — every multi-electron atom.
- It **cannot explain the different chemical behaviour** of the various elements.
- **Electrons do not move around the nucleus in circular orbits.**

The two surviving ideas — quantised levels, and photon emission on transition — are kept by the quantum mechanical model. The circular orbit is discarded.

## De Broglie: the wave nature of matter

**Louis de Broglie** (French, 1892–1987) theorised that **if light can have material properties, matter should exhibit wave properties**. Particles of matter should behave like waves and exhibit a wavelength, just as waves of light behave like particles:

$$\lambda = \frac{h}{mv}$$

This wavelike behaviour of particles is referred to as **matter waves**. For macroscopic objects $m$ is so large that $\lambda$ is immeasurably small, which is why a thrown ball shows no wave behaviour; for an electron the wavelength is comparable to atomic dimensions, so it matters enormously.

## The Heisenberg uncertainty principle

**The position and the velocity of a moving object cannot simultaneously be measured and known exactly.**

$$\Delta x \cdot \Delta p \ge \frac{h}{4\pi}$$

This is **inconsequential for macroscopic objects** — the uncertainty is far below anything measurable. For an electron it is decisive: knowing the position precisely means the momentum is entirely uncertain, and vice versa.

This is what kills the orbit. An orbit specifies both where the electron is and how fast it is going at every instant, and the uncertainty principle says no such description can be correct. The replacement is a statement of **probability**, not of trajectory — which is the quantum mechanical model of 5.3.
