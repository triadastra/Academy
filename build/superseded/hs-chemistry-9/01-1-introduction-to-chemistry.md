# 1.1 Introduction to Chemistry

This lesson sets out what chemistry studies, how the subject is divided, and how scientific claims are established and classified — including the distinction between a theory and a law, which is misunderstood more often than any other idea in the course.

## What chemistry is

**Chemistry** is the study of the **composition of matter and the changes it undergoes**. (The word derives from "alchemy".)

Two supporting definitions:

- **Matter** is anything that has mass and occupies space.
- **Composition** is the chemical identity of a substance.

Changes in matter are either **physical** or **chemical**, a distinction developed in 2.1.

## Areas of chemistry

| Area | Studies | Examples |
|---|---|---|
| **Organic** | Chemicals containing carbon | Pharmaceuticals, plastics |
| **Inorganic** | Chemicals generally without carbon | Minerals, metals |
| **Biochemistry** | Processes in living organisms | Metabolism, fermentation |
| **Analytical** | The composition of matter | Food nutrients, quality control |
| **Physical** | Mechanisms, rates, and energy transfer | Reaction rates |

The organic/inorganic boundary has exceptions — some carbon compounds, such as carbonates and oxides, are treated as inorganic.

## The scientific method

> Observation → Hypothesis → Experiment → Result → Conclusion

The variables in an experiment:

- **Independent variable** — the variable that is **changed**.
- **Dependent variable** — the variable that is **measured**.
- **Controlled variables** — factors kept constant.
- **Control group** — a group that does not receive the experimental treatment.

Controlled variables and a control group exist for the same reason: without them, any change in the dependent variable might have been caused by something other than the independent variable.

## Theories and scientific laws

A **theory** explains **why** something happens. It can predict outcomes and is open to revision.

A **scientific law** states **that** something happens. It summarises observed phenomena.

- **Theory:** Apples fall from trees because of the gravitational pull exerted by Earth.
- **Law:** When released from a height, objects fall to the ground.

A theory is not a "lesser" version of a law, and theories do not become laws with more evidence. They answer different questions — one describes, the other explains.

## Qualitative and quantitative data

- **Qualitative** — descriptive: "a light grey, hard, shiny material".
- **Quantitative** — numerical: "a substance with an electronegativity of 2.5".

## Two laboratory conventions

**Recording uncertainty.** Scale readings should be expressed as $\pm$ half the unit of the last significant figure, and the whole quantity bracketed before the unit — write $(n \pm m)\,\text{unit}$, not $n \pm m\ \text{unit}$.

**Reading a meniscus.** Always read from the **bottom of the meniscus** in a beaker or graduated cylinder, with your eye level with it.
